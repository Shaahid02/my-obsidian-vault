WWW Companion '25, Xiangyu Li, Yawen Zeng, Xiaofen Xing, Jin Xu and Xiangmin Xu, South China University of Technology and Pazhou Lab with ByteDance, pages 296 to 305, DOI 10.1145/3701716.3715232. Four LLM agents arranged as a hedge fund, three asset analysts covering Bitcoin, the Dow components and forex, and one fund manager who allocates capital across them. Each analyst runs the same workflow, 23 tools, three memory stores and eight actions, and coordination happens through three scheduled or triggered meetings rather than through a fixed reporting hierarchy. Backtested from 1 January 2021 to 31 December 2023 against nine baselines on nine metrics, headline result 71.60 % annualised and 405.34 % total return for a stated API cost of fifteen dollars over the whole three years. Code is public.

This is the closest thing in my set to the layer I am actually building. Every other multi-agent paper I have reviewed decomposes a decision and then recomposes it through prose, a hierarchy or a facilitator, and [[TradingAgents, Multi-Agents LLM Financial Trading Framework]] is the extreme case where there is no quantity in the system that could be weighted at all. HedgeAgents has one. Manager Otto solves an explicit constrained optimisation over a weight vector with return, variance and tail-risk terms, and publishes the objective. So <font color="#ffc000">the orchestration layer exists here as a numerical object rather than as an organisational metaphor</font>, which makes this the paper my own weighting function has to be positioned against, and the positioning turns on what the weights are over and what they are conditioned on rather than on whether weighting is novel.

> [!NOTE] What "hedging" means in this paper
> Not options hedging in the derivatives sense, despite Otto's transcript mentioning put options once. The operational meaning throughout is portfolio-level diversification across three weakly correlated asset classes, rebalanced on a 30-day cycle, with an emergency review when volatility spikes. The system holds crypto, US equities and a currency at the same time so that a drawdown in one is offset by the others, and the word "balanced" in the title refers to this allocation, not to a hedged position within any single asset.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **LLM and RL trading agents collapse in adverse regimes, and the paper measures this rather than asserting it.** The stated observation is that state-of-the-art systems lose roughly 20 % when markets fall rapidly or fluctuate frequently, and Figure 2 splits the test period into rapid rise, rapid decline and extreme volatility windows and reports total return and Sharpe separately in each. In the rapid-decline window 8 of 9 baselines are negative on both, and in the frequent-fluctuation window all 9 are. This is the most useful thing in the paper's framing, because it converts "existing methods are not robust" into a regime-conditioned table that another paper can be held against.

  ![[Pasted image 20260926140001.png]]

- **The diagnosis of why is that there is no risk management mechanism inside the model or system.** The authors' reading is that these frameworks optimise a return objective and have nothing that adjusts exposure when conditions change, so the only defence available is whatever the policy learned in training. This is a fair description of the baselines they chose, and it motivates both the budget allocation solve and the extreme-market trigger. The contribution is then stated as importing hedging into a multi-agent setting, an integration claim framed as one, which means the paper's defence rests entirely on its ablation table.

#### Data and setup

Three asset classes, Bitcoin, the Dow Jones component stocks and forex, sourced from Yahoo Finance and the Alpaca News API. Daily OHLCV plus adjusted close and volume, daily news headlines, and <font color="#ffff00">60 standard technical indicators per asset</font>, spanning 1 January 2015 to 31 December 2023. The split is 2015 to 2020 for training and <font color="#ffc000">1 January 2021 to 31 December 2023 for testing, three full years</font>, which is by a wide margin the longest evaluation window in my agent set and is the paper's strongest methodological choice. During testing only historical prices are accessible, and all baselines are trained and tested in the same environment with Optuna for hyperparameter search.

Nine metrics, two profit measures in total and annualised return, three risk-adjusted in Sharpe, Calmar and Sortino, two risk measures in maximum drawdown and volatility, and two diversity measures, entropy and effective number of bets, taken from the PRUDEX-Compass benchmark. The last two are not defined anywhere beyond the citation, which matters because diversity is one of the two things the title claims. Baselines span rule-based mean-variance, zero mean reversion and time-series momentum, reinforcement learning in SAC, DeepTrader and AlphaMix+, and LLM-based in FinGPT, FinMem and FinAgent, with buy-and-hold on each asset reported separately. Bitcoin's row is the one to keep in view, 43.97 % total return with a 76.63 % maximum drawdown over the same window.

The backbone is `gpt-4-1106-preview` at temperature 0.7, memory is text similarity retrieval over `text-embedding-3-large` with top-K of 5. <font color="#ff0000">No transaction cost appears anywhere in the paper, no fee, no spread, no slippage and no market impact</font>, on a system that rebalances three asset classes monthly and liquidates positions during emergency sessions.

#### Model architecture and training

Nothing is trained. The paper presents the agent policy inside a reinforcement learning objective, but the policy itself is an LLM call, the action is produced by parsing the model's JSON output against a prompt template populated with market state and retrieved memory, and no gradient is taken anywhere in the system. The loop is memory retrieval, decision making, reflection update, with market information written to one store and the reasoning behind each decision to another.

![[Pasted image 20260926140002.png]]

The three coordination meetings are the actual contribution and they differ in what triggers them, which is the detail that matters most for my own design:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Budget Allocation Conference | Every 30 days. Each analyst reports profit and loss with budget expectations and reasons, Otto consolidates and re-solves the allocation |
| Experience Sharing Conference | End of each investment cycle. Analysts recapitulate a typical case over multiple rounds, insights are distilled into the shared general experience store |
| Extreme Market Conference | Triggered when daily amplitude exceeds 5 % or cumulative three-day amplitude exceeds 10 %. Analysts present holdings and losses, Otto issues countermeasures |

In the budget conference the manager first asks each analyst for an expected return, then builds three portfolio-level indicators from those numbers, expected total return, portfolio risk and conditional tail risk,

$$I_{etr} = \sum_{i=1}^{3} \omega_i \rho_i, \qquad I_{pr}^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{j=1}^{3} \omega_i \omega_j I_{ij}, \qquad I_{cvar} = \frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_{1}^{\alpha} \mathrm{VaR}(p)\, dp$$

where $\omega_i$ is the weight on asset $i$, $\rho_i$ is the expected return the analyst for that asset produced, $I_{ij}$ is the covariance between assets $i$ and $j$, and $\mathrm{VaR}(\alpha)$ is value at risk at confidence level $\alpha$ estimated by historical simulation. The allocation is then the solution to

$$\omega^* = \arg\max_{\omega}\; I_{etr} - \lambda_1 I_{pr} - \lambda_2 I_{cvar}, \quad \text{s.t.} \sum_i \omega_i = 1,\; \omega_i \ge 0$$

with $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ as risk aversion coefficients, neither of which is given a value anywhere in the paper. This is mean-variance with a CVaR penalty, so the optimiser is entirely conventional. What is not conventional is that <font color="#ffc000">the expected returns entering it are numbers an LLM wrote in a report, with no calibration check and no confidence interval</font>, which is the part I return to in limitations.

The extreme-market session is the one place where competing suggestions are combined by a stated rule. Otto's own suggestion and those of the other agents are blended as

$$\mathcal{S} = (1-\lambda_3)\,\phi_B(S_B, S_C) + \lambda_3\,\phi_B(S_E)$$

where $S_B$, $S_C$ and $S_E$ are the suggestions from each source, $\phi_B$ is a prompt template and $\lambda_3$ balances their importance. Writing it this way makes it look like a weighted combination of vectors, but $\phi_B$ is a prompt and the addition is over text, so <font color="#ff0000">$\lambda_3$ is a number inserted into a prompt rather than a coefficient applied to anything</font>, and its value is never stated either. This is the formula I would have expected to be the paper's orchestration mechanism and it is the weakest thing in it.

#### Findings

- **The headline margins are large and hold across every metric, on a three-year window.** 71.60 % annualised and 405.34 % total against the best baseline FinAgent at 53.54 % and 261.98 %, with Sharpe 2.41 against FinGPT's 1.93, Calmar 11.02 against 7.64 and Sortino 58.00 against 39.57, improvements of 33.75 %, 54.72 %, 24.49 %, 44.28 % and 46.58 % respectively. Maximum drawdown is 14.21 % against FinGPT's 17.08 %, which is the result I find most persuasive, because the same system that produces the highest return also produces the lowest drawdown, and on a three-year window covering the 2022 crypto collapse that is not a combination a short favourable sample can manufacture.

  ![[Pasted image 20260926140003.png]]

- **The single-asset experiment is where the hedging claim is actually isolated, and it partly survives.** Run without any hedging on one asset at a time, cumulative returns over three years are about 210 % on Bitcoin, 160 % on AAPL and 9 % on the yuan, against FinAgent's roughly 135 % and FinMem's 120 % on AAPL. So the per-asset agents beat the per-asset baselines before any allocation happens, which means the analyst workflow with its memory and tools is carrying a substantial share of the headline number independently of the coordination layer. The paper presents this as evidence of versatility and it is, but it also quietly bounds how much of the result the hedging mechanism can claim.
- **The ablation says all three conferences contribute and that the budget conference dominates returns while the extreme-market conference dominates risk.** Single modules give 43.88 %, 45.58 % and 40.97 % annualised for experience sharing, budget allocation and extreme market respectively, pairs give 50.68 %, 44.57 % and 59.81 %, and the full system gives 71.60 %. Removing budget allocation costs the most return, dropping to 44.57 %, and removing the extreme-market module raises drawdown to 24.44 % and cuts Sharpe to 1.93.

  ![[Pasted image 20260926140004.png]]

  Two things in that table cut against the reading. The experience-sharing plus extreme-market row reports drawdown of 12.02 % and Sharpe of 2.24, both better on risk than the full system's Table 1 figures of 14.21 % and 2.41 on drawdown, so <font color="#ffc000">on these numbers adding the budget conference buys return by taking on drawdown</font>, which is a coherent result but not the monotone one the discussion describes. And the full-system drawdown in this table is 8.68 % against 14.21 % in Table 1, a direct contradiction between two tables in the same paper for the headline risk metric, with the ablation prose computing its 71.93 % increase against 14.21.
- **Memory retrieval is worth more than any single conference.** Removing it drops annualised return by 57.71 % and Sharpe by 39.3 %, larger than the effect of removing any one coordination meeting. The supporting t-SNE plot shows the three memory types forming distinguishable clusters in embedding space, which the authors read as evidence the hierarchy is semantically meaningful. That is a reasonable reading of a projection but not a test of it, and no retrieval-quality metric is reported.
- **The LLM backbone comparison undercuts the choice the paper made, and the paper does not notice.** Across six backbones, `gpt-4-1106-preview` wins annualised return at 71.60 % and total return at 405.34 %, but ChatGLM-6B reports Sharpe 2.88, Calmar 12.26, Sortino 66.64, drawdown 9.19 % and volatility 0.78 %, every one better than GPT-4's, on a six-billion-parameter open model. The authors observe that larger models take more aggressive positions and then select GPT-4 anyway on the return metrics. On this evidence <font color="#ffc000">the risk-adjusted headline is a backbone choice as much as an architecture result</font>, and the best risk-adjusted configuration of their own system is not the one in Table 1.
- **The temporal isolation check is real and most papers in my set do not have one.** Since the backbone's training data predates much of the 2021 to 2023 test window, the authors rerun the system over the first three quarters of 2024 and report 68.44 % total return with Sharpe 2.1, close to the main result. Thirty-six extreme-market sessions fired over those nine months, roughly four a month, which is a useful number in itself for anyone sizing the cost of an event-triggered review.

#### Limitations

- **The expected returns feeding the optimiser are LLM outputs with no calibration, which makes the mean-variance solve look more quantitative than it is.** Each analyst writes a $\rho_i$ in a report, those three numbers go straight into the objective, and the covariance matrix is the only input estimated from data. There is no check that an analyst claiming 12 % is better calibrated than one claiming 8 %, no interval, and no record of realised against predicted. The optimiser is exact arithmetic over inputs of unknown quality, and <font color="#ff0000">a well-specified objective over uncalibrated inputs inherits the uncertainty of the inputs while hiding it behind the precision of the output</font>. This is the sharpest version of the comparable-confidence problem I have seen posed in any paper I have read, and the paper does not pose it.
- **The allocation is over assets, not over agents, and the two coincide here only because the mapping is one to one.** Three analysts, three asset classes, one expert each, so a budget weight and an influence weight are the same number by construction. That is a modelling convenience specific to this setup, and it means the paper never has to answer how much weight to give an agent whose view overlaps another's, how to combine disagreeing views on the same asset, or what to do when an agent's channel is uninformative. On this design those questions cannot arise, so nothing here bears on them either way.
- **Three assets is a thin basis for a diversification claim and the paper's own diversity metric says so.** Effective number of bets is 1.53 against a ceiling of 3, so in risk terms the portfolio is closer to one and a half independent positions than to three, and given Bitcoin's 405 % contribution over a window where buy-and-hold Bitcoin returned only 43.97 % with a 76.63 % drawdown, the weight is plainly concentrated. The entropy figure of 3.13 is reported as best in class but neither metric is defined in the paper, so the reader cannot reconcile them, and <font color="#ffc000">the "balanced-aware" claim in the title rests on two numbers whose definitions are not given</font>.
- **No execution friction of any kind, on a system that rebalances across three asset classes and liquidates during emergencies.** Bitcoin and forex both carry real spreads, and the extreme-market transcript shows a full Bitcoin liquidation two days after a 10.22 % decline, which is the worst possible moment to assume costless execution. Against the rest of my set this joins TradingAgents at exactly zero, with MacroHFT at a flat 2 bps, Jung and Lee at 0.1 % fee plus 0.1 % slippage and FinHEAR at a single 5 bps check. A 405 % return on a costless book is an upper bound rather than a return.
- **Three hyperparameters that control the system's behaviour are never given values.** $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ set the trade-off between return, variance and tail risk in the allocation, and $\lambda_3$ sets how much the manager's view outweighs the analysts' in an emergency. Together they determine how conservative the system is, which is the property the paper is about, and none appears in the text, the tables or the implementation section. The confidence level $\alpha$ for the VaR estimate is also unstated. The code is public so the values presumably exist somewhere, but the published result cannot be reproduced from the paper.
- **Single runs, no dispersion, no significance testing.** Every number is one run at temperature 0.7, which is not a deterministic setting. The three-year window makes this far less damaging than in the sixty-day papers, but the ablation differences between adjacent configurations, 44.57 % against 50.68 % for instance, are exactly the size sampling noise could account for, so the ablation the paper's defence rests on is a set of point estimates.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **It is the existence proof that the orchestration layer can be a solved optimisation rather than a prompt, and it also shows where that stops working for me.** Otto's argmax over $\omega$ with return, variance and CVaR terms is a concrete template for my allocation step, and I should adopt its shape, an explicit objective with a stated risk penalty rather than a manager agent deciding in prose. What does not carry over is the one-to-one mapping. My technical, fundamental and volatility agents all look at the same CSE universe, so their weights are influence over a shared decision rather than capital across separate books, and I cannot collapse the two the way this paper does. That difference is the cleanest statement I have of what my weighting function has to do that this one does not.
- **The conditioning comparison is now a three-way contrast and this paper sits in the middle of it.** MacroHFT conditions continuously on trend slope and a realised-volatility tertile but over six identical sub-agents. TradingAgents conditions on nothing. HedgeAgents conditions on a 30-day calendar clock for allocation and on a hand-set amplitude threshold for emergencies, 5 % daily or 10 % over three days, which is market-state-aware in the trigger but not in the weights, since the thresholds are fixed and the allocation between them is not revisited. <font color="#ffc000">My contribution is the continuous version, weights that are a function of liquidity and persistence at every step rather than a scheduled re-solve punctuated by a threshold alarm</font>, and I should put that three-way contrast in the review chapter as a table.
- **The event-triggered conference is the most transferable mechanism here and it solves a cost problem I have not yet sized.** Thirty-six emergency sessions over nine months against fifteen dollars of API spend over three years, which works because the expensive deliberation is event-driven rather than daily. My four agents plus an orchestrator over roughly 250 CSE trading days, repeated across three weighting schemes and several ablations, is where my budget goes, so the lesson is to make the costly deliberation conditional on a state trigger and keep the per-day path lightweight. The CSE analogue of their amplitude threshold should be an Amihud illiquidity spike or a volatility-forecast jump rather than raw price amplitude, since a thin book can produce a 5 % move on trivial volume.
- **The uncalibrated-expected-return problem is my comparable-confidence problem with the consequences made visible, and it changes what I have to build.** Their optimiser consumes three LLM-authored return forecasts as if they were estimates. Mine consumes confidence scores from a technical agent, a retrieval-grounded fundamental agent and an econometric volatility agent, which is worse, because those three do not even share a generating process. The decision this forces is that <font color="#ffff00">each agent's confidence has to be calibrated against its own realised hit rate on a held-out period before it enters the weighting function</font>, reported as a reliability curve per agent, so that the orchestrator combines calibrated probabilities rather than self-reported numbers. That is a component of the build I had listed as an open question and this paper is the reason to promote it to a work item.
- **Their diversity metrics are the precedent for the weight-entropy diagnostic I have already pre-registered, and the precedent comes with a warning.** Reporting entropy and effective number of bets alongside returns is exactly the discipline I committed to, so I can cite this as prior practice rather than justifying it from scratch. The warning is that their entropy of 3.13 reads as healthy diversification while their effective number of bets of 1.53 says the portfolio is concentrated, and without definitions a reader cannot tell which to believe. So my entropy diagnostic needs its formula stated in the text, its range given, and a second concentration measure beside it, or it will be as uninterpretable as theirs.
- **The three-year test window is the standard I should be arguing my own protocol against, and it makes my data constraint the binding problem.** Three years across three asset classes plus a post-cutoff replication on 2024 is far better evidence than sixty days on three stocks, and it is why the drawdown result here is credible where TradingAgents' Sharpe of 8.21 was not. The only confirmed ready-made CSE set covers twelve months, which against this benchmark is short enough that risk-adjusted figures will be fragile, so pushing the CSE data request through and establishing how far back `cse.lk` retention actually goes is now the item gating the evaluation chapter rather than a convenience.
- **The backbone result is a reason to run my own backbone check and to report it honestly.** A six-billion-parameter open model beating GPT-4 on Sharpe, Calmar, Sortino, drawdown and volatility inside the same architecture signals that risk-adjusted performance here is not purely an architecture property. If my orchestration advantage shrinks when the backbone changes, that is a finding about my contribution and has to be reported either way, and it raises the practical possibility that a smaller local model is adequate for the CSE agents.
- **The hedging mechanism itself does not port to the CSE and I should say so rather than borrowing the framing.** The diversification here works because Bitcoin, the Dow and the yuan fail at different times. The CSE is one market with heavy sector concentration and a handful of holders large enough to move prices, so cross-asset offsetting of this kind is not available, and the balance my system can offer has to come from conditioning exposure on liquidity and volatility state instead. That is a genuine difference in the problem rather than a gap in their work, and it belongs in the review chapter as the reason the hedge-fund template stops at the orchestration layer.
