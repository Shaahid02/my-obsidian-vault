*Journal of Financial Economics* 161, Ardia at HEC Montréal, Guidotti at USI Lugano, Kroencke at FHNW and Remaco Asset Management, and the paper introduces <font color="#ffff00">EDGE</font>, an Efficient Discrete Generalized Estimator of the effective bid–ask spread computed from open, high, low, and close prices and nothing else. The contribution has two halves worth keeping separate. The first is an analytical correction: every low-frequency spread estimator before this one assumes prices are observed continuously, meaning at least one trade between any two time instants, and when that fails the estimate is biased downward. <font color="#ffc000">Ardia et al. derive estimators that hold under discretely observed prices, with a correction term built from the probability that the open or close coincides with the high or low, which is a direct consequence of too few trades per period.</font> The second is an efficiency argument: prior estimators use a subset of the four prices and discard the rest, so the authors derive several estimators from different price combinations and combine them by GMM to minimise estimation variance. The result is validated on roughly 1.6 million CRSP stock-months against a TAQ-based benchmark, then applied to US stocks back to 1926 and to 2163 Binance crypto pairs.

This is tooling, not a competitor architecture, there is no agent in it and no trading decision, but it goes at the part of my design that is currently weakest. The cost work rests on Amihud illiquidity, turnover and zero-return frequency, all proxies for illiquidity rather than measurements of what a trade pays, and the alternative in the systems literature is the flat percentage that [[MacroHFT, Memory Augmented Context-aware Reinforcement Learning On High Frequency Trading]] and [[Deep Reinforcement Learning Framework for Diversified Dynamic Portfolio Allocation Across Global Equity Markets]] use. EDGE produces an effective spread in basis points per counter per month from daily OHLC, the one data type I am certain the CSE publishes for every listed counter, with no quote feed and no vendor dependency. More than that, the bias story here is about infrequent trading, the defining condition of the market I am working on, so the paper is not just a convenient tool, its central argument is about my setting. It sits alongside [[Online portfolio selection with state-dependent price estimators and transaction costs]] and [[A reinforcement learning approach to optimal execution]] as the measurement layer those papers assume and never supply.

> [!NOTE]
> **Effective spread, quoted spread, and why the distinction carries the paper.** The quoted spread is the posted distance between best bid and best ask. The effective spread is the realised distance between the transaction price and the unobserved fundamental price, $S = 2D(P - \tilde{P})/\tilde{P}$ where $D$ is $+1$ for buyer-initiated and $-1$ for seller-initiated trades. They differ because dealers often execute inside the quotes, and one of this paper's headline empirical results is that the end-of-day quoted spread overstates the effective spread by up to 100%. For a cost model the effective spread is the right object, and it is the harder one, since $\tilde{P}$ is never observed.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **The continuous-observation assumption is universal in the low-frequency literature and it fails in exactly the markets where spread measurement matters most.** Roll (1984) estimates the spread from the serial covariance of closing returns, $S^2 = -4\,\mathbb{C}\text{ov}[\Delta c_t, \Delta c_{t-1}]$, and the squared estimate turns out negative in 50% of cases on a yearly sample of daily closes, which is the problem Hasbrouck (2009) attacks with a Gibbs sampler and Corwin and Schultz (2012) attack by adding high and low prices. Abdi and Ranaldo (2017) combine close with high and low to get $S^2 = 4\mathbb{E}[(c_{t-1}-\eta_{t-1})(c_{t-1}-\eta_t)]$, where $\eta_t$ is the average of the high and low log-prices. All of them require, explicitly or implicitly, that there is at least one trade between any two time instants. When the number of trades per period is finite, which it always is, the estimate is biased downward, and the bias grows as trading thins.
- **A second, compounding problem is that each estimator uses a subset of the four prices, which inflates estimation variance, and the variance then feeds back into the bias through the non-negativity fix.** Because $\hat{S}^2$ can come out negative in small samples, the standard practice is to reset negative estimates to zero, and Jahan-Parvar and Zikes (2023) show this introduces a strong upward bias that does not decline with sample size, with volatility as its primary driver. So higher estimation variance means more truncation means more upward bias. <font color="#ffc000">The net effect the authors identify is that current estimators understate spreads when they are expected to be largest and overstate them when they are expected to be smallest, which is worse than a uniform bias because it compresses exactly the cross-sectional variation a cost model needs.</font>
- **The reframing is to derive estimators under three general assumptions and let the discreteness appear as an analytical term rather than as an error.** The assumptions are that fundamental returns are not serially correlated, that they are uncorrelated with bid–ask bounces, and that bounces are uncorrelated with zero mean. These are weaker than what any of the prior contributions needed, Roll additionally requiring equally likely buys and sells, Corwin and Schultz and Abdi and Ranaldo both requiring a geometric Brownian motion for the fundamental price with constant drift and volatility, which rules out heteroskedasticity and jumps. From the weaker set the authors derive several estimators from different price combinations, then combine them optimally.

The premise doing quiet work throughout is that the effective spread is the whole of the transaction cost, since nothing in the model has an order size in it. The paper never claims otherwise and the title is honest about scope, so I deal with it once, in limitations.

#### Data and setup

Two evaluations, a simulation and a CRSP–TAQ empirical study, plus three applications.

| Component | Setting |
| --- | --- |
| Simulation design | Corwin and Schultz (2012) setup, as reused by Abdi and Ranaldo (2017) |
| Simulated sample | 10,000 months, each 21 trading days, each day 390 minutes |
| Fundamental price | $\tilde{P}_m = \tilde{P}_{m-1}e^{\sigma z}$, $\tilde{P}_0 = 1$, $z$ standard Gaussian |
| Volatility | daily sd 3%, per-minute sd $3\%/\sqrt{390}$ |
| Trade prices | $\tilde{P}_m$ times one minus (plus) half the spread, 50% chance of a bid (ask) |
| Trading frequency | trade probability varied so expected trades per day runs from 1 to 390 |
| No-trade days | previous day's close forward-filled into all four prices |
| Spreads tested | 0.50%, 1.00%, 2.00% through 8.00% |
| Empirical sample | CRSP US Stock Database, 1926–2021, NYSE / AMEX / NASDAQ, share codes 10 and 11 |
| Benchmark | Holden and Jacobsen (2014) effective spread from TAQ, May 1993 – December 2021 |
| Merged sample | 1,637,621 stock-months, CUSIP and ticker matching recovering 99% of CRSP stocks |
| Intraday sample | 711,161 stock-months with both minute and daily estimates, October 2003 – December 2021 |
| Crypto sample | Binance, 2163 pairs, 53,865 pair-months, July 2017 – December 2021, daily / hourly / minute |

Non-transaction-based prices, which CRSP reports as quote-derived midpoints, are treated as missing, as are days where open or close falls outside the high–low range. The resulting missing-estimate frequency in the merged sample is 1.24% for EDGE against 0.03% for CS and 0.14% for ROLL, EDGE's higher rate coming from needing open prices and at least three observations per stock-month. The informative detail is what happens in those cases: where CHL is missing and CS is not, CS returns zero in 90% of cases, and AR in 100%, which the authors read as the asset always trading at the same price so their correction's denominator is zero and the estimate is genuinely undefined. An undefined estimate is more honest than the implicit zero the alternatives produce.

> [!WARNING]
> **The simulation's "infrequent trading" is not frontier-market infrequent.** Panel B of the simulation sets the daily trade probability to 1%, which on a 390-minute day means roughly four expected trades per day, and the bias plot extends down to one trade per day. That is thin by US standards and it is the right stress test for the method, but a CSE counter in the bottom half of a liquidity screen can go whole days without a trade, and the paper's design forward-fills those days rather than modelling a counter that trades on five days in a month. The estimator handles the forward-fill correctly through its indicator; what is untested is the regime where the number of usable observations per stock-month falls into single digits.

#### Model architecture and training

There is no architecture, this is an estimator, and the whole mechanism rests on one indicator variable. Define

$$\tau_t = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } h_t = l_t = c_{t-1} \\ 1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

which flags the case where the high, the low and the previous close all coincide, meaning either that every trade in the period executed at the previous closing price, which becomes increasingly likely as trades per period fall, or that there was no trading at all and the four prices were filled forward. Setting $\tau_t = 0$ on those periods is what prevents the estimator from treating a forward-filled price as evidence about the spread, and conditioning every expectation on $\tau_t = 1$ is what makes the derivation go through without assuming continuous observation.

The spread estimator measured at the open then comes out as

$$S_o^2 \;=\; \frac{-8\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[(\bar{\eta}_t - \bar{o}_t)(o_t - c_{t-1})\right]}{\mathbb{P}[o_t \neq h_t,\ \tau_t = 1] \;+\; \mathbb{P}[o_t \neq l_t,\ \tau_t = 1]}$$

where:

- $o_t, h_t, l_t, c_t$ are the open, high, low and close log-prices of period $t$, and $\eta_t = (h_t + l_t)/2$ is the mid log-price
- bars denote de-meaned returns, $\bar{r}_t = r_t - \tau_t\,\mathbb{E}[r_t]/\mathbb{E}[\tau_t]$, which is the de-meaning that survives the conditioning on $\tau_t$
- the denominator is the entire discreteness correction, and it is the only thing separating this from the classical form: if prices are observed continuously the open and close can never coincide with the high or low, both probabilities equal one, the denominator collapses to two, and the expression reverts to the familiar $-4\mathbb{E}[\cdot]$ shape

<font color="#ffc000">The correction is therefore not a robustness patch bolted onto an existing estimator, it is a term that is identically one under the old assumption and shrinks the denominator, inflating the spread estimate, exactly in proportion to how often the price failed to move within the period.</font> The same construction applied to other price combinations gives four Discrete Generalized Estimators, labelled OHL, CHL, OHLC and CHLO, the first pair measuring the spread at the open and the second at the close. Their CHL estimator reduces exactly to the Abdi and Ranaldo (2017) estimator when continuous observation is imposed and returns are zero-mean, so the prior work is recovered as a special case rather than contradicted.

The efficiency half comes from the observation that these four behave in opposite ways: when the spread is small relative to volatility the estimators using the mid-price have lower variance, and when the spread is large and observed returns are driven by bid–ask bounces, pairing mid-to-open with open-to-mid returns cancels a variance term that pairing with the closing price does not. Combining OHL with CHLO and OHLC with CHLO gives two moment conditions, and GMM with a diagonal weighting matrix reduces to a variance-weighted average, which is EDGE:

$$S^2_{\text{EDGE}} = w_1\,\mathbb{E}[x_{1,t}] + w_2\,\mathbb{E}[x_{2,t}]$$

$$x_{1,t} = \tfrac{\pi_o}{2}(\bar{\eta}_t - \bar{o}_t)(o_t - \eta_{t-1}) + \tfrac{\pi_c}{2}(\bar{\eta}_{t-1} - \bar{c}_{t-1})(c_{t-1} - \eta_{t-1}), \qquad x_{2,t} = \tfrac{\pi_o}{2}(\bar{\eta}_t - \bar{o}_t)(o_t - c_{t-1}) + \tfrac{\pi_c}{2}(\bar{o}_t - \bar{c}_{t-1})(c_{t-1} - \eta_{t-1})$$

where $\pi_o = -8/(\mathbb{P}[o_t \neq h_t, \tau_t=1] + \mathbb{P}[o_t \neq l_t, \tau_t=1])$ and $\pi_c$ is its counterpart at the previous close, and the weights are $w_1 = \mathbb{V}\text{ar}[x_{2,t}]/(\mathbb{V}\text{ar}[x_{1,t}] + \mathbb{V}\text{ar}[x_{2,t}])$ with $w_2 = 1 - w_1$. In estimation the sample counterparts replace the expectations, variances and probabilities, so there is nothing to fit and nothing to tune, which is the practical appeal. Negative squared estimates are handled with $\hat{S} = \sqrt{\max\{0, \hat{S}^2\}}$ applied once to the final estimate rather than within rolling windows, following Jahan-Parvar and Zikes's finding that the within-window version produces an upward bias that never declines. Implementations in several languages, plus the computed spread data, are at `github.com/eguidotti/bidask`.

#### Findings

- **The discreteness term the paper corrects for is large, it is concentrated in small stocks, and it has been shrinking for thirty years, which makes it a measurement of market structure in its own right.** The probability that the daily open or close coincides with the high or low ranges from about 25% for large-cap US stocks to 75% for small-caps over 1926–2021, peaking in the 1930s and 1940s, and collapsing after 2000 with electronification to roughly 2–5% for large-caps while small-caps still sit near 15% at the end of the sample. <font color="#ffc000">The authors' own reading is that the correction is less significant for recent samples and becomes essential for small stocks and for older periods, and crucially that it also depends on sampling frequency, since going from daily to intraday prices reduces the trades observed per interval and pushes the probability back up.</font> So the correction is a function of exactly two things that both bind on my market, how thinly the asset trades and how finely I sample it.

- **In simulation, the two leading alternatives break down under infrequent trading and EDGE does not, with the Corwin–Schultz estimator returning zero below roughly thirteen trades per day.** At 390 trades per day all estimators are unbiased and recover the 1% spread used in the simulation. By 100 trades per day CS estimates 0.75%, by around twenty trades per day it has fallen to 0.1%, and below thirteen it returns exactly zero. AR degrades more gracefully but is still biased down to about 0.30% at one trade per day and 0.62% at roughly two. EDGE tracks 1.00% across the entire range, including simulations where only a single trade per period is expected on average. This is the single most transferable result in the paper for my purposes and I report it as the paper's evidence rather than as a general claim, since the simulation's price process is a geometric Brownian motion and its no-trade handling is forward-filling rather than genuine absence.

- **On variance, EDGE is uniformly the most precise across spread sizes from 0.50% to 8.00%, and the building blocks swap roles in the way the theory predicts.** Under infrequent trading the table below reports the mean estimate with its standard deviation in parentheses, against the true spread in the left column, and the column to watch is CS, whose small standard deviations are the collapse to zero rather than precision.

  Reading across, EDGE is within 0.21 of the true value at every spread size while AR is 1.41 low at 8.00% and CS is 5.34 low, and EDGE's standard deviation is the smallest or tied at every row. The uniformity comes from the building blocks swapping roles, OHLC and CHLO carrying lower variance for small spreads and OHL and CHL for large, with EDGE shifting weight between them rather than being tuned to a favoured region.

- **Empirically EDGE dominates on every cut of 1.6 million stock-months, and its margin is widest where trading is thinnest.** Against the TAQ-based benchmark EDGE achieves the highest Pearson correlation at 78.86% and Spearman at 66.68%, the lowest MAPE at 16.21 and RMSE at 1.23, and the smallest fraction of non-positive estimates at 25.63%, against AR at 68.13 / 53.55 / 19.90 / 1.41 / 31.87 and CS at 45.55 / 33.77 / 35.90 / 2.61 / 29.18. It is highest in every market venue, sub-period, size quintile, spread quintile and trading-frequency quintile. The trading-frequency cut is the one I care about: EDGE scores 74.77% in the least-traded quintile falling to 55.48% in the most-traded, while ROLL runs the other way at 65.10% down to 22.29% and AR falls from 67.81% to 39.36%. The size-decile plot has the same shape, EDGE flat near 70% across all ten deciles while the others spread out below it. <font color="#ffc000">The lead is therefore not an average that washes out in the hard cases, it is largest in the hard cases, which is the property a frontier-market application needs.</font> The comparison is descriptive throughout, no standard errors on the differences and no formal test, which at this sample size is defensible but means the quintile tables are read by eye.

- **Sampling more finely beats sampling for longer by three orders of magnitude, and the empirical gain from minute prices is the most striking number in the paper.** The estimation variance is roughly proportional to $\sigma_1^4/N$ for $N$ observations at per-period volatility $\sigma_1$, so the standard error scales with $\sigma_1^2/\sqrt{N}$. Going from a monthly sample of daily prices ($N = 21$) to a yearly one ($N = 252$) reduces the standard error by $\sqrt{252/21} = 3.5$ times; going instead to a monthly sample of minute prices ($N = 21 \times 390$) reduces it by $390^{3/2} = 7702$ times, which the authors note would take 1,245,699,037 daily prices to match, roughly five million years of trading. The empirical payoff on 711,161 stock-months is correspondingly large: negative estimates fall from 34.15% to 0.02%, Pearson correlation with the benchmark rises from 56.17% to 88.79% and Spearman from 43.47% to 97.31%, MAPE from 23.68 to 5.17. The crypto application makes the point from the other side, AR and CS differing roughly tenfold between daily and minute sampling, 0.18% against 0.02% and 1.85% against 1.45% respectively, while EDGE stays within 0.68% to 0.70% at every frequency, so the frequency-insensitivity is a property of the estimator rather than of the US sample.

#### Limitations

- **The non-negativity truncation is reduced rather than solved, and the residual upward bias is largest precisely where spreads are smallest.** 25.63% of EDGE estimates from daily prices are non-positive in the merged sample, and in the large-cap experiment 41% of stock-months come out negative before truncation. The consequence the authors report themselves is that EDGE averages 0.42% for large-caps in December 2021 against a benchmark of 0.06%, which is a bias roughly seven times the quantity being measured, and they attribute it correctly to the small-sample truncation. <font color="#ff0000">The two mitigations they name, taking $\sqrt{|\hat{S}^2|}$ minus the expected spread under negative estimates, and using the tick size as a lower bound, are both explicitly left to future research and neither is evaluated anywhere in the paper.</font> The practical remedies offered are to widen the estimation window or to use intraday prices, both of which cost something I may not be able to pay. So the estimator's headline property, unbiasedness, holds for $\hat{S}^2$ and not for the $\hat{S}$ anyone actually uses.
- **The three assumptions are weaker than the prior literature's but they still exclude the frictions that characterise a thin book, and none of them is tested against data.** Assumption 1 rules out serially correlated fundamental returns, which sits uneasily beside the long-memory results on the ASPI and SL20 in [[Modeling Long-Term Volatility Memory Dynamics in the Colombo Stock Exchange]], though that tension is about return persistence rather than volatility persistence and I would want to test rather than assert it. Assumption 3 requires bid–ask bounces to be uncorrelated with zero mean, which rules out order-flow persistence, and the authors are explicit that work letting bounces carry information, Chen et al. (2017) being their example, is outside scope. The assumptions are inherited from the literature and weakened, which is a real contribution but not an empirical validation.
- **There is no order size anywhere in the model, so what EDGE measures is the cost of a marginal trade.** The effective spread is computed from the transaction prices that happened, at whatever sizes they happened in, and the estimator has no mechanism for saying what a larger order would have paid. For a deep market that is close enough; for a book where a single order can be a meaningful fraction of daily volume it is the intercept of a cost function and not the function. The scope is honest and never overclaimed, but it means EDGE answers only the first of the four open items in my cost work and leaves the nonlinear impact form, the participation-rate sweep and the thin-book depth calibration untouched, which is where [[HLOB – Information persistence and structure in limit order books]] and the Almgren–Chriss line have to carry the weight.
- **The benchmark is a US quote-based construct, the degradation over time is unexplained in the direction that matters, and the one genuinely illiquid application has no benchmark at all.** Correlation with the Holden–Jacobsen benchmark falls monotonically from 82.93% in 1993–1996 to 53.98% in 2016–2021, which the authors attribute to spreads shrinking relative to volatility. That attribution is plausible and is consistent with the spread-quintile table, where correlation runs 17.84% in the narrowest quintile against 71.38% in the widest, but it is an interpretation of a pattern rather than a tested explanation. The crypto section, which is the closest thing here to an illiquid non-US market, has no benchmark whatsoever, the authors stating plainly that trade and quote data are unavailable to them, so what it demonstrates is internal consistency across sampling frequencies and not accuracy. <font color="#ffc000">I have not found a validation of EDGE against a quote-based benchmark in any emerging or frontier market in the literature I have covered.</font>
- **The estimator needs at least three usable observations per stock-month and the paper never reports performance conditional on a small count.** The covariance structure requires three observations, periods with $\tau_t = 0$ are excluded, and EDGE is missing in 1.24% of CRSP stock-months on that basis, which the authors treat as a minor cost and which is correct for CRSP. The thinnest cut they report is the bottom trading-frequency quintile of US-listed stocks, which still trades most days. For a counter with five trading days in a month the estimator is defined and will return a number computed from a handful of covariance terms, deep inside the small-sample regime where their own truncation bias is worst, and nothing in the paper tells me how that number behaves. That is the gap between what the paper validates and what I would be doing with it.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **This closes the measurement half of the cost model, on the one data type I am certain to have.** The current plan prices trades through Amihud, turnover and zero-return frequency, none of which is denominated in cost, and the alternative on offer in the systems literature is the flat 2bps that the DRL portfolio work assumes or MacroHFT's 0.02%. EDGE gives a per-counter, per-period effective spread in basis points from daily OHLC, which the CSE publishes for every counter with no quote feed required. The concrete step is to compute EDGE over the 374-day per-counter archive and use it as the spread term in the cost function, so the constant becomes a measured number that varies by counter and by date, which is the minimum needed before any statement about cost-aware allocation means anything on this market.
- **The infrequent-trading bias result is the cleanest evidence I have for why thin-book calibration is a real problem rather than a rhetorical one.** Below roughly thirteen trades per day the Corwin–Schultz estimator returns exactly zero, and AR is biased down by about a third at a few trades per day, which means any cost model built on those estimators would charge approximately nothing for trading the least liquid counters on the CSE. That is backwards in the most expensive possible direction, and the simulation figure makes the point more cleanly than anything in the CSE literature does. It is worth citing directly in the proposal when defending the thin-book calibration item, which the revised gap note rates at 3 and which needs this kind of external support.
- **The computation yields two new candidate inputs to the liquidity state, and both measure something the existing three do not.** Amihud is price impact per unit of volume, turnover is activity, zero-return frequency is staleness, and none of them is a transaction cost, so the spread itself gives the liquidity term a component denominated in the same units as the objective. The second is the $\tau_t$ indicator, the fraction of days on which the open or close coincides with the high or low, which falls out of the EDGE computation at no extra cost and is on this paper's own argument a direct consequence of too few trades per period, running at 75% for US small-caps before electronification. It is a candidate staleness measure with a cleaner interpretation than zero-return frequency, since it does not require the price to be literally unchanged and so separates a counter that traded in a narrow range from one that did not trade at all. Both go into the ablation protocol as additions to test rather than adopt, which turns the question from whether liquidity matters into whether a measured spread and a measured staleness rate carry anything beyond the three proxies already there.
- **The truncation bias sets the aggregation window, and that decision has to be made before any of this runs rather than discovered afterwards.** A quarter of EDGE's daily-price estimates are non-positive on CRSP, and 41% negative for large-caps where the spread is small relative to volatility, with the resulting upward bias at 35bps against a 6bps benchmark. A monthly estimate from the per-counter archive is roughly 21 observations, squarely in that regime. The decision is a quarterly window on the per-counter track, accepting a cost term that cannot move within a quarter, and monthly on the index track where ten years of daily data supports it, with the fraction of non-positive estimates reported alongside every spread series in both cases. Reporting that fraction is not optional here, it is the only visible signal that the truncation is distorting the number.
- **The frequency result reverses my default assumption that depth of history is the binding constraint, and that is worth acting on now.** A month of minute prices gives a standard error 7702 times smaller than a month of daily prices, where a year of daily prices gives only 3.5 times, so if any intraday CSE feed exists at any granularity, even hourly or five-minute, it is worth far more to the cost model than extending the daily archive backwards. This is a question to put to the supervisor before spending time on data depth, and it connects to the volatility estimator decision too, since the same constraint that rules out five-minute realised volatility on this market is what would rule out minute-frequency EDGE.
- **EDGE supplies the intercept of the cost function and my contribution has to be the slope, which is a cleaner scoping of the sub-gap than I had.** The effective spread prices a marginal trade and the paper has no order size in it, so the participation-rate sweep, the nonlinear impact form and the depth calibration remain open and remain mine. Framing it as EDGE for the spread and an Almgren–Chriss or queue-reactive treatment for the size-dependent part makes the contribution narrower and more defensible than the earlier wording, and it uses this paper as support rather than letting it look like competition. It also means the cost term entering the objective has two separately validated pieces, which matters because the claim that cost belongs inside the objective rather than deducted afterwards is the part of that sub-gap that actually survives.
- **Claim it as an adopted measurement with the implementation cited, and keep the novelty statement off it.** EDGE is a 2024 JFE paper with public implementations in several languages, open spread data and a Harvard Dataverse replication package, so there is no contribution in running it on a new market and no reason to imply one. The defensible sentence is that I have not found an application of EDGE, or a validation of it, on the CSE or any comparable frontier market in the literature I have covered, and that the contribution is what an orchestrator does with a cost term that varies by counter and date rather than the estimator that produces it. One sentence, offered before anyone asks.
