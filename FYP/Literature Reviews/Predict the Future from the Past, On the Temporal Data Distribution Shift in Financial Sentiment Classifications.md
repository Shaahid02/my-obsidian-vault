EMNLP 2023 main conference, pages 1029 to 1038, Guo, Hu and Yang, The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology. The paper asks what happens to a financial sentiment classifier when the market it was fitted on stops being the market it is deployed into, and it answers with a rolling monthly experiment over three years of <font color="#ffc000">StockTwits</font> data, six model families from logistic regression to GPT-3.5, and three different ways of deciding what data a model gets retrained on. The finding is that every fine-tuned model degrades from in-sample to out-of-sample, that the degradation tracks a measurable <font color="#ffc000">temporal distribution shift</font>, and that the choice of retraining strategy buys you either in-sample capability or out-of-sample stability but not both. The mitigation they propose is a two-stage arrangement, an <font color="#ffff00">OOD detector</font> that flags samples the current model is likely to get wrong, and an <font color="#ffff00">autoregressive model over past models' predictions</font> that answers for those samples instead.

Sentiment is out of my project as of the meeting of 20 September ([[Scope sentiment analysis dropped]]), so I am not reading this for a sentiment pipeline and nothing in the last section proposes one. I am reading it for three things that survive the cut and are not about sentiment at all: the retraining-strategy comparison, which applies to every fitted component I have and bears directly on the CSE data-depth blocker, the $\Delta F1$ framing, which is the cleanest statement I have found of what a robustness metric for an individual agent should look like and also of how such a metric misleads, and the autoregressive-over-past-models construction, which is an orchestration mechanism in disguise. It also supplies a well-documented fixed-influence failure case to sit alongside the FinAgent and AlphaAgents ones already in the matrices, which is exactly the use the supervisor's Literature Matrix exception was meant to permit.

> [!NOTE]
> **In-sample and out-of-sample here mean past and future, not train and test.** The conventional split in the financial NLP literature, and in [[Sentiment Analysis for Sinhala Language using DeepLearning Techniques]], randomises over a pooled corpus, so training and test data come from the same distribution by construction and no shift is possible. Here $(X_t, Y_t)$ is the text and labels from month $t$, the model $M_t$ is trained on data up to $t$, the *in-sample* score is measured on the held-out portion of month $t$, and the *out-of-sample* score is measured on month $t+1$, which the model has never seen and which comes from a different distribution because the market moved. Every number in the paper is an average over 35 such monthly pairs.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **Financial sentiment classification has been evaluated under an assumption that guarantees no distribution shift exists.** The authors point at Malo et al. (2014) and Cortis et al. (2017) as the convention, a dataset randomly split into training and testing so that the two halves are exchangeable, and argue that this measures something no deployed system experiences. The framing is established by construction rather than by an experiment isolating it, which is fine because the construction is not in dispute, but it does mean the contribution is the measurement rather than the observation.
- **Temporal shift had been studied in healthcare and the conclusion there does not transfer.** Guo et al. (2022, 2023a) found pretrained language models robust to temporal shift on clinical prediction tasks such as hospital readmission, and the authors' position is that financial markets move far more violently than clinical practice does, so the healthcare result is the wrong prior. This is the paper's most defensible motivation and it is the one it actually tests.
- **Robustness work in NLP targets non-temporal shift and the two baselines they inherit are built for a different problem.** Spurious tokens masking (Wang et al., 2022) and counterfactual data augmentation (Wang and Culotta, 2021) both address out-of-domain generalisation by intervening on the training data, which presumes the failure is a property of the corpus. Under temporal shift the failure is a property of *when*, and the paper's own results show both methods fail to help, which is a useful negative result even though it is only established on this one dataset.

#### Data and setup

| Item | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Source | StockTwits, a Twitter-like platform for the investing community |
| Period | 2014-01-01 to 2016-12-31, 36 monthly subsets, 35 evaluation pairs |
| Size | 418,893 messages, each with a publish date |
| Labels | User-generated, bullish = 1, bearish = 0, binary, no neutral class |
| Split per month | 7 : 1.5 : 1.5 train / validate / test, random within the month |
| Models | Logistic regression on bag-of-words, LSTM with a linear head, BERT base uncased, RoBERTa base, FinBERT, GPT-3.5 (text-davinci-003) |
| GPT-3.5 protocol | Two-shot in-context learning, examples drawn at random from $(X_t, Y_t)$, no fine-tuning |
| Metrics | $F1_{in}$, $F1_{out}$, and $\Delta F1 = F1_{in} - F1_{out}$, reported per class and averaged |

The label row is the one that shapes everything downstream. These are not annotated sentiment labels, they are self-declared directional tags that posters attach to their own messages, so what the classifiers are learning is a stance about where a stock is going, and what shifts between months is partly the language and partly the population of people posting and what they were willing to declare. The authors treat this as financial sentiment throughout and the equivalence is plausible but untested, and it matters because a shift in the tagging population is not the same object as a shift in sentiment language even though both would show up in their distribution-shift measure.

Before fitting anything they give model-free evidence that a shift exists, defining sentiment positivity as $\#pos/(\#pos + \#neg)$ per month and plotting it against monthly stock returns. The two co-move, with a Spearman correlation of 0.397 for Apple and 0.459 for Tesla, and the empirical conditional probability of a label given a specific token, $p(y\ |\ \text{"Tesla"})$, varies across months.

#### Model architecture and training

The three retraining strategies are the experimental variable and they are worth restating precisely, because the naming is easy to get backwards. **ODNM**, old data new model, trains a fresh model at each $t$ on everything available up to $t$, so it sees the most diverse history. **NDNM**, new data new model, trains a fresh model at each $t$ on month $t$ alone, so it sees only the most recent distribution. **NDOM**, new data old model, initialises $M_t$ from $M_{t-1}$ and continues training on month $t$, so it accumulates knowledge while still adapting, which is continual learning under a different name. Logistic regression and the LSTM have no NDOM row because they build dataset-specific vocabularies and the old model's vocabulary does not carry over.

For the additional analysis they need a scalar measure of how far month $t+1$ has moved from month $t$, and the one they use is a weighted sum of KL divergences over the conditional label distributions for every token in the pretrained tokenizer's vocabulary:

$$\text{Shift}(t, t+1) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_t(y|v)\ \text{KLD}\big(p_t(y|v)\ \|\ p_{t+1}(y|v)\big)$$

where:
- $\mathcal{V}$ is the vocabulary of the pretrained tokenizer, so the measure is defined over subword units rather than words
- $p_t(y|v)$ is the empirical probability of label $y$ among the month-$t$ messages containing token $v$
- the weight on each token is its own conditional label probability, not its frequency, which is an unusual choice I come back to below

The mitigation has two pieces. The first is an OOD detector, and the interesting part is how the training labels for it are manufactured, since nothing in the data says which samples are out of distribution. Given a target classifier $M_i$ and a later sample $(x_j, y_j)$ with $i < j$, restricted to samples that the contemporaneous model $M_j$ classifies correctly so that the sample is known to be learnable:

$$OOD(M_i, x_j) = \begin{cases} 0, & \text{if } M_i(x_j) = y_j\\ 1, & M_i(x_j) \neq y_j \end{cases}$$

So a sample is defined as out of distribution exactly when the old model gets it wrong and a current model would not, which makes the detector a predictor of the target model's errors rather than a density estimator over inputs. The detector itself is a two-layer MLP over the `[CLS]` representation, $f(M,x) = W_2(\text{GELU}(W_1(M^{\texttt{[CLS]}}(x)) + b_1)) + b_2$, trained with cross-entropy while the sentiment model stays frozen, and one set of MLP parameters is shared across all $M \in \{M_1, ..., M_N\}$ regardless of time.

The second piece is the substitute predictor for flagged samples, a linear combination of what the last $p$ models say about the sample:

$$\hat{y}_{t+1} = \sum_{k=0}^{p-1} \alpha_k M_{t-k}(x_{t+1}) + \epsilon$$

where $\alpha_k$ are regression coefficients fitted on past data, $\epsilon$ is the error term, and $p = 3$ empirically. The authors are explicit that this differs from a weighted ensemble in that the weights follow recency rather than being fixed per member, more recent models receiving higher weight, which is precisely the distinction my own orchestration argument turns on one level up. The detector and the AR coefficients are fitted on 2014-01 to 2015-06 and the mitigation is evaluated on 2015-07 to 2016-12, with AdamW, a learning rate grid of $[2\times10^{-3}, 2\times10^{-4}, 2\times10^{-5}]$ and batch size in $\{32, 64\}$.

#### Findings

- **Every fine-tuned model degrades, and the minority class degrades several times harder than the aggregate hides.** All $\Delta F1$ values for fine-tuned models in Table 1 are positive. The averaged figures look survivable, BERT at NDNM losing 4.70 F1 points and RoBERTa 3.96, but the per-class split shows where the loss actually is: BERT's negative-class $\Delta F1$ under NDNM is 8.27 against 1.13 for the positive class, and logistic regression loses 10.04 points on the negative class against 1.07 on the positive. The bearish label is the minority in a corpus that is mostly bullish, and it is the one that stops generalising first. Up-sampling the minority class, reported in their Appendix A, does not change this conclusion.

  ![[Pasted image 20260924000102.png]]

- **The retraining strategies trade capability against stability and neither end of the trade is usable on its own.** NDOM has the best in-sample performance everywhere it applies, BERT at 79.1 average $F1_{in}$ against 72.5 for NDNM and 67.4 for ODNM, and RoBERTa at 80.4, but it carries a $\Delta F1$ of 3.31 and 2.45 respectively. ODNM inverts this completely, with the smallest drops in the table, 1.19 for BERT, 0.97 for RoBERTa, 1.00 for FinBERT, on top of the weakest in-sample scores. The authors' reading, that a long range of historical data evens out the shift, is consistent with the numbers, and I would add the mechanical part they leave implicit: by month 30 an ODNM model's single new month is a small fraction of its training set, so the model barely moves between $t$ and $t+1$ and a model that does not move cannot drop. Two-shot GPT-3.5 sits outside the trade entirely, posting a $\Delta F1$ of $-1.10$ with $F1_{in}$ of 66.4 and $F1_{out}$ of 67.5, flat and weak, and the authors note that its pretraining corpus covers 2014 to 2017 so these months may not be out of distribution for it at all, which makes the flat line uninterpretable as evidence about model scale.
- **The degradation correlates with the measured shift, though the evidence is weaker than the prose claims.** Spearman correlations between $\Delta F1$ and the token-level KL shift come out at 0.385 for BERT ($p = 0.020$), 0.345 for RoBERTa ($p = 0.039$) and 0.281 for FinBERT ($p = 0.096$). The paper describes this as a significant positive correlation, which holds for two of the three at conventional thresholds and not for FinBERT, and a $\rho$ near 0.3 over 35 monthly observations establishes that shift is one contributing factor rather than the mechanism. Taken as the weaker claim it is still the most useful result in the paper, because it is what licenses using a shift signal as a trigger at all.
- **The models lean on tokens with no sentiment content, and the evidence is visible rather than statistical.** Fitting logistic regression to the full corpus and reading off the 30 largest absolute coefficients returns a list where the genuine sentiment words, *bears*, *bulls*, *fall*, *dump*, *strong*, *shorts*, *squeeze*, are a minority among ticker fragments and company names, *incbr*, *nakd*, *plx*, *owens*, *caterpillar*, *kellogg*, *pharmaceuticals*.

  ![[Pasted image 20260924000103.png]]

  The argument the authors build on this is that a model predicting $p(y|v)$ for $v = \text{"Tesla"}$ is not doing sentiment analysis, it is memorising the market's current opinion of Tesla, and it fails the moment that opinion turns. The demonstration is on the most interpretable model rather than on the transformers whose degradation is being explained, so the link between spurious features and the observed $\Delta F1$ is an interpretation the authors offer rather than a result they isolate.
- **The mitigation works, the effect is small in absolute terms, and the detector it depends on is very imprecise.** Against the NDOM baseline the method reduces $\Delta F1$ by 31% for BERT (2.05 to 1.42), 26% for RoBERTa (1.62 to 1.26) and 32% for FinBERT (2.01 to 1.36), with $F1_{in}$ unchanged by construction since the sentiment models are not retrained. Expressed as out-of-sample F1 the same result is a gain of 0.63, 0.36 and 0.65 points. Both inherited baselines make things worse: STM costs BERT 2.09 points of $F1_{in}$ and raises its $\Delta F1$, and CDA costs FinBERT 3.54 points.

  ![[Pasted image 20260924000104.png]]

  The detector underneath achieves OOD recall of 0.86 for BERT and 0.91 for FinBERT at OOD precision of 0.23 and 0.15, which the authors defend on the grounds that recall is what matters when the aim is to catch every sample the model would fail on. That defence is only sound if routing a correctly-classified sample to the AR model is free, and nothing in the paper establishes that it is.

#### Limitations

- **There is no ablation separating the detector from the ensemble, which leaves the paper's own mechanism unestablished.** With OOD precision at 0.23, roughly three in four flagged samples are ones the current model would have handled, so the large majority of the AR model's workload is samples it was not designed for, and performance still improves. The obvious control, applying the AR combination to every out-of-sample prediction and dropping the detector entirely, is not run. If that control matched the reported numbers, the contribution would reduce to a recency-weighted ensemble over model checkpoints, which is a much older idea, and <font color="#ff0000">without it the claim that OOD detection is what produces the gain is the paper's interpretation of a result rather than a result</font>. This is the first thing I would raise about the paper.
- **$\Delta F1$ rewards a model that is uniformly mediocre, and the paper's own table shows it.** The metric is a raw difference of F1 scores with no normalisation by level, so ODNM BERT posts the smallest drop in its block, 1.19, while sitting 11.7 points below NDOM on in-sample capability, and GPT-3.5 wins the metric outright by being flat and weak. The authors see the trade-off and discuss it in prose, but they do not propose a combined criterion, so the table cannot be read down any single column to pick a strategy. Any use I make of this metric has to carry the level alongside the gap.
- **The shift measure weights tokens by conditional label probability rather than by frequency, and it is estimated over a subword vocabulary.** Under the weighting $\sum_v p_t(y|v) \text{KLD}(\cdot)$ a rare token appearing in three bullish messages contributes with weight near 1, the same as a token appearing ten thousand times, so the statistic is dominated by the sparse tail where $p_t(y|v)$ is estimated from a handful of observations and the KL divergence between two such estimates is mostly noise. The paper gives no smoothing, no minimum count and no sensitivity check, and since the 0.385 correlation is the evidence linking degradation to shift, the estimator's behaviour in the tail is load-bearing.
- **The labels are self-declared directional tags on a social platform, so label shift and market regime are entangled by construction.** A StockTwits user tagging their own post bullish is stating a position, not expressing a sentiment that a third party annotated, and when the market turns both the language and the composition of who posts change together. The paper's distribution-shift measure cannot separate these, and the model-free evidence it opens with, sentiment positivity tracking returns at $\rho = 0.397$ and $0.459$, is as consistent with the posting population changing as with sentiment language changing. The authors flag the social-media source as a generalisation limit, which is the right flag on the wrong axis.
- **One platform, one language, one market, three years, and no significance testing on the result that matters.** Everything is US equities on StockTwits from 2014 to 2016, and the authors name news articles and analyst reports as untested. The mitigation table reports single averaged numbers over 18 monthly evaluations with no variance, no repeated seeds and no test against the baseline, so a 0.36-point out-of-sample gain for RoBERTa is presented with the same evidential weight as the 0.65-point gain for FinBERT. Their own analysis is restricted to the monthly horizon, which they identify as a limitation, and it is a real one given that the natural remedy for a model degrading over a month is to re-decide more often than monthly.
- **Nothing connects the performance drop to money, which the authors state plainly.** The paper measures F1 and stops, and a drop of 8 points on the minority class may be economically trivial or catastrophic depending on how the predictions enter a portfolio. They name trading simulation as future work. The same boundary appears in [[HLOB – Information persistence and structure in limit order books]], where $p_\text{T}$ is a round-trip probability rather than a P&L, and it is becoming the recurring shape of the gap between this literature and anything I would have to defend as a trading result.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **This is the strongest available argument that a component's competence is time-varying and measurable, which is what $C_{i,t}$ in the orchestrator has to be.** The open item in the matrices is how an LLM analyst, an econometric estimator and an RL allocator produce confidence on a common scale, and this paper supplies half an answer: for any component that emits a prediction with a later-observable outcome, the gap between its fitted-period accuracy and its next-period accuracy is computable without knowing anything about how it works. Applied per agent and rolled forward, that gives $A_T$, $A_V$, $A_L$ and $A_F$ a comparable backward-looking competence estimate on the same footing as the $p_\text{T}$ construction from the HLOB note. The caution travels with it: a gap metric alone ranks a uniformly weak agent above a strong but drifting one, so the orchestrator needs level and gap jointly, not the difference on its own.
- **The retraining-strategy result maps directly onto the refit policy for every fitted agent I have, and the CSE data-depth blocker forces me into the worst cell of their table.** ODNM, NDNM and NDOM are three answers to a question I have to answer separately for the FIGARCH estimation window, the liquidity model and any RL policy, and this paper's evidence is that the long-history option minimises drift at a real cost in capability while continual update inverts that. The problem is that ODNM presumes a long history, and the only confirmed ready-made CSE set covers February 2025 to February 2026, which does not support it. That pushes me toward NDOM-style continual update, the strategy with the largest minority-class drop here, so the drop has to be measured and reported rather than assumed away. This strengthens the case for treating data depth as the gating item it already is in [[Scope sentiment analysis dropped]], and it gives me a defensible citation for why twelve to eighteen months is not merely inconvenient.
- **The minority class degrading four to eight times harder than the aggregate is a reporting rule for the whole evaluation protocol.** Bearish posts are the minority in this corpus and they are where generalisation fails first, and the CSE analogue is obvious: down moves, thin sessions and the illiquid tail of counters are the minority states, and an aggregate metric over a mostly-calm sample will hide exactly the regime the orchestration argument exists to handle. Every component-level number I report should be broken out by market state rather than averaged, and the friction-aware evaluation layer should be run separately on the illiquid subset.
- **The autoregressive-over-past-models construction is an orchestration mechanism with the recency argument already made, and it is worth testing as a baseline.** $\hat{y}_{t+1} = \sum_k \alpha_k M_{t-k}(x_{t+1})$ weights past versions of one component by recency rather than assigning fixed ensemble weights, and the authors' own framing of why, that the future distribution is an aggregation of recent distributions plus a stochastic term, is the temporal counterpart of my spatial argument about heterogeneous agents. It also sharpens what my Baseline 2 has to be: a static learned weight over agents is one control, and a recency-weighted combination that ignores market state entirely is a second, cheaper one that my state-conditional weighting has to beat before the conditioning set earns its place.
- **The shift-detection idea belongs in the conditioning set as a candidate, and the detector's precision is the warning that comes with it.** $L_t$, $V_t$, $P_t$ and $C_{i,t}$ are a hypothesis about what weighting should respond to, and this paper suggests a fifth candidate, a direct estimate of how far the current input distribution has moved from the one the components were fitted on. The evidence for it is moderate, a $\rho$ of roughly 0.3 to 0.39, and the detector built on it runs at 0.15 to 0.23 precision, so a naive port would have the orchestrator re-weighting on a signal that is wrong three times out of four. If I test a shift input, it gets tested against the always-blend control this paper skipped, and I should expect to report that it does not earn its slot.
- **The spurious-correlation table is a third documented fixed-influence failure case and it is directly citable in the review chapter.** A component whose largest learned coefficients sit on ticker fragments and company names is one whose apparent skill is a memorised snapshot of market opinion, and nothing in the system notices when that snapshot expires. That is the same structure as FinAgent's auxiliary strategies returning 16% against buy-and-hold's 29% and AlphaAgents dropping its sentiment agent for insufficient coverage, and the three together make the fixed-influence argument from three different directions rather than one.
- **It is the cleanest citation I have for why sentiment was investigated and then excluded from scope.** Under the Literature Matrix exception this paper stays in as evidence the area was examined: a dedicated financial sentiment model, fine-tuned on 418,893 in-domain messages, degrades by 8 F1 points on its minority class from one month to the next, leans on tokens with no sentiment content, and needs a second system bolted on to recover a fraction of a point. That is a component with a maintenance burden and a measurable decay rate, and the attribution argument in the scope note applies with force: a 5% return improvement out of a system containing this could not be traced to anything.
