arXiv:2005.13665v3, Zhang, Zohren and Roberts, out of the Oxford-Man Institute of Quantitative Finance. Daily-rebalanced long-only allocation across four index ETFs, where the entire contribution is that the <font color="#ffc000">Sharpe ratio is the training objective itself</font> rather than a metric computed after a forecaster has been fitted, so the network maps raw price and return history to portfolio weights in one differentiable pass and is updated by gradient ascent on realised portfolio Sharpe. The model is called DLS, it is a single-layer LSTM with 64 units and a softmax head, and the asset universe is deliberately tiny, four ETFs tracking US total stock (VTI), US aggregate bond (AGG), commodities (DBC) and volatility (VIX). Tested 2011 to April 2020, so the COVID crash sits at the right edge of the sample. No code release is mentioned.

This is the ancestor of every RL portfolio paper already in the vault and it is worth reading as the minimal case rather than as a competitor to them. [[FreQuant, A Reinforcement-Learning based Adaptive Portfolio Optimization with Multi-frequency Decomposition]] and [[Deep Reinforcement Learning Framework for Diversified Dynamic Portfolio Allocation Across Global Equity Markets]] both replace this objective with a reward and inherit a replay buffer, an exploration schedule and a critic in exchange, and [[Online portfolio selection with state-dependent price estimators and transaction costs]] keeps the explicit objective but drops the learning. Zhang et al. keep the explicit objective and keep the learning, which makes this the cheapest end-to-end allocator in my set by a wide margin and therefore the most plausible control for asking whether my multi-agent apparatus earns its complexity on a short CSE history. The second reason it matters is that its volatility-scaling layer, which turns out to be doing most of the risk work, is exactly the component my persistence sub-gap is about.

> [!NOTE]
> **This is not reinforcement learning, and the paper is explicit about why.** RL maximises an expected cumulative reward, which has to decompose additively over steps to fit an MDP. Sharpe does not decompose that way, since it is a ratio of two moments taken over the whole trading period, so it cannot be an RL reward and the authors say so directly. Instead the loss is computed over the full $T$-step trajectory and gradients flow back through every weight decision that produced it, which is direct policy optimisation of a non-additive objective. The lineage is Moody and Saffell's direct reinforcement rather than Sutton and Barto.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **Forecast-then-optimise trains against the wrong loss.** Classical pipelines predict expected returns and feed them to an optimiser, so the model minimises a prediction error that is not the portfolio's reward, and the paper cites Moody, Wu, Liao and Saffell for the argument that return forecasting is not guaranteed to maximise performance. This is the same end-to-end argument FreQuant makes about bolting an allocator onto a forecaster, arriving twenty years earlier.
- **MPT's distributional assumptions are not met.** Mean-variance requires Gaussian returns so that mean and variance are sufficient for the decision, and the paper points at Cont's stylised facts for fat tails and the resulting extreme losses that a variance term does not price.
- **The asset universe is too large and too correlated to diversify inside.** More than 75% of US stocks are highly correlated with the market index, so adding them to a portfolio buys little, and the paper's response is to trade four index ETFs of different asset classes whose rolling pairwise correlations are shown in Figure 1 to be low or negative across 2006 to 2020. Note the figure also shows those correlations moving substantially over the sample, which cuts against the stability the premise needs.
- **Direct reinforcement was only ever demonstrated single-asset.** Moody and Saffell's framework is named as the origin of this training scheme, but their work optimises one instrument over 1970 to 1994 with no discussion of how a portfolio should be handled, and the paper positions itself as the portfolio extension tested on a modern sample.

The assumption running under all of it is that four asset-class indices are enough breadth for diversification to do its job. That holds only while the four remain weakly correlated, and Figure 1 is the paper's own evidence that the correlations are themselves time-varying, which means the diversification benefit the whole universe choice rests on is a quantity the model never observes and never conditions on.

#### Data and setup

Four instruments, one market, daily observations from 2006 to April 2020, with the model retrained every two years on all data available up to that point and no validation period held out across time:

| Component | Setting |
| --- | --- |
| Assets | VTI (stock), AGG (bond), DBC (commodity), VIX (volatility) |
| Full sample | 2006 to April 2020, daily |
| Test period | 2011 to April 2020 |
| Retraining | every 2 years, expanding window |
| Input features | close price and daily return per asset |
| Lookback | 50 days, so $50 \times 8 = 400$ features per observation |
| Network | single-layer LSTM, 64 units, softmax output |
| Optimiser | Adam, minibatch 64, 10% of training data as validation, stops at 100 epochs |

What to look for there is the test period. 2011 to February 2020 is one of the longest uninterrupted US equity bull runs on record, and a long-only allocator that learns to sit in equities will look good over it almost regardless of what else it does, so the one genuinely adversarial stretch in the whole evaluation is the final two months.

Baselines are seven: four annually rebalanced fixed allocations of the kind pension funds run, at 25/25/25/25, 50/10/20/20, 10/50/20/20 and 40/40/10/10 across shares, bonds, commodities and volatility; mean-variance and maximum diversification, both estimating moments from a rolling 50-day window and updated daily; and the diversity-weighted portfolio from Stochastic Portfolio Theory, which sizes by market capitalisation and carries a theoretical claim of outperforming the index with certainty. Everything runs at $C = 0.01\%$ and again at $C = 0.1\%$, with and without volatility scaling to $\sigma_{tgt} = 0.10$.

> [!WARNING]
> There is no seed averaging, no walk-forward significance testing and no reported hyperparameter search, and the paper frames the last of these as a virtue, saying it purposely keeps the network simple to show the pipeline works rather than fine-tuning the right hyperparameters. That is a defensible stance for a demonstration and it is not a substitute for an error bar. Every number in Table 1 is one training run of one architecture on one fixed sequence.

#### Model architecture and training

The objective is the Sharpe ratio of realised portfolio returns over the trading period, written out so that every term is differentiable in the network parameters:

$$L_T = \frac{E(R_{p,t})}{\sqrt{E(R_{p,t}^2) - (E(R_{p,t}))^2}}, \qquad E(R_{p,t}) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} R_{p,t}$$

where:
- $R_{p,t} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_{i,t-1} \cdot r_{i,t}$ is realised portfolio return, positions set at $t-1$ meeting returns at $t$
- $r_{i,t} = p_{i,t}/p_{i,t-1} - 1$ is asset $i$'s simple return
- $w_{i,t} \in [0,1]$ with $\sum_i w_{i,t} = 1$, the long-only budget constraint

The constraint is imposed architecturally rather than by projection, the raw network outputs passing through a softmax so weights are positive and sum to one by construction, which is the detail that lets the whole thing stay unconstrained from the optimiser's point of view and be trained by plain gradient ascent, $\theta_{new} := \theta_{old} + \alpha \frac{\partial L_T}{\partial \theta}$. Three blocks make up the network, an input layer that concatenates prices and returns across all four assets into a $(50, 8)$ tensor, a neural layer, and the softmax output feeding realised returns into $L_T$.

![[Pasted image 20260927120001.png]]

The neural layer is where the paper's architecture comparison sits, and it is entirely prose. FCN is rejected for severe overfitting on parameter count, CNN for oversmoothing and underfitting because convolution and parameter sharing overfilter the input, and LSTM is chosen because its gating summarises long history with fewer trainable parameters. No numbers are given for any of the three, so this is a reported design decision rather than a measured ablation, and I am treating it as such.

Test-time returns are the piece I would actually reuse, since they carry both the cost model and the volatility scaler in one expression:

$$R_{p,t} = \sum_{i}^{n} \frac{\sigma_{tgt}}{\sigma_{i,t-1}} w_{i,t-1} \cdot r_{i,t} - C \sum_{i}^{n} \left| \frac{\sigma_{tgt}}{\sigma_{i,t-1}} w_{i,t-1} - \frac{\sigma_{tgt}}{\sigma_{i,t-2}} w_{i,t-2} \right|$$

where:
- $\sigma_{tgt}$ is the target volatility, set to 0.10
- $\sigma_{i,t-1}$ is an ex-ante volatility estimate for asset $i$, an exponentially weighted moving standard deviation over a 50-day window on $r_{i,t}$
- $C$ is the cost rate, charged on the change in scaled position, so turnover is measured after scaling rather than before

Two things about that expression matter more than they look. The scaling ratio sits inside the turnover term, so a move in $\sigma_{i,t-1}$ generates a trade even when the learned weight has not changed at all, which means the volatility estimator is a source of turnover in its own right. And the scaler is applied at test time only, so the policy is never trained against the positions it is actually evaluated on.

#### Findings

![[Pasted image 20260927120002.png]]

- **DLS takes the best Sharpe and Sortino in all three regimes, and the margin collapses as costs rise.** Without scaling at $C = 0.01\%$ it reaches Sharpe 1.858 and Sortino 3.135 against the best baseline's 1.228 and 2.932, with scaling at the same rate 1.962 and 3.322 against 1.579 and 2.629, and with scaling at $C = 0.1\%$ 1.403 and 2.327 against Allocation 4's 1.299 and 2.172. So a tenfold increase in the cost rate takes the Sharpe advantage from roughly 24% over the best baseline down to 8%, on a single run with no error bar attached to either number.
- **The edge is in the size of the wins rather than their frequency, which the paper does not remark on.** DLS never has the highest percentage of positive returns in any of the three blocks, 0.537 against MV's 0.562, 0.559 against Allocation 4's 0.565 and 0.547 against Allocation 4's 0.559, but it has the highest average-gain-to-average-loss ratio in all three, 1.518, 1.375 and 1.272 against next-best figures of 1.349, 1.303 and 1.244. It is right slightly less often and right bigger, which is a meaningfully different characterisation from the one the Sharpe column alone suggests, and it is the behaviour I would expect from an objective that punishes the variance of losses.
- **Volatility scaling is doing much of the risk work, and it lifts the baselines more than it lifts DLS.** Scaling equalises the standard deviation of returns at roughly 0.105 for all eight methods, which is what makes the Sharpe comparison fair and is a protocol choice worth copying. But Allocation 1 goes from Sharpe 0.929 to 1.526 and MV from 0.759 to 1.120 purely from the scaler, against DLS's 1.858 to 1.962, so a large share of the unscaled headline gap was DLS simply running at lower volatility than the baselines rather than allocating better. The middle block is the honest comparison and the paper presents all three, which I credit, while its summary sentence reports the best performance across all evaluation metrics except for a slightly larger drawdown. That last caveat is understated: in the scaled block DLS records an MDD of 0.123, fifth of eight, behind Allocation 3 at 0.105, Allocation 1 at 0.111, Allocation 4 at 0.112 and DWP at 0.115, and at $C = 0.1\%$ it is fifth again at 0.125, so for a method whose stated appeal is the confidence to hold through hard times it is mid-table on the one metric that measures holding through hard times.
- **The crisis behaviour is real but the protection comes from the scaler, not the policy.**

  ![[Pasted image 20260927120004.png]]

  Through January 2020 the model holds a diverse book, moves out of equities into bonds in early February after a dip in the stock index, and is concentrated in bonds when the crash starts on 24 February. Bonds then fell too in mid-March, and the paper's own account of what saved it is precise and worth quoting in substance: the original positions did not change much and the scaled positions decreased a lot for the bond index because volatility spiked. So the learned policy stayed in the falling asset and the exposure came down because $\sigma_{i,t-1}$ rose, which is a non-learned component of the test-time pipeline doing the de-risking.
- **Daily rebalancing is what the cost rate attacks, and the boxplots show where.**

  ![[Pasted image 20260927120003.png]]

  DLS delivers the highest realised trade returns on stock and bond sleeves but accumulates by far the largest annual costs on the bond sleeve, around 0.003 against roughly 0.0015 for the reallocation strategies, because it adjusts positions every day while those rebalance once a year and only see daily changes through the scaler. The paper's reading is that the gap diminishes at higher cost rates because of turnover, which the figure supports.
- **The diversity-weighted portfolio collapses under cost, and that is the most useful negative result here.** DWP posts Sharpe 0.493 unscaled, 0.818 scaled, and $-0.753$ at $C = 0.1\%$ with an MDD of 0.627 and an expected return of $-0.083$. A strategy carrying a Stochastic Portfolio Theory claim of outperforming the index with certainty goes to an annualised loss from a cost rate change of ten basis points, which is the sharpest single illustration anywhere in my set that cost assumptions, not alpha, determine rankings.

#### Limitations

- **Everything is one realisation, and the retraining protocol is described in a sentence.** No seeds, no confidence intervals, no significance tests, no repeated splits. The model retrains every two years on an expanding window, which gives roughly five refits across the test period, but the results are reported as one aggregate path with no per-fold breakdown, so there is no way to see whether the advantage is stable across refits or generated in one or two of them. This is the same diagnostic gap I flagged in FreQuant, and it is the contrast that makes Kashif and Ślepaczuk's sixteen-fold walk-forward with HAC and bootstrap testing the protocol standard in my set rather than the exception.
- **The test window is a bull market and the single crisis in it is handled by a component that was not trained.** Nine years of US equity expansion followed by two months of crash, with the crash response traceable to the volatility scaler as the paper's own Figure 5 discussion makes clear. On this evidence I can say the pipeline trains stably and produces a sensible allocation over a favourable sample, and I cannot say the learned policy handles regime change, because the design does not separate the policy's contribution from the scaler's. An unscaled run through Q1 2020 would have answered it and is not reported.
- **How $L_T$ is computed during training is never stated, and it changes what the model optimises.** Sharpe is a ratio of moments over a trading period, the minibatch size is 64, and the paper does not say whether the objective is evaluated per minibatch, per epoch or over the full training sequence. A Sharpe estimated over 64 daily observations is an extremely noisy quantity and optimising it is not the same thing as optimising the annualised Sharpe the results report, so this is a reproducibility hole sitting directly on the paper's single contribution.
- **The cost model is flat and proportional, and the asset choice removes execution friction from the problem by construction.** There is no spread, no market impact, no partial fill and no depth anywhere, just a rate on scaled turnover at two values. Worse for my purposes, the universe is four of the most liquid ETFs in existence, chosen explicitly for high liquidity and small expense ratios, so the setting is one where a flat rate is close to right. <font color="#ff0000">The execution-friction blind spot is not so much unaddressed here as designed out of the experiment</font>, which is now the third portfolio paper I have written that sentence about, though this one at least sweeps the rate.
- **The volatility instrument is named as an index and never as a tradable ticker.** VTI, AGG and DBC are given as ETFs, and the fourth is described as the Volatility Index (VIX). There is no instrument that tracks spot VIX, and the ETPs that approximate it hold futures and carry roll costs that dwarf the one to ten basis points charged here. The paper does not say which series it uses, so I cannot resolve this from the text, but if the backtest trades the spot index then the volatility sleeve's contribution, which Figure 4 shows is the most consistently positive of the four, is not attainable at the stated cost. Flagging it as an unanswered question rather than an error.
- **The architecture claims and the RL framing are both asserted without a baseline.** FCN overfits, CNN underfits and LSTM wins are stated with no comparative numbers, and Section 2 discusses reinforcement learning at some length as a related approach without running a single RL baseline, so the claim that Sharpe cannot be directly optimised under RL is left as an argument rather than an experiment. Both are fair for a short paper and both mean the design choices are reported rather than evidenced.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **This is the right control for whether the multi-agent apparatus earns its complexity, and it is cheap enough to actually run.** A single 64-unit LSTM over a 400-dimensional input with a softmax head and no reward engineering, no replay buffer and no critic is the smallest end-to-end allocator I have found, which matters directly given that the only confirmed ready-made CSE set covers roughly a year of daily data. It belongs beside the training-free OLPS family from Guo et al. in my baseline table, and if a four-agent orchestrator cannot beat a plain differentiable Sharpe maximiser on the same universe and the same costs, that is something I want to find in my own ablation rather than in a viva.
- **The objective is the part to change, and the framework makes that a small edit.** Any differentiable function of realised returns slots into $L_T$, and the conclusion says so, naming Sortino and the degree of diversification as candidates. That is the direct hook for my orchestration gap, because a term that penalises exposure as a function of measured illiquidity, and a term that decays permitted exposure according to forecast variance and persistence together, are both differentiable functions of realised returns and observable state. Framed that way my contribution is a conditioned objective inside a known training scheme rather than a new architecture, which is a much easier claim to defend and a much easier thing to build.
- **Their volatility scaler is the component my persistence sub-gap corrects, and they flag the weakness themselves.** Positions are scaled by $\sigma_{tgt}/\sigma_{i,t-1}$ where $\sigma_{i,t-1}$ is an exponentially weighted 50-day standard deviation, and the conclusion notes that these volatility estimates are lagged and do not necessarily represent current market volatilities, proposing to infer forward volatility inside the network as future work. An EWMA estimator assumes shocks decay exponentially, which is precisely the assumption the long-memory result in [[Modeling Long-Term Volatility Memory Dynamics in the Colombo Stock Exchange]] contradicts for the CSE, and the consequence is concrete: after a shock the scaler re-levers on a schedule that is too fast, so exposure returns while the shock is still decaying hyperbolically. Pairing a forecast conditional variance with a persistence estimate to set a decay schedule on the scaling ratio is an answer to their stated future work and is testable on their own four-ETF setup before I touch CSE data at all.
- **The cost sweep is the experiment I keep saying nobody runs, and running it here nearly inverts the ranking.** Ten basis points against one takes DLS's Sharpe advantage from 24% to 8% and takes DWP from 0.818 to $-0.753$, which tells me that at a CSE-plausible round-trip cost the daily-rebalanced version of this design is probably dominated by an annual reallocation rule. Two responses follow, rebalance less often and treat the frequency as a searched parameter rather than a fixed daily choice, or bring the cost term inside the objective using the closed-form transaction remainder factor from Guo et al. so the optimiser trades off predicted return against cost rather than paying it afterwards. The four portfolio papers now line up cleanly on this dimension:

  | Paper | Cost rate | Treatment | Rate swept |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Zhang et al. (this)** | 1 bps and 10 bps | Flat, on post-scaling turnover, applied at test time | Yes, two points |
| **FreQuant** | 33 bps long, 40 bps short | Self-referential, fixed-point iteration, in the reward | No |
| **Guo et al. (SOPS)** | 5 bps | Self-referential, exact closed form with uniqueness proof | No |
| **Kashif & Ślepaczuk** | 2 bps | Flat, post-hoc on turnover | No |

  Read across the rate column and the only paper that varies its cost assumption is the one with the crudest cost model, which is the gap my own protocol sits in: take FreQuant's or Guo's treatment of the self-referential fee, and sweep it the way this paper does, over a range that reaches frontier-market levels rather than stopping at ten basis points.

- **The asset-class diversification premise does not transfer, and saying so early scopes what I reuse.** The universe exists because four indices across four asset classes are weakly correlated while individual equities are not, and the CSE has no liquid commodity ETF, no volatility index and a government bond market that is not accessible on the same terms. What is tradable is equities plus cash, so the diversification has to come from counter and sector selection inside one asset class, which is the correlated case the paper explicitly says to avoid. What survives the transfer is the training scheme, the softmax budget constraint, the scaling layer and the cost sweep, and what does not is the universe argument, so I should not present this as a CSE-transferable portfolio design.
- **The sensitivity measure is a cheap diagnostic for whether my orchestrator's weights are actually moving.** The paper normalises the gradient of the objective with respect to each input feature,

  $$S_i = \frac{\left|\frac{dL}{dx_i}\right|}{\max_j \left|\frac{dL}{dx_j}\right|}$$

  which gives a time-varying importance per feature at no extra training cost for any differentiable model. That is the same diagnostic role I wanted weight entropy for, showing that a mechanism sold as adaptive is in fact varying, and it is stronger than entropy because it is per input rather than per output, with the caveat that a gradient magnitude is a local sensitivity and not a causal attribution, so it reports alongside an ablation rather than instead of one. Their own result from it is also a usable prior for a short-history market: over 400 features the most recent observations consistently carry the highest weight and importance decays in lag, which leads them to suggest a smaller lookback, and on a roughly 240-day CSE sample a shorter window is doubly attractive because it also increases the number of usable training observations. Pinning the smallest window that still gives a stable volatility estimate is the same open item already sitting against SEMA's $(w, d)$ pair from Guo et al., so the two resolve in one experiment.
- **DWP's collapse is a baseline lesson and a warning about theoretically-motivated strategies.** Stochastic Portfolio Theory carries a formal claim of outperforming the market index with certainty, and the realisation here is unprofitable at ten basis points with a 62.7% maximum drawdown because it turns over heavily. I am carrying a market-cap-weighted or diversity-weighted passive rule as one of my baselines, and this says to report it at every cost rate in the sweep rather than at one, because its ranking is a function of the cost assumption rather than of the strategy.
