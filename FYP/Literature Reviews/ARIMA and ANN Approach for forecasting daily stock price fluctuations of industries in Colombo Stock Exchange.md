2020 IEEE conference paper, Wijesinghe out of the Department of Computing and Information Systems and Rathnayaka out of the Department of Physical Sciences and Technology, both at Sabaragamuwa University of Sri Lanka. A two-stage study that first uses PCA and K-means clustering over the twenty GICS sector series to pick the sector and the individual counter contributing most to the ASPI, then forecasts the ASPI itself with an ARIMA model and a Back Propagation Neural Network and compares the two on MAE and MAPE. The headline is that <font color="#ffc000">BPNN beats ARIMA, MAE 4.708423 against 29.6975 and MAPE 0.1783333 against 0.4672206</font>, and the recommendation to investors is to buy Sampath Bank in the Banks and Finance sector.

This sits on the same baseline layer as [[Stock Price Prediction Using ARIMA Model, Evidence from Colombo Stock Exchange]], the univariate price-only model my agent signals have to beat, and it's the paper that adds the neural comparison to that layer, so between the two of them I have the linear and the non-linear CSE baselines from the local literature. It's also the study that [[Machine Learning-Driven Sri Lankan Stock Market Prediction, Harnessing Economic Indicators and Sentiment Analysis]] is pointing at when its related work says ARIMA and BPNN have been run on ASPI data, so it's a load-bearing citation in the small CSE forecasting chain. What I actually take from it is the specification and the failure mode, because the comparison it reports is not a comparison of two models on the same task, and the numbers in the results tables can't be reconciled with each other.

> [!WARNING]
> **The two reported metric pairs are internally inconsistent, and neither reproduces from the paper's own Table 4.** MAE divided by MAPE recovers the average price level of the evaluation window, and that gives roughly 6,356 for ARIMA against 2,640 for BPNN, so the two models cannot have been scored on the same test set. Recomputing both metrics from the five tabulated days gives MAE 7.886 and MAPE 0.120% for ARIMA and MAE 2.732 and MAPE 0.041% for BPNN, neither of which matches the table. Something other than what's described produced these figures, and the paper prints nothing that lets me work out what.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **CSE-specific comparisons of linear and neural forecasters barely exist.** The authors state plainly that there are very few studies in the literature focused on high-volatility stock price indexes for the CSE, and that to their knowledge there's no strong empirical evidence that economic and financial time series here have been predicted with BPNN fitted against traditional econometric methods like ARIMA. That's a fair reading of the local literature in 2020, and it's still close to fair now.
- **The sector and counter selection question is the actual novelty.** The surveyed work, Ayodele and Adebiyi on NYSE, Yulin Du's ARIMA against BP comparison, Samarawickrama and Fernando's RNN variants on CSE counters, all forecast a series someone else chose. This paper asks which series is worth forecasting first, using clustering over sector contributions, and then forecasts it. That ordering is genuinely different from everything else in my CSE set.
- **The framing is an investment recommendation rather than a forecast accuracy study**, which is unusual for a paper of this shape and is where most of my criticism lands, because the pipeline produces a sector, a company and a price forecast, and validates only the last of the three.

#### Data and setup

ASPI values of the twenty sectors plus daily closing prices of nine listed banks, extracted from the CSE database and supplemented with other financial documents, covering 2008 to 2017. The paper reports <font color="#ffc000">2,221 price samples classified into 20 sectors</font>, with missing values and outliers present and removed by preprocessing along with seasonality and trend. An 80/20 train and test split, no validation partition, no rolling origin. The abstract says the selected sector's businesses are analysed over 2013 to 2017 while the data section and the conclusion both say 2008 to 2017, and nothing reconciles the two windows.

Stationarity is checked with ADF, plotting rolling statistics, and ACF and PACF. The printed Dickey-Fuller output reports a test statistic of −17.03348 with $p = 8.29\times10^{-30}$ on 2,215 observations against critical values of −3.4333, −2.8628 and −2.5675, and states in its own header that the series is stationary at the 5% level. The text underneath then says that test statistic greater than critical value means the data set is non-stationary and proceeds to difference it anyway. The differencing plot that follows runs on a $\pm0.10$ vertical scale, which is a returns scale rather than an index-points scale, so by this point at least two transformations are in play and the paper never says which series feeds which stage.

#### Pipeline

The flow is preprocessing, then feature engineering, then a fork where clustering picks what to invest in while ARIMA and ANN forecast the ASPI in parallel, with the two branches only rejoining at the recommendation.

PCA on the twenty sector series finds five components covering the variance, and K-means over the component loadings produces an average contribution to clusters per sector: Banks and finance 3.16, Diversified 2.51, Hotels and travels 2.50, IT 2.20, Motors 2.66. The quantity itself is never defined, it has no units, no formula and no dispersion, and the table is presented in descending order with Motors at 2.66 sitting below Hotels at 2.50, so even the ordering doesn't hold. Repeating the exercise inside Banks and Finance gives four components out of nine and cluster contributions of NTB 1.95, NDB 2.34, Sampath 2.39 and Commercial 2.08, with the other five listed banks absent from the table entirely, and Sampath wins by 0.05 on the undefined scale.

#### Model specification

Order selection is on AIC, SBIC and HQIC, and the selected model is ARIMA(4,1,3) at AIC 71180.553, BIC 71243.107 and HQIC 71202.003. The candidate grid is never shown, so I can't tell how many orders were compared or how close the runner-up was, which is the one thing I most wanted after the 0.00001 AIC separation in the other CSE ARIMA paper.

![[Pasted image 20260927190002.png]]

The fitted model, written out from the printed coefficients, is what I'd reimplement from:

$$\Delta y_t = 0.7765 + \sum_{i=1}^{4}\phi_i\,\Delta y_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t + \sum_{j=1}^{3}\theta_j\,\varepsilon_{t-j}$$

where:
- $\Delta y_t$ is the first difference of the ASPI, and the constant 0.7765 is the daily drift, insignificant at $t = 1.562$ and $p = 0.118$
- $\phi = (-0.3832,\; 0.4989,\; 0.8402,\; -0.1507)$, all significant at $p = 0.000$, summing to 0.805
- $\theta = (0.5995,\; -0.3631,\; -0.8877)$, all significant at $p = 0.000$, summing to −0.651
- $\sigma_\varepsilon = 24.405$ index points, from the reported S.D. of innovations

Two things in that printout matter more than the coefficients. The header reports <font color="#ffc000">7,712 observations</font>, which is more than three times the 2,221 samples the data section describes and is roughly thirty years of daily trading rather than ten, so the series the ARIMA was fitted on is not the series the paper describes. And the moving average coefficients summing to −0.651 is partway toward the −1 that signals a difference being undone by the MA polynomial, which combined with an ADF that had already rejected the unit root is consistent with over-differencing, though I can't confirm it because the printout is cropped immediately below the "Roots" header where the inverted roots would have been.

The BPNN is a two hidden layer feedforward network trained for 100 epochs, ReLU on the hidden layers and sigmoid on the output, with the neuron count per layer searched between 10 and 100 by trial and error. The selection criterion for that search isn't stated, and on an 80/20 split with no validation partition the only held-out data available to select against is the test set.

#### Findings

- **The two models are close on the tabulated week and both beat persistence there, which the paper never checks.** Over the five days in Table 4 the actual ASPI falls from 6,674.91 to 6,535.09 and both forecasts track the fall, ARIMA to within 14.99 points at worst and BPNN to within 6.92. A no-change forecast anchored on the previous day's actual gives a mean absolute error of 34.96 over the four days where it can be computed, against 9.23 for ARIMA and 2.90 for BPNN, so unlike [[Stock Price Prediction Using ARIMA Model, Evidence from Colombo Stock Exchange]] this paper's tabulated forecasts do clear the naive baseline. That's the strongest thing in it and the authors don't claim it.

  ![[Pasted image 20260927190003.png]]

- **Those same numbers are too good for the task the table says they describe.** A five-day-ahead dynamic forecast of a daily index landing at roughly a quarter of the naive error for ARIMA and a twelfth for BPNN would be a remarkable result on any market, and the pattern is far more consistent with one-step-ahead prediction refit against each realised value. The BPNN plot supports that reading, since the predicted line tracks every local turn of the true line across roughly 1,540 test points with a small lag, which is the signature of a network that has learned close to the identity map on the most recent observation:

  $$\hat{y}_{t+1} = f_\theta\!\left(y_t, y_{t-1}, \dots, y_{t-p}\right), \qquad \mathbb{E}\left|y_{t+1} - \hat{y}_{t+1}\right| \longrightarrow \mathbb{E}\left|y_{t+1} - y_t\right| \;\text{ as } f_\theta \to \text{identity in } y_t$$

  ![[Pasted image 20260927190004.png]]

- **If the BPNN is one-step and the ARIMA is multi-step, the 6.3-fold MAE gap is mostly a horizon artefact rather than a model result.** I can't settle this from the paper, both models are described as forecasting the coming week and no horizon is stated for either, but the implied evaluation levels of 6,356 and 2,640 already say the two were scored on different data, and a horizon mismatch is the simplest thing that produces both that and the size of the gap. I'm reporting this as the most likely explanation, not as something the paper confirms.
- **The BPNN plot cannot be the ASPI over 2008 to 2017.** Its vertical axis runs from 10,000 to 20,000 while the ASPI traded between roughly 1,500 and 7,800 across that decade and the Table 4 actuals sit near 6,600, and its horizontal axis runs to about 1,540 points, which is 20% of 7,712 rather than 20% of 2,221. The observation count in the ARIMA printout and the length of this plot agree with each other and disagree with the stated dataset, so whatever series both models actually saw, the data section doesn't describe it.
- **The sector recommendation reduces to buying the index.** Banks and Finance contributing most to the ASPI is a statement about capitalisation weight, since the ASPI is capitalisation-weighted and the largest sector mechanically dominates it, and the paper converts that into advice to invest there. No forward return is computed for the recommended sector or for Sampath Bank, no comparison against the other nineteen sectors' realised performance appears, and the recommendation is never evaluated at all.

#### Limitations

- **The error metrics don't reconcile, which puts the central claim out of reach.** The implied evaluation levels differ by a factor of 2.4 between the two models, the abstract reports ARIMA's MAPE as 0.472206 while Table 5 says 0.4672206, and neither figure reproduces from Table 4. <font color="#ff0000">On this evidence I can't verify the comparison the paper exists to make</font>, and I'd want the per-day predictions over the full test window before citing the ranking as anything more than a direction.
- **No residual diagnostics anywhere, and no variance modelling on a window containing 2008.** There's no Ljung-Box test on the ARIMA residuals, so the Box-Jenkins adequacy step is skipped rather than failed, which is a step down from the other CSE ARIMA paper where at least the correlogram was printed even though the conclusion drawn from it was wrong. There's also no ARCH test and no conditional variance model across a decade opening on the global financial crisis, which [[Sri Lankan Stock Market Volatility Analysis, An ARMA-GARCH Approach]] and [[Modeling Long-Term Volatility Memory Dynamics in the Colombo Stock Exchange]] both establish is the wrong assumption for this market.
- **The clustering stage is not reproducible.** The average contribution to clusters has no definition, no formula and no units, the relationship between PCA loadings and K-means membership is described only as each feature's contribution being analysed using K-means, the elbow method is named but the chosen $k$ is never reported, five of the nine banks are missing from the results table, and the final recommendation turns on a 0.05 difference in the undefined quantity. This is the half of the paper with the novel idea in it and it's the half I can't rebuild.
- **Everything is selected and evaluated on one split with no repetition.** One 80/20 partition, one forecast origin, one seed, no rolling or expanding window, no confidence intervals, and a neural architecture search over neuron counts from 10 to 100 with no validation partition to select against. Given that the same 2020s CSE literature already contains [[Machine Learning-Driven Sri Lankan Stock Market Prediction, Harnessing Economic Indicators and Sentiment Analysis]] treating a shuffled split as a design improvement, protocol discipline is the thing the local work most consistently lacks.
- **The stationarity handling contradicts itself and probably over-differences.** The ADF output says stationary at the 5% level, the text says non-stationary, $d = 1$ is applied, and the resulting MA polynomial sums to −0.651. If the input series was already the returns series that the $\pm0.10$ differencing plot suggests, then the ARIMA is differencing returns, which inflates the MA structure and is consistent with what the fitted coefficients look like. I can't prove this without the roots, and the printout cuts them off.
- **An investment recommendation with no strategy, no costs and no execution.** The paper tells investors to buy a specific counter on a market where [[Role of Market Liquidity in Sentiment-Based Return Predictions, Evidence from Sri Lanka]] finds unexpected illiquidity shocks depressing returns by 8 to 10 basis points, and nothing in it takes a position, applies a fee, models a spread or computes a return. The authors are honest about the sample not covering pre-2008 and about black swan events being unpredictable, and the future work pointing at Fourier-modified ARIMA with ANN plus sentiment analysis is the right direction, it's just that none of it addresses whether the recommendation was any good.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **Horizon and refit policy go in my results table as columns, next to the baseline columns.** This paper is the reason. Two models get compared, one of them is probably one-step-ahead and the other probably isn't, and the reader has no way to tell because neither the forecast horizon nor the refitting scheme is stated anywhere. Every forecast number my system reports carries the horizon, whether the forecast was dynamic or refit against realised data, and the origin count, and those three fields are fixed columns rather than prose.
- **I score with a scaled error against the in-sample naive, so different horizons stay comparable.** MAPE on an index level near 6,600 makes a 30-point error look like 0.47% and tells me nothing, so the quantity I report is:

  $$\mathrm{MASE} = \frac{\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\left|y_{T+h} - \hat{y}_{T+h}\right|}{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{t=2}^{n}\left|y_t - y_{t-1}\right|}$$

  where the denominator is the mean absolute one-step change over the training window, so a value below 1 means the model beat no-change and the number stays interpretable across counters with different price levels. On the four comparable days in Table 4 this paper scores roughly 0.26 for ARIMA and 0.08 for BPNN, and it's precisely because those are implausibly strong for a week-ahead forecast that the scaled number is the one worth printing.
- **My ARIMA baseline for the CSE inherits this specification and adds the diagnostics both local papers skipped.** Low-order ARMA on the differenced daily index, order chosen on out-of-sample scaled error across rolling origins with the information criteria as a tie-breaker, but with the ADF result actually determining $d$ rather than being overridden by the text, a Ljung-Box pass as a hard gate, and the candidate grid printed. Between this paper's ARIMA(4,1,3) on eight parameters and the other's subset (2,1,1), I have a sensible order range to search rather than a single inherited order.
- **The neural baseline stays a plain two-hidden-layer MLP on price history, and I state upfront that it's close to a persistence mimic.** A network that tracks the index with a one-day lag is reproducing yesterday's close and the plot here shows exactly that, so the honest use of a BPNN or LSTM price-only baseline in my work is as a floor that the agent ensemble has to clear, not as a component that carries signal. [[Deep Learning with Long Short-Term Memory Networks for Financial Market Predictions]] gives me the properly evaluated version of the same family to calibrate expectations against.
- **The capitalisation-weight confusion is a direct warning for how my orchestrator ranks counters.** Contribution to index variance is not expected return, and any selection layer I build that scores counters by how much they move the ASPI will rediscover Banks and Finance for arithmetic reasons. My selection has to be on forecastable excess return net of costs, evaluated forward, and if a sector or counter ranking appears in my system it gets a realised-return column beside it rather than standing on a clustering score.
- **Concentration in Banks and Finance is still a real structural fact I can use, just not as a recommendation.** If a handful of banking counters dominate ASPI variance, then cross-counter dependence on the CSE is concentrated rather than diffuse, which matters for how I weight a portfolio and for how much independent information my per-counter agents actually carry. I'd want to re-establish that with a variance decomposition on returns over my own window before leaning on it, because a 2008 to 2017 PCA on levels isn't evidence about today's market.
- **Any quantity that decides something in my system gets a formula and a dispersion estimate.** The average contribution to clusters is the cautionary example, a number with no definition that nevertheless selects the sector, the counter and therefore the entire recommendation, and it fails on a 0.05 margin. Where my orchestrator produces a per-agent reliability weight, the definition goes in the write-up, the sensitivity of the decision to that weight gets reported, and a margin that thin gets treated as a tie rather than a ranking.
- **This is the second CSE forecasting paper in my set whose results tables contradict its own text, and that shapes how I use the local literature.** Between the Ljung-Box misreading in one and the unreconcilable metrics here, I take specifications, windows and variable choices from these papers and recompute every headline number I intend to cite. It also sharpens what my own contribution is, because on this market the shortage isn't models, it's evaluation that survives being checked.
