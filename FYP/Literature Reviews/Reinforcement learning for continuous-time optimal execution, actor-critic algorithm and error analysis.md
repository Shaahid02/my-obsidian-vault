Finance and Stochastics 30(3), 2026, 597-655, Wang, Gao and Li, all at the Department of Systems Engineering and Engineering Management at CUHK, received October 2023, accepted February 2025, published online 10 March 2026, open access. The paper takes the <font color="#ffc000">parent-order liquidation problem</font> in continuous time under the Almgren-Chriss model, regularises it with Shannon entropy to induce exploration, solves the resulting <font color="#ffff00">exploratory mean-quadratic-variation (EMQV)</font> control problem in closed form, and then uses that closed form as the parametrisation for an actor-critic RL algorithm with five learnable scalars in total. The algorithmic contribution is a third update step, <font color="#ffff00">recalibration</font>, sitting alongside the usual policy evaluation and policy gradient, and the theoretical contribution is a finite-time error bound with conditions on the learning rates under which the algorithm converges linearly to the global optimum. It is tested on three simulators, an Almgren-Chriss simulator, a historical-order-flow simulator built from LOBSTER data, and a stochastic order book model, against the classical estimate-then-plug-in approach and against SAC. No code release is mentioned.

This is the third execution paper in the vault and it occupies the position the other two leave empty. [[A reinforcement learning approach to optimal execution]] solves child-order timing by assuming the order is infinitesimal so the book cannot react, [[Cost-Efficient Reinforcement Learning for Optimal Trade Execution on Dynamic Market Environment]] makes impact endogenous through a queue-reactive simulator and learns model-free with planning on top, and Wang et al. keep impact but write it down parametrically, which buys an analytical solution and therefore a policy with almost no parameters to learn. The distance from my own build is the same as with the other two, one agent liquidating one position with no portfolio and no orchestration, but what I want here is more specific: this is the strongest available argument that <font color="#ffc000">a small model-derived parametrisation can beat a large model-free one when data is scarce and the model is wrong</font>, which is the situation I expect on the CSE. It also supplies a risk-adjusted execution objective and a TWAP-relative reporting convention that the other two notes lack.

> [!NOTE]
> **The risk measure is quadratic variation along the path, not terminal variance, and the difference is what makes the problem time-consistent.** Almgren and Chriss use a mean-variance objective measuring risk only at the end of the horizon, which is famously time-inconsistent, so the optimal precommitted policy is not optimal when reconsidered mid-execution. Forsyth et al. replace it with the quadratic variation of the mark-to-market portfolio value, $\mathbb{E}[\langle P^\nu \rangle_T] = \mathbb{E}[\int_0^T \sigma^2 S_0^2 (q_t^\nu)^2 dt]$, which charges risk continuously in proportion to squared inventory held. Under arithmetic Brownian motion the resulting optimal inventory trajectory is identical to the mean-variance one, so nothing is lost in the classical solution, and the dynamic programming problem becomes time-consistent, which is what allows an RL agent to learn one stationary feedback policy rather than a schedule committed to at $t=0$.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **Actor-critic RL in finance parametrises with neural networks, and nobody shows those algorithms converge.** The authors' position is that DDPG and SAC use general function approximation with a large number of parameters, which makes performance sensitive to engineering choices, makes the learned policy uninterpretable, and offers no convergence guarantee in general. They cite divergence actually observed in some cases of the mean-variance actor-critic of Jia and Zhou, so this is not purely hypothetical. The proposed alternative is to solve the exploratory control problem analytically first and let the solution dictate the functional form, which is the same move Wang and Zhou made for mean-variance portfolio selection and which this paper ports to execution.
- **The continuous-time RL theory covers policy evaluation and policy gradient separately but not their interaction, and the execution problem sits outside its existing templates anyway.** Wang et al. (2020) introduce the entropy-regularised exploratory control framework and Jia and Zhou develop policy evaluation, policy gradient and q-learning in continuous time, but none of it addresses parametrisation or the convergence of actor-critic learning where the two updates compete, which is where this paper's error analysis lands and the part with the widest applicability. The problem itself is linear-quadratic but nonstandard, because the terminal condition assigns $-\infty$ to any nonzero inventory at $T$ to force exact liquidation, so the HJB equation has to be solved from scratch and the resulting value function carries $\coth(K(T-t))$ terms that blow up at the horizon.
- **The classical statistical approach estimates a model and plugs it into a formula, and nobody has tested what that costs out of sample.** Estimating market impact parameters is known to be difficult, and the authors point out that any estimation error gets amplified through the mathematical form of the optimal control, so the interesting question is empirical: how do estimate-then-optimise, model-derived RL and model-free RL compare in a genuine out-of-sample test with a data distribution shift between training and test periods. That question is the one the empirical section is built to answer and it is the most useful thing in the paper for my purposes.

#### Data and setup

The simulation study and the empirical study serve different purposes and it is worth keeping their parameters separate. The row to read hardest in the table below is the risk-aversion row, because it turns out to determine whether the whole exercise is worth doing.

| Item | Simulation study | Empirical study |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Environment | AC simulator, known ground truth | HD simulator (LOBSTER order flow) and CST simulator (Cont-Stoikov-Talreja order book) |
| Data | $S_0=100$, $q_0 = 5\times10^5$, $\kappa = 2.5\times10^{-7}$, $\eta = 2.5\times10^{-6}$, $\sigma = 30\%$ | 7 Nasdaq stocks, 6 sectors, Jul 2016 to Mar 2017 |
| Horizon | $T = 1$ day | $T = 1$ day, 6 hours from 10:00 |
| Inventory | fixed | 10% of ADTV per stock, 57,500 to 374,000 shares |
| Risk aversion | $\lambda^{\text{low}} = \eta$, $\lambda^{\text{mid}} = 10^3\eta$, $\lambda^{\text{high}} = 10^4\eta$ | same three levels |
| Discretisation | $N = 100$, $n = 15$ Gauss-Hermite nodes, $L = 500$ iterations | same hyperparameters |
| Protocol | 100 independent runs, error against known optimum | rolling quarters, train 2016Q3 test 2016Q4, train 2016Q4 test 2017Q1 |
| Comparators | SAC across depth 1-2 and width 128-256 | SAC, and STAT, the estimate-then-plug-in classical approach |

The rolling-quarter protocol is the part I would reuse directly, since training on one quarter and testing on the next means every reported number is genuinely out of sample in time, which is what lets the authors make a claim about robustness to distribution shift rather than just to noise. STAT is re-estimated for every test day using the five preceding days, the most favourable treatment they could reasonably give it, and they note its performance degrades when the estimation days sit further from the test day.

The HD simulator is the interesting one. It replays historical LOBSTER order flow into a reconstructed book, injects the agent's market order, and lets the exchange matching rules carry the consequences forward, so a sell order consuming 100 shares at 104 and 150 at 103 not only pays temporary impact on that fill but permanently changes every subsequent book state, since a historical cancellation of 200 shares at 103 now finds only 50 left to cancel. Impact is simulated in training and in testing, which the authors contrast with Nevmyvaka et al., who simulate it only at test time.

#### Model architecture and training

The analytical core is what everything else hangs off. Adding a cumulative Shannon entropy term to the mean-quadratic-variation objective and allowing stochastic feedback policies $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ gives the EMQV problem

$$\sup_{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Pi}\ \mathbb{E}\Big[\int_0^T\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(-\nu(S_t^{\boldsymbol\pi}+\eta\nu)-\lambda\sigma^2S_0^2(q_t^{\boldsymbol\pi})^2-\zeta\ln\boldsymbol\pi_t(\nu)\big)\boldsymbol\pi_t(\nu)\,d\nu\,dt + h_T(q_T^{\boldsymbol\pi},S_T^{\boldsymbol\pi})\Big]$$

where:
- $\nu$ is the trading rate and $\boldsymbol\pi_t(\nu)$ the density over it, so the whole integrand is an expectation over randomised actions
- $-\nu(S_t + \eta\nu)$ is the execution revenue net of temporary impact $\eta$, and $\lambda\sigma^2S_0^2 q_t^2$ is the running quadratic-variation risk charge
- $\zeta > 0$ is the temperature, the willingness to explore, and $-\zeta\ln\boldsymbol\pi$ is the entropy bonus
- $h_T$ is $0$ at zero inventory and $-\infty$ otherwise, which is what forces exact liquidation

Solving the associated HJB equation gives the result the paper is built on, that the optimal exploratory policy is Gaussian with mean equal to the classical deterministic optimal control and constant variance,

$$\boldsymbol\pi^*(\nu;t,q,S) = d_{\mathcal{N}}\Big(\nu;\ -qK\coth\big(K(T-t)\big),\ \frac{\zeta}{2\eta}\Big), \qquad K = \sqrt{\frac{\lambda\sigma^2S_0^2}{\eta}}$$

and this is the sentence worth carrying: exploration variance is proportional to the temperature and inversely proportional to the temporary impact parameter, which matches the intuition that in a market where trading fast is expensive you should sample less aggressively around your intended rate. The variance does not decay as $t \to T$, unlike the portfolio case, because here the control does not enter the diffusion term. Letting $\zeta \downarrow 0$ recovers the classical Almgren-Chriss policy exactly, so the exploratory solution nests the deterministic one rather than approximating it.

The parametrisation falls straight out. The policy takes $\boldsymbol\pi^\Phi = d_{\mathcal{N}}(\nu; -q\varphi_1\coth(\varphi_1(T-t)), \zeta\varphi_2)$ with true optimum $\varphi_1^* = \sqrt{\lambda\sigma^2 S_0^2/\eta_{\text{env}}}$ and $\varphi_2^* = 1/(2\eta_{\text{env}})$, and the value function takes a three-parameter form $\Theta = (\theta_1,\theta_2,\theta_3)$ built from the analytical expression. <font color="#ffc000">Five scalars total</font>, against a SAC implementation carrying several networks of width 128 to 256. Policy evaluation minimises the martingale loss function of Jia and Zhou by one gradient step, and the policy gradient is computed from the analytical value function with the unknown environment parameters replaced by implied counterparts recovered by inverting a $3\times3$ matrix $M(\Phi)$ linking $\Theta$ to $\psi = (\kappa,\eta,\sigma^2)$.

That inversion is what makes recalibration possible, and recalibration is the paper's algorithmic novelty. In standard actor-critic the critic and actor parameters are treated as independent, but here they are linked through the model: after a policy gradient step moves $\Phi^{(\ell)}$ to $\Phi^{(\ell+1)}$, the critic parameters $\widetilde\Theta^{(\ell)}$ still describe the old policy's value function and are a poor starting point for the next policy evaluation. The fix is to hold the implied environment parameters fixed and re-express them under the new policy,

$$\Theta^{(\ell+1)} = M(\Phi^{(\ell+1)})M(\Phi^{(\ell)})^{-1}\widetilde\Theta^{(\ell)}$$

which costs one matrix product per iteration and guarantees, under a learning-rate condition, that the policy evaluation error at the start of iteration $\ell+1$ is strictly smaller than it was at $\ell$ by a factor $d(\ell) \in (0,1-\varepsilon)$. Without it there is no such guarantee, and Remark 5.11 is explicit that the quantity needed to bound the error is not even estimable in that case.

The finite-time error bound in Theorem 5.13 decomposes the performance gap into a shrinkage component from the policy gradient update that decays geometrically, plus two inflation components driven by the policy evaluation error entering through discrete convolutions of the shrinkage and recalibration factors. Convergence needs the inflation terms to die faster than the shrinkage works, which translates into the requirement that the policy steps be sufficiently small. That the actor should move slowly relative to the critic is consistent with the trust-region intuition from Schulman et al., arrived at here from a different direction.

Exploratory revenue is collected by Gauss-Hermite quadrature rather than random sampling: at each decision time the agent computes 15 trading rates from the quadrature abscissae, sends each to the environment, records the revenue, and resets the environment to its state at $t_i$ before the next. Quadrature approximates the integral of revenues under the Gaussian without injecting sampling noise, and it requires being able to rewind the market, which I flag below.

#### Findings

- **Both of the paper's training-design choices show up in the variance of the policy parameter and nowhere else.** Across 100 independent runs at medium risk aversion, recalibration takes the standard deviation of the learned $\varphi_1$ from 43.57 down to 1.17, a factor of 37, while the value function parameters barely move, $\theta_1$ at $1.64\times10^{-10}$ against $1.50\times10^{-10}$; without it the average path overshoots below $\varphi_1^*$ around iteration 150 and settles above it, with it the path descends monotonically. Using the exploratory state rather than the sample state does the same thing again, $\varphi_1$ standard deviations of 1.67 and 1.82 at medium and high risk aversion against 167.60 and 307.54, because the exploratory state has already averaged over action randomness. Both mechanisms are exactly what the theory predicts, a critic left describing the previous policy feeds a biased gradient to the actor, and the recalibration comparison is the cleanest ablation in the paper since only that step changes. The exploratory state is observable here only because the drift of the exploratory price process is the mean of the control distribution, which is a property of this model rather than of execution problems generally.

- **In the AC simulator EMQV converges to the true optimum and SAC does not come close.** Performance gaps against the analytically known optimal policy, in basis points, are $-9.881\times10^{-10}$, $-1.022\times10^{-3}$ and $-3.075\times10^{-4}$ for EMQV at low, medium and high risk aversion, against $-19.311$, $-113.647$ and $-109.405$ for the best depth and width configuration of SAC. The inventory trajectories make the failure mode legible: EMQV traces the optimal curve almost exactly at all three risk levels, while several SAC configurations hold nearly the full position until the horizon and then dump it, and others liquidate far too fast. The authors are careful to say further tuning might improve SAC, and the honest reading is that in an environment where the model is exactly right, a five-parameter policy derived from that model is very hard to beat, which is close to a tautology but still worth the demonstration.

- **Out of sample on real data the picture is much closer, and the paper's own table does not support the strongest version of its claim.** Across 21 stock and risk-aversion combinations, EMQV is the best performer in 9 and SAC in 10, which is not the ordering the abstract's "stands out as the best overall" would suggest on its own. What does hold up is the stability claim: SAC underperforms TWAP in 11 of 21 cases regardless of which simulator trained it, and its standard errors are enormous, AAPL at high risk aversion coming in at $-1.73\times10^5$ with a standard error of 18,323 on the CST simulator, while EMQV's standard errors stay in the same order of magnitude as its means. The case for EMQV here is consistency rather than peak performance, and that is a defensible claim from this evidence.

- **The classical estimate-then-plug-in approach degrades sharply exactly where the risk term starts to matter.** STAT is essentially indistinguishable from TWAP at low risk aversion, shows a decent but smaller improvement than EMQV at medium, and at high risk aversion turns in $-905.742$ on AAPL and $-203.392$ on MSFT against EMQV's $+102.285$ and $+85.983$ on the historical simulator. The authors' explanation is that estimation error in the AC parameters is amplified through the closed-form control, and since $K$ scales as $\sqrt{\lambda\sigma^2S_0^2/\eta}$, a fixed relative error in $\eta$ produces a larger absolute error in the trading rate as $\lambda$ grows. This is the result I would cite, because it separates learning the information-to-action mapping from estimating a model and then computing it, on the same model and the same data.
- **At low risk aversion none of this is worth doing, and the paper says so.** EMQV's improvement over TWAP at $\lambda^{\text{low}}$ is marginal across all seven stocks, several entries being $-0.020$ to $+0.046$ basis points, because TWAP is nearly optimal for a risk-neutral agent. Whether execution scheduling is worth any of this machinery is therefore a question about how much the trader actually cares about inventory risk over the horizon, not a question about algorithms.

#### Limitations

- **The parametrisation cannot express anything the Almgren-Chriss model cannot, and every advantage in the paper traces back to that.** Linear permanent impact, linear temporary impact, arithmetic Brownian motion with constant volatility, no spread, no depth, no order imbalance, no intraday seasonality in any of them. The authors are upfront that this is model misspecification error and that SAC could in principle do better with richer features, and their own empirical table, where SAC wins 10 of 21 cases on real data, is consistent with that being a real cost rather than a theoretical one. <font color="#ff0000">The five-parameter policy is a rigid functional form, and on a market whose impact is materially nonlinear in participation rate it will be rigidly wrong</font>, which is precisely the situation a thin frontier order book creates.
- **The convergence guarantee is real but not operational.** Theorem 5.15 gives linear convergence provided the learning rates satisfy conditions involving $L_G$, $\mu_G$, $\varepsilon_\Lambda$ and $c_{\text{RC}}$, and the paper states plainly that the factors determining these are not easy to estimate. The iterates must also be constrained to a compact rectangle fixed in advance, and Remark 5.9 notes the constants degrade as that rectangle grows, so the guarantee is conditional on a box whose right size is unknown. The analysis also excludes time-discretisation, quadrature and finite-sample error by construction, a reasonable scoping decision that means the theorem covers a different algorithm from the one in the experiments.
- **Exploration requires rewinding the market, which no live venue permits.** Collecting the exploratory revenue means sending 15 orders of different sizes at the same decision time and resetting the environment between each, feasible in a simulator and impossible against a real exchange. The sample-state variant needs no reset and works, at orders of magnitude more variance, so the configuration carrying the headline results is simulator-only and the deployable one is the weaker of the two, a distinction the paper makes but does not foreground.
- **Market orders only, no spread, no queue, no fees, and the last step dumps whatever is left.** Remark 4.4 concedes that after discretisation the zero-inventory target is not met by the policy itself, so the implementation simply trades all remaining shares at $t_{N-1}$, which is a forced market order of unmanaged size. There is no passive execution anywhere, no partial fill, no cancellation, and no commission in the objective, so the entire savings available from earning the spread rather than paying it is outside the model. This is the same restriction as in the queue-reactive paper and it bites harder in a wide-spread market.
- **Seven large-cap Nasdaq names, two test quarters, one horizon, one participation rate.** Everything runs at 10% of ADTV over one day, with no sweep over order size even though impact is the subject and impact scales with size, and the test window is 2016Q4 and 2017Q1, a calm period with no regime break to test the robustness claim against. The paper's argument that EMQV is more robust to distribution shift than STAT rests on a shift between adjacent quarters in a stable market, which is the mildest available version of that test.
- **The comparison against SAC is not tuned symmetrically.** SAC's depth and width are selected from the AC simulation study and then carried into the empirical study, while EMQV's hyperparameters are also carried over but EMQV has five parameters and almost nothing to tune. The authors acknowledge the reported SAC result may not be the best achievable, which is the correct caveat, and it means the empirical comparison establishes that SAC is harder to configure reliably rather than that it is worse. Those are different claims and only the first is supported here.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **This completes the execution-friction argument with a third position, and it is the one that fits a data-poor market.** The other two notes bracket the problem, impact assumed away and impact learned model-free, and this paper takes the middle route: write the impact model down, solve it, learn only the handful of numbers the solution needs. For the CSE the appeal is direct, since a five-scalar policy can be fitted on a short archive where a SAC agent cannot. The caution that goes with it is the nonlinearity of impact in a thin book, which is exactly what the AC form cannot express, so this is a starting point to extend rather than a method to adopt wholesale.
- **The STAT-versus-EMQV result is the cleanest available evidence for learning over calibrating, and it transfers to the CSE with the effect amplified.** Same model, same data, same closed form, and the only difference is whether the parameters are estimated statistically and plugged in or learned from interaction with the environment; the learned route degrades far more gracefully when the model is wrong. On the CSE, where impact parameters would be estimated from sparse and lumpy turnover data, the estimation error is larger than anything in this paper, so the amplification effect they document at high risk aversion is the failure mode I should expect from a naive calibrate-then-optimise cost model. That is an argument for building the execution component as a learner even if the underlying model is a simple parametric one.
- **Adopt the QV-adjusted PnL as the execution objective, because neither of the other two execution notes carries a risk term.** The quantity to optimise and report is $\text{PnL}^{\boldsymbol\pi} = \mathbb{E}[x_T^{\boldsymbol\pi}] - \lambda\mathbb{E}[\langle\int_0^\cdot q_t^{\boldsymbol\pi}dS_t^{\boldsymbol\pi}\rangle_T]$, terminal cash minus a risk charge on the path of inventory held, reported relative to TWAP in basis points. Moallemi and Wang give me the half-spread unit and Chen et al. give me ATC, and both are pure cost measures that are indifferent between a reliable schedule and a volatile one. Over a one-day CSE liquidation the volatility term is not small, so the risk-adjusted version is the honest metric and the three together are what I should report.
- **Sweep the risk-aversion parameter rather than fixing it, because it decides whether the execution layer earns its place.** The finding that TWAP is nearly optimal at low $\lambda$ and that every method's advantage appears only at medium and high risk aversion is a reason to treat $\lambda$ as a reported axis rather than a hyperparameter to tune away. If a CSE execution component only beats TWAP at risk aversions higher than any plausible mandate, that is a negative result worth having early and cheaply, and it is a one-line addition to an experiment I would run anyway.
- **The recalibration idea generalises directly to my orchestrator, and I should design for it rather than discover the need later.** The structural condition is that the critic's parametrisation depends on the actor's parameters through a known map, which is exactly what happens if the orchestrator's value function is written in terms of the agent weights it emits. When that holds, resetting the critic in lockstep after every actor update costs one matrix product and, on this evidence, is the difference between converging and oscillating. The design implication is to keep the link between the two parametrisations explicit and invertible instead of treating actor and critic as independent black boxes, which is the same discipline as encoding known structure in the output layer from the child-order note.
- **Use the rolling-quarter train-and-test protocol as the evaluation backbone.** Train on one period, test on the next, roll forward, average over all test days is the right shape for a short archive, and it supports a distribution-shift claim rather than just a noise claim. The CSE version is harder in the useful direction, since the quarter-to-quarter shifts there are larger than between 2016Q4 and 2017Q1, so a method that survives the roll on CSE data has cleared a higher bar than this paper's does.
- **Report the spread across training runs, not a single fitted policy.** The sample-state variant is the only one I could build against recorded CSE data, and it is the one whose learned policy parameter varies by orders of magnitude between runs. The paper's own habit of reporting standard deviations over 100 independent trainings is the right response, and it is a discipline the other two execution notes let me get away with omitting.
- **The residual-RL suggestion in the discussion is the template for combining a defensible baseline with a learned component.** Treating the analytically derived policy as a baseline control and learning a residual on top of it with a flexible algorithm gives a floor that cannot be worse than the model-based solution by much, and a mechanism for the learner to exploit anything the model misses, such as depth or imbalance if the CSE ever exposes it. That is the same "beat the best simple thing" discipline as the baseline set in the queue-reactive note, applied to the architecture rather than to the evaluation, and it is a cleaner way to add a learned execution component to the orchestrator than starting from a blank policy network.
