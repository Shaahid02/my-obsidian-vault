South Asian Journal of Finance, Volume 6 Issue 01, 2026, pp. 76 to 92, Tharshiga, Paranika and Subramaniam, all at the Department of Financial Management, University of Jaffna. A univariate Box-Jenkins study asking whether an ARIMA model can forecast the CSE Price Index, fitted on 2,362 daily observations from 1 July 2014 to 30 June 2024 and validated on a one-month out-of-sample window in July 2024. DOI 10.4038/sajf.v6i1.142.

This is the most recent ARIMA-on-CSE paper I have and the only one covering a full decade through to mid-2024, so it sits directly on the baseline layer of my own evaluation, the univariate price-only model that every agent-based signal has to beat before it earns its place. That is what I came for and it is not really what the paper delivers. The headline, <font color="#ffc000">MAPE of 3.9% and therefore strong short-term forecasting performance</font>, comes from a forecast path that is a straight line, and the paper's own Table 4 contains everything needed to show that a no-change random walk would have beaten it on every single day of the test month. I read this as a useful specification record and an unusually clean example of the benchmark failure I keep finding in the local literature, alongside [[The Long-Term Impact of Macroeconomic Stability Variables on Stock Price Volatility]], rather than as evidence that ARIMA forecasts the CSE.

> [!WARNING]
> **Two claims in this paper are contradicted by its own tables.** The residuals are described as a realisation of a white noise process while Figure 3 reports Ljung-Box probabilities of 0.000 from lag 5 through lag 36, and the forecast is described as accurate while the tabulated predictions rise by 54 points across a month in which the index fell 670. Both are checkable from the printed output, and neither is acknowledged anywhere in the text.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **No decade-length ARIMA validation exists for the CSE.** The authors position this against Ranasinghe (2021), which compared ARIMA against neural networks on CSE and found ARIMA reliable in calm periods but beaten under heightened volatility, and against [[Sri Lankan Stock Market Volatility Analysis, An ARMA-GARCH Approach]], which added a GARCH variance equation rather than testing plain ARIMA on its own. The claim is that the standalone linear model has never been given a clean, long-window test here, which is fair.
- **The window is chosen to span regimes rather than avoid them.** July 2014 to June 2024 deliberately contains the 2019 Easter Sunday attacks, the COVID-19 lockdown low of 4,247.95, the speculative peak of 13,462.39 in January 2022, and the 2022 forex and sovereign crisis. This is the opposite choice to the macro ARDL paper's decision to stop at December 2019, and it is the single best thing about the design.
- **The efficiency question is the real target.** The paper frames ARIMA's predictive success as a test of weak-form efficiency, reading significant autoregressive structure as evidence of the partial, evolving inefficiency that Lo's (2004) Adaptive Market Hypothesis predicts, and that framing is what the hypothesis $H_1$ is actually about.

#### Data and setup

Daily closing values of the CSE Price Index from the official CSE database, cross-checked against Bloomberg historical series, 1 July 2014 to 30 June 2024, giving <font color="#ffc000">2,362 usable observations after differencing and lag adjustment</font>. Everything is run in EViews 13. There is no train and validation split inside the decade, the model is estimated on the whole sample and then forecast forward into July 2024.

Stationarity is tested with ADF under three specifications. At level the statistics are −0.980153 for intercept, −2.052616 for trend and intercept, and 0.528235 for none, all with probabilities above 0.05, and after first differencing they move to −13.33849, −13.37516 and −13.31616 with probabilities of 0.0000, so the index is clean I(1) and $d=1$ is settled. The differenced correlogram shows a PACF cut-off after lag 2 and a decaying ACF, which is read as AR(2) with MA(1).

#### Model specification and estimation

Eight candidate ARMA orders are compared on AIC, SBIC, adjusted $R^2$, $\sigma^2$ and standard error, and the grid is worth looking at closely because the selection it supports is much weaker than the paper presents.

![[Pasted image 20260925170001.png]]

ARMA(2,1) wins at AIC 11.64805 against ARMA(1,1) at 11.64806, a difference of 0.00001 in EViews' per-observation units, which on 2,362 observations is <font color="#ffc000">0.024 absolute AIC units</font>, far inside the range where the two models are indistinguishable. ARMA(0,1) at 11.64854 and ARMA(1,2) at 11.64864 are barely further away. Then the last three rows break the pattern entirely, with (2,2), (0,2) and (2,0) all landing near 11.7338, roughly 203 absolute AIC units worse, and adjusted $R^2$ collapsing from 0.0825 to approximately zero. A one-parameter change cannot move a likelihood that far on this sample, so those three rows look like estimations that did not converge or were run on a different transformation, and either way the grid is not the clean comparison it is presented as.

The estimated model is also not the model the paper names. Table 3 reports C, AR(2), MA(1) and SIGMASQ with no AR(1) term anywhere, so what was actually fitted is a subset specification with $\phi_1$ restricted to zero:

$$\Delta y_t = \mu + \phi_2\left(\Delta y_{t-2} - \mu\right) + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1}$$

where:
- $\Delta y_t$ is the first difference of the CSE Price Index
- $\mu = 2.445160$ is the unconditional mean of the differenced series, with $t = 1.072697$ and $p = 0.2835$
- $\phi_2 = -0.038071$ with $t = -4.178964$, and there is no $\phi_1$
- $\theta_1 = 0.301976$ with $t = 37.81713$

The printed inverted AR roots of $-0.00 \pm 0.20i$ confirm this, since $\sqrt{0.038071} = 0.195$ is exactly what the roots of $1 - \phi_2 L^2$ give when $\phi_1 = 0$, and a genuine ARIMA(2,1,1) would not produce a purely imaginary conjugate pair. It is a defensible model, gap ARMA specifications are a normal thing to fit, but it is called ARIMA(2,1,1) in the title, the abstract, every table caption and the conclusion, and anyone reimplementing from the paper builds the wrong thing.

![[Pasted image 20260925170002.png]]

Fit is thin and the authors are upfront about it, $R^2 = 0.083698$ falling to an adjusted 0.082532, read as roughly 8% of short-term variation in the index explained by its own past. SIGMASQ of 6679.119 puts the residual standard deviation at about 81.7 index points per day, which against a level moving between 4,248 and 13,462 is a daily sigma of somewhere between 0.6% and 1.9% depending on where in the decade you stand, and that spread is itself the problem with estimating a single constant variance across this window.

#### Findings

- **The autoregressive structure is real and economically tiny.** AR(2) at −0.038071 with $p = 0.0000$ is a genuinely significant negative dependence, the kind of short-horizon reversal you would expect in a thin market, but the coefficient means a 100 point move two days ago maps to a 3.8 point offset today against a daily residual sigma of 81.7. Statistical significance at $n = 2{,}362$ is close to free, and the paper reads this term as supporting exploitable inefficiency without ever putting the magnitude next to the noise.
- **MA(1) at 0.301976 carries almost all of the fit, and its standard error of 0.007985 is implausibly small.** A $t$ of 37.8 on a moving average term is not something I have seen on a daily equity series, and combined with SIGMASQ being estimated as a free parameter it suggests the maximum likelihood step is doing something other than what the write-up describes. I am flagging this as unresolved rather than diagnosed, since the paper prints no likelihood profile or convergence detail to check it against.
- **The out-of-sample forecast is a straight line, and the line is the insignificant constant.** Under dynamic forecasting the AR and MA terms have no observed innovations to work with beyond the first few steps, so the expected increment converges to the unconditional mean and the forecast collapses to:

  $$\hat{y}_{T+h} = y_T + \sum_{i=1}^{h}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta y_{T+i}\mid\mathcal{F}_T\right] \;\longrightarrow\; y_T + \mu h$$

  The tabulated predictions confirm this exactly, the day-on-day increments run 3.240, 2.337, 2.415, 2.449, 2.446 and then settle at <font color="#ffc000">2.44516 for every remaining day</font>, which is $\mu = 2.445160$ to five decimals. So the entire three-month forecast path is generated by a drift term the paper's own Table 3 reports as insignificant at $p = 0.2835$, and the AR and MA structure that the efficiency argument rests on contributes nothing beyond the first week.
- **On the test month the model drifts up while the market falls.** Predictions rise 54.45 points across the 23 trading days while the index fell 669.93 points, from 12,076.49 to 11,406.56, a decline of 5.55%. The relative errors the paper reports, widening monotonically from −0.50% to −7.22%, are described as the expected decay of accuracy with horizon, and that reading is only available because the sign of the miss is never discussed.

  ![[Pasted image 20260925170003.png]]

- **A no-change forecast beats it on every day of the test window.** Taking the naive random walk anchored at the last observed level, my recomputation over the 23 tabulated days gives a mean absolute percentage error of <font color="#ffc000">2.57% for the random walk against 3.62% for the ARIMA</font>, RMSE of 346.10 against 465.40, and the random walk is closer to the actual on 23 of 23 days. My 3.62% is close enough to the paper's reported 3.9% that the two are almost certainly measuring the same thing, and the number is never actually derived anywhere in the text. On this evidence the paper's central accuracy claim survives only because no benchmark was computed.

#### Limitations

- **The white noise conclusion is contradicted by the printed correlogram.** The text states that the Ljung-Box Q statistics are insignificant at the early lags and that the residuals are a realisation of a white noise process, and Figure 3 reports $p = 0.039$ at lag 3, $p = 0.001$ at lag 4, and $p = 0.000$ continuously from lag 5 to lag 36, ending at $Q(36) = 185.20$. Residual autocorrelations of 0.073, 0.080, 0.063 and 0.063 at lags 6 through 9 and 0.106 at lag 24 are visible structure the model did not capture. <font color="#ff0000">The Box-Jenkins adequacy criterion is failed and reported as passed</font>, which means the model was never actually validated and the significance of the AR and MA terms cannot be taken at face value either, since their standard errors assume the very independence the correlogram rejects.

  ![[Pasted image 20260925170004.png]]

- **A constant variance model is fitted to a decade with obvious volatility clustering, and the paper's own residual plot shows it.** Figure 5 has residuals hugging zero from 2014 to 2019 and then swinging through bands of several hundred points across 2020 to 2022, which is exactly the ARCH signature. The methodology section says heteroskedasticity was examined, no test appears anywhere, and Durbin-Watson at 2.001405 is presented as the serial correlation evidence despite being the one statistic that is biased toward 2 in a specification containing moving average errors. The irony is that the fix is cited in the paper's own literature review, since Samarawickrama and Pallegedara's ARMA-GARCH work on the ASPI is discussed approvingly two pages earlier.
- **One forecast origin, no benchmark, no rolling evaluation.** The accuracy claim rests on a single 23-day window starting 1 July 2024, with no persistence baseline, no buy-and-hold comparison, no rolling re-estimation, and no repetition of the exercise at other origins. [[Sri Lankan Stock Market Volatility Analysis, An ARMA-GARCH Approach]] runs a 45-day rolling window on the same market and finds the in-sample and out-of-sample rankings disagree, so the local literature already contains the demonstration that a single in-sample selection does not determine forecast performance.
- **Significant autocorrelation is upgraded into exploitable inefficiency without a strategy, a cost model, or a trade.** The conclusion tells investors they can use ARIMA to design a short-term trading strategy, and nothing in the paper tests that, no positions are taken, no transaction costs are applied, and no return is computed. On a market where [[Role of Market Liquidity in Sentiment-Based Return Predictions, Evidence from Sri Lanka]] finds unexpected illiquidity shocks depressing returns by 8 to 10 basis points, a 3.8 point conditional offset on an 81.7 point daily sigma is well inside the friction, so the weak-form inefficiency claim is about statistical detectability and the paper presents it as being about profit.
- **Two of the literature review's supporting citations do not say what they are cited for.** The claim that ARIMA-MLP and ARIMA-RNN hybrids outperform plain ARIMA in volatile financial environments is attributed to Zhang, Wang and Chen (2022), and the matching reference is Chen et al. (2022) on forecasting tuberculosis incidence in Anhui during COVID-19. The claim that Ahammad, Rahman and Chowdhury (2024) compared ARIMA and LSTM across South Asia maps to a reference on financial indicators shaping share price movement on the Dhaka SME Board, which is not a forecasting comparison. Section V also opens by announcing that the study examined the impact of dividend, which it did not. None of this changes the empirics, it just means the review section cannot be used as a bibliography without checking each entry.
- **Univariate and index-level throughout, which the authors acknowledge.** No macro variables, no sentiment, no volume, no turnover, no spreads, no individual counters, no structural break treatment across a decade that contains at least three. The stated future direction is ARIMA-GARCH or ARIMA-LSTM hybrids, which is the right direction and is also where the local literature already is.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **This is my benchmark-discipline citation, and the cleanest one I have.** A published 2026 journal paper reports strong short-term forecasting performance from a forecast that loses to a no-change baseline on 23 of 23 days, and the entire gap between those two readings is one line of arithmetic the paper never ran. My results tables carry persistence and buy-and-hold as fixed columns for exactly this reason, and when I justify that in the proposal this is the example I point at rather than arguing the principle in the abstract.
- **I report a scaled error, not a raw one, and I state the estimator up front.** MAPE on a price level is nearly uninformative because the denominator barely moves across a month, so the metric that decides anything is the ratio against the naive forecast:

  $$U_2=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{h=1}^{H}\left(y_{T+h}-\hat{y}_{T+h}\right)^2}{\sum_{h=1}^{H}\left(y_{T+h}-y_{T}\right)^2}}$$

  where the denominator is the random walk's squared error from the forecast origin, so $U_2 < 1$ means the model beat no-change and $U_2 \geq 1$ means it did not. On this paper's Table 4 the value is <font color="#ffc000">1.345</font>. Every forecasting number my system reports gets this alongside it, computed per counter, and the same quantity is what decides whether the price-derived agent gets any weight at all on a given day.
- **The drift-collapse is a design constraint on the technical agent, not just a criticism of this paper.** Any multi-step forecast from a low-order ARIMA degenerates to a drift line within a handful of steps, so a price-only agent has real information at a one to three day horizon and essentially none beyond it. That sets the rebalancing frequency my orchestrator can meaningfully condition price signals at, and it means multi-day forecast horizons from the technical agent should be produced one step at a time against realised data rather than dynamically.
- **The ARIMA baseline I build gets AR(1) included and the residuals actually tested.** I can reuse the specification, daily CSE index, $d=1$ confirmed by ADF, low-order ARMA on the differenced series, but with the full $(2,1,1)$ rather than the subset the paper fitted, with lag selection reported against a criterion I state, and with a Ljung-Box pass as a hard gate rather than a sentence. Given how thin the AIC separation is here, between 11.64805 and 11.64806, I select on out-of-sample scaled error across rolling origins and treat the information criteria as a tie-breaker only.
- **The residual plot in this paper is my third independent CSE reading of volatility clustering, and it strengthens the case for the persistence layer.** Fitted with constant variance and still showing residual bands widening by a factor of several across 2020 to 2022, it lines up with the near-unit persistence in the ARMA-GARCH note and the fractional integration in [[Modeling Long-Term Volatility Memory Dynamics in the Colombo Stock Exchange]]. Three papers, three different specifications, same signal, so conditioning exposure on forecast variance and decay speed rather than on a fixed risk parameter is supported by the local evidence rather than imported from elsewhere.
- **The regime coverage is the part I take directly.** July 2014 to June 2024 spanning the Easter attacks, the COVID low at 4,247.95, the January 2022 peak at 13,462.39 and the 2022 crisis is close to the window my own evaluation needs, and having a published paper establish that this decade contains multiple distinct regimes saves me arguing for the period from first principles. Where I differ is that I treat those regimes as something to report results across separately, since a single pooled accuracy figure over a decade this heterogeneous is not a number anyone can act on.
- **The 8% in-sample $R^2$ is a useful floor to state honestly.** Roughly 8% of daily variation explained by price history alone is what the linear univariate channel is worth on this market, which is small and is not zero, and it is the number I quote when I argue that the price-derived agent should be one conditioned input among several rather than the backbone. It also sets a sane expectation for my own baseline, since a technical agent reporting far more than this on CSE daily data is more likely to have a leakage problem than a discovery.
- **The efficiency framing is worth borrowing and the inference is not.** Partial, time-varying weak-form inefficiency under the Adaptive Market Hypothesis is the right theoretical home for a system whose whole premise is that signal reliability moves with market state, and this paper states that case well. What I do not repeat is the step from a significant coefficient to an exploitable one, so any claim I make about predictability gets a net-of-cost return attached to it, using the liquidity-aware slippage model rather than a flat fee.
