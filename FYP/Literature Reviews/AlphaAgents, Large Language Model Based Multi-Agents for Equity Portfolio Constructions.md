arXiv preprint (2508.11152v1), August 2025, Zhao, Lyu, Jones, Garber, Pasquali and Mehta, all six authors out of BlackRock. A role-based multi-agent LLM system for equity research and stock screening, built from three specialist agents, fundamental, sentiment and valuation, that each analyse a stock from their own data and toolset and then argue it out in a structured debate until they converge on a single BUY or SELL call. The two things it actually contributes are the debate mechanism as a hallucination check and the treatment of investor risk tolerance as a prompt-level design variable rather than a post-hoc filter.

This sits on the orchestration side of my reading, alongside [[FinCon, A Synthesized LLM Multi-Agent System with Conceptual Verbal Reinforcement for Enhanced Financial Decision Making]] and [[TradingAgents, Multi-Agents LLM Financial Trading Framework]], and the useful thing is that it takes the opposite position to FinCon on the single most important architectural question in this space. FinCon routes everything through a manager and forbids lateral chatter on the grounds that peer-to-peer discussion is an unpriced cost. AlphaAgents makes debate the whole point, on the grounds that disagreement between agents is the only signal you get that one of them is hallucinating. Both papers are arguing from intuition rather than from a controlled comparison, neither runs the ablation that would settle it, so what I have is two credible positions and an open question, which is worth writing down as an open question rather than picking a side on vibes.

The other reason to read this one closely is that it is deliberately modular. It does not produce portfolio weights at all, it produces a screened list, and the conclusion is explicit that the intended use is as an input to Mean-Variance Optimization or Black-Litterman rather than as a replacement for them. That is a cleaner separation of concerns than most of the multi-agent literature manages, and it is roughly the shape I want, LLM agents generating a signal, something auditable turning that signal into positions.

## Major differences to mine

No social media or code-mixed sentiment path anywhere. The sentiment agent reads Bloomberg news bodies, analyst ratings and disclosures, all English, all institutional, all wire-quality. CSE sentiment does not live there, it lives in Sinhala and Singlish retail chatter and in a handful of local outlets, which is the problem [[Enhancing Multilingual Sentiment Analysis with Explainability for Sinhala, English, and Code-Mixed Content]] is built around. Dropping a Bloomberg-shaped sentiment agent onto the CSE gets me an agent with nothing to read.

The <font color="#ff0000">information density assumption</font> is the hard blocker, and this paper hands me the evidence for it directly rather than making me infer it. The sentiment agent was excluded from the single-agent portfolio comparison entirely because there was insufficient news coverage for some of the fifteen stocks. Fifteen US technology large caps, and news coverage was still too thin to evaluate that agent. That is the exact failure mode I should expect on the CSE, at far greater severity, and it is worth quoting in the proposal because it comes from the paper's own experiment rather than from my speculation.

No risk calculating agent. Risk enters this system only as a prompt adjective, the agents are told they are advising a risk-averse or risk-neutral investor and they reason about volatility qualitatively from there. There is no risk model, no conditional volatility estimate, no drawdown constraint, nothing that would survive contact with the volatility persistence [[Modeling Long-Term Volatility Memory Dynamics in the Colombo Stock Exchange]] measures on the CSE. FinCon at least computes CVaR on realised PnL, which is backward looking but is at least a number. AlphaAgents has the valuation agent compute annualised volatility with a calculator tool and then leaves the interpretation of that number to GPT-4o.

No transaction costs, no liquidity constraint, no turnover penalty. Equal weights across whatever the agents select, held for four months, rebalancing not modelled. On a whale-dominated thin market that omission does a lot of unearned work, and it is the same gap [[Deep Reinforcement Learning Framework for Diversified Dynamic Portfolio Allocation Across Global Equity Markets]] closes by putting turnover directly inside the reward.

Fundamental data arrives through 10-K and 10-Q filings[^1] pulled from a US disclosure regime with a fixed schedule, fixed sections and machine-readable structure. CSE annual reports are PDFs of varying structure with no equivalent of the MD&A section boundary, which is precisely the chunking problem [[Financial Report Chunking for Effective Retrieval Augmented Generation]] is about, so the Report RAG tool here is not portable as-is even though the idea behind it is.

## Standout features

<font color="#ffff00">Reflection-enhanced prompting</font> in the sentiment agent, where the model summarises, critiques its own summary, then refines it, rather than extracting directly.

Multi-agent debate as an explicit hallucination control, with round-robin turn taking and a consensus stopping condition rather than a fixed number of rounds.

<font color="#ffc000">Risk tolerance embedded in the agent instructions</font> rather than applied as a filter afterwards, so the same agent genuinely reasons differently about the same stock under different investor profiles.

An <font color="#ffc000">observability platform (Arize Phoenix)</font> used as the evaluation layer for the RAG-driven agents, with faithfulness and relevance scored per retrieval and tool invocation monitored to check the valuation agent is actually calling its calculator rather than doing arithmetic in-context.

Full discussion transcripts logged and human-reviewable, with the ability to override the agents' conclusion, which is the explainability property I want and which most of this literature only gestures at.

Mathematical tools handed to the agent specifically so the LLM does not have to do the arithmetic itself, an anti-hallucination measure that costs almost nothing to implement.

#### Gap it's addressing

- Agent-based systems in finance have predominantly been reinforcement learning based, and those are task-specific and operate on structured data rather than being driven by language. The survey view of that literature is in [[The Evolution of Reinforcement Learning in Quantitative Finance, A Survey]], and the paper's framing is that RL agents cannot ingest the unstructured disclosure and news material that human analysts spend most of their time on.
- Prior LLM finance work emphasises chain-of-thought prompting but neglects <font color="#ffc000">structured agent interaction strategies</font>. FinRobot, FinMem, MarketSenseAI, FinAgent and FinVerse are all named as prior art, and the claim is that none of them treat the interaction protocol between agents as a design object in its own right. Against the systems I have already reviewed this is partly fair and partly not, [[FinVision, A Multi-Agent Framework for Stock Market Prediction]] and FinCon both have deliberate interaction structures, so the gap is narrower than stated, but the specific combination of cooperative and adversarial reasoning under a consensus stopping rule is genuinely underexplored.
- Agent-specific risk tolerance has received little attention. This is the more defensible half of the gap claim. Risk preference in the LLM agent literature is usually either absent or bolted on at the portfolio construction stage, and the idea of conditioning the analysis itself on an investor profile, so that the same evidence produces a SELL for one client and a BUY for another, has not really been tested.
- Behavioural bias mitigation is the framing motivation running underneath all of this. Rooted in Kahneman and Tversky's Prospect Theory, the argument is that loss aversion and overconfidence lead to suboptimal decisions, that existing practical remedies like awareness checklists and frontier anchoring fail because these biases are unconscious, and that delegating the analysis to agents that do not have the biases is a structurally different fix. I would treat this as motivation rather than as a tested claim, because nothing in the experiment measures bias.

> [!NOTE] Role Prompting
> Role prompting is a prompt engineering technique that involves guiding a conversational agent by assigning it a specific role or context, so that generated responses are more relevant and contextually appropriate to the task.

#### Data and setup

Each agent is granted access only to the data relevant to its role and assigned task, which is the information-partitioning idea FinCon also uses, and the partition is worth having as a grid because it is effectively the system's interface specification.

| Feature | Availability | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ticker | All agents | Unique symbol of a publicly traded company's stock |
| Price (OHLC) | Valuation agent | Initial, highest, lowest and final trading price within a specific trading period |
| Volume | Valuation agent | Total number of shares traded during a specific period |
| 10-K / 10-Q | Fundamental agent | Financial disclosure data, including major company prospectus and financial statements |
| Bloomberg ID | Sentiment agent | Ticker's corresponding unique identifier for Bloomberg News |
| News body | Sentiment agent | Main textual content of a news article detailing news, company disclosure and analyst rating changes |

Sources are 10-K and 10-Q filings, Bloomberg financial news, and historical price and volume from Yahoo Finance. The thing to notice scanning down the availability column is that no agent sees more than two features, and no two agents share anything beyond the ticker, so there is no redundancy in the system at all, each signal has exactly one agent responsible for it and no second opinion on it. That is what makes the sentiment agent's news-coverage failure fatal rather than degraded, there is nothing behind it.

The experiment itself is small and the paper does not hide that. Fifteen randomly selected technology sector stocks, serving as both the stock-picking pool and the benchmark, so the benchmark is an equal-weight portfolio of the same fifteen names the agents are choosing from. All selections equal-weighted, with weighting by model confidence flagged as future work. Agents were run on data and news from January 2024, portfolio selection on 1 February 2024, performance monitored over the subsequent four months. GPT-4o as the backbone for every agent after experimenting with various GPT models, no open-weight comparison.

#### System architecture and agent tooling

Three specialist agents, each emulating a role from a human portfolio management team.

1. **Fundamental Agent** qualitatively and quantitatively analyses an equity's projected trajectory and financial performance from 10-K and 10-Q reports, sector and company trends and financial statement data.
2. **Sentiment Agent** analyses financial news, analyst ratings and disclosures, and reports the prevailing sentiment toward the equity and its likely impact on the stock price.
3. **Valuation Agent** analyses prices and volumes and produces a valuation assessment informing the stock's relative significance within a portfolio.

![[Pasted image 20260809205115.png]]

The framework is explicitly designed for extension, and the paper names a Technical Analysis Agent for short-term trend detection and a Macro Economist Agent for policy and macro data as the obvious next additions, neither implemented.

Role prompts are short and are given verbatim in the paper, which is worth keeping in full because the phrasing is doing real work, particularly the last line of the fundamental agent's prompt.

|                   |                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Valuation Agent   | As a valuation equity analyst, your primary responsibility is to analyze the valuation trends of a given asset or portfolio over an extended time horizon. To complete the task, you must analyze the historical valuation data of the asset or portfolio provided, identify trends and patterns in valuation metrics over time, and interpret the implications of these trends for investors or stakeholders.                                                                                                                                                                             |
| Sentiment Agent   | As a sentiment equity analyst your primary responsibility is to analyze the financial news, analyst ratings and disclosures related to the underlying security; and analyze its implication and sentiment for investors or stakeholders.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| Fundamental Agent | As a fundamental financial equity analyst your primary responsibility is to analyze the most recent 10K report provided for a company. You have access to a powerful tool that can help you extract relevant information from the 10K. Your analysis should be based solely on the information that you retrieve using this tool. You can interact with this tool using natural language queries. The tool will understand your requests and return relevant text snippets and data points from the 10K document. Keep checking if you have answered the users' question to avoid looping. |

"Your analysis should be based solely on the information that you retrieve using this tool" is a grounding constraint and "keep checking if you have answered the users' question to avoid looping" is a termination heuristic, both patched in at the prompt level because the agent loop has no mechanism for either. They work well enough for a demonstration, but a prompt line is not a guarantee, and there is no reported measurement of how often either instruction is violated.

**Valuation agent tooling.** A computational tool calculates return and volatility so the LLM does not have to. Annualised cumulative return, assuming 252 trading days, is

$$R_{\text{annualized}} = \left(1 + R_{\text{cumulative}}\right)^{\frac{252}{n}} - 1$$

where $R_{\text{cumulative}}$ is the cumulative return over the period and $n$ is the number of trading days, and annualised volatility scales daily volatility by the usual square-root-of-time factor,

$$\sigma_{\text{annualized}} = \sigma_{\text{daily}} \times \sqrt{252}$$

where $\sigma_{\text{daily}}$ is the daily volatility. Neither formula is interesting in itself. What is interesting is the design decision they represent, that every number the agent reasons over is computed outside the model and passed in, so a hallucinated figure has to come from the tool rather than from the LLM's arithmetic, and Phoenix is then used to verify the tool was actually invoked. That is a cheap and directly reusable pattern.

**Sentiment agent tooling.** An LLM-based summarisation tool condenses each news item before an overarching sentiment judgement is made, using <font color="#ffff00">reflection-enhanced prompting</font>, with the guiding prompt "provide a concise summary along with an informed recommendation on whether to invest in this stock". The paper draws the distinction with RAG deliberately, a summarisation tool lets the agent read all the data and form an opinion, where retrieval only surfaces fragments matching a query, so the choice between them is a choice about whether the agent needs coverage or precision. For sentiment they argue coverage matters more, which I think is right when the news volume is small enough to fit, and is a different tradeoff from the one [[Optimizing LLM Based Retrieval Augmented Generation Pipelines in the Financial Domain]] is optimising.

**Fundamental agent tooling.** Two tools. The Report Pull Tool generates yfinance API calls for the specific stock, with iterative call checks to confirm the call is correct and the data actually came back. The Report RAG Tool chunks by report section to keep contextually coherent units, uses GPT-4o as the embedding model, and carries a domain-expertise instruction telling the agent which section to look in for what. It is invoked several times per stock against a fixed question set covering cash flow and income, operations and gross margin, areas of concern, and progress towards stated objectives. Section-boundary chunking is the same conclusion [[Financial Report Chunking for Effective Retrieval Augmented Generation]] reaches from the retrieval side, so two independent papers landing on element-level structure rather than fixed-size windows is a reasonably strong prior for my own pipeline.

**Orchestration.** <font color="#ffc000">Microsoft AutoGen</font> provides the group chat and assistant agent infrastructure, with AutoGen Studio as the interface. A group chat assistant coordinates, and the collaboration prompt is "You are a helpful assistant skilled at coordinating a group of other agents to solve a task. You make sure that every agent in the group chat has a chance to speak at least twice. When all agents provide their analysis, consolidate inputs of all agent into a report. Reply TERMINATE at the end when everything is done."

> [!NOTE] Multi-Agent Debate
> Debate is managed via a round robin approach. Each agent receives the query along with peer analyses, and the discussion continues until consensus is reached. Each agent gets at least two turns. The coordinator prompt adds the binding constraint, "Each agent can not decide for the whole group. They are tasked with coming to a consensus. You must invoke all agents before deciding to Terminate."

> [!WARNING] Consensus is not correctness
> The stopping rule is agreement, not accuracy. Three agents running on the same backbone model can converge on a shared wrong answer, and nothing in this design detects that case, it looks identical to a successful debate from the outside. The hallucination-reduction claim rests on disagreement being informative, which holds when the agents' errors are independent, and GPT-4o driving all three is exactly the condition under which they are not. The paper does not test this.

#### Risk tolerance as a design variable

Risk profiles are embedded directly into the agent instructions rather than applied as a screen afterwards, which the paper motivates as mimicking how human clients express preferences, contextually rather than through fixed numerical thresholds. The worked illustration is a valuation agent issuing a SELL on a company under a risk-averse prompt, citing high volatility despite recent outperformance, and a BUY on the same company under a risk-neutral prompt, emphasising momentum with caution. Same agent, same data, opposite call.

The part I find more useful than the headline is the negative result buried next to it. A risk-seeking profile was also tested and its outputs were <font color="#ffc000">nearly indistinguishable from the risk-neutral case</font>, so it was excluded from the final evaluation. The authors read this as a limitation of prompt-based differentiation for adjacent risk profiles, and I think that reading is correct and under-explored. Prompt conditioning appears to give you coarse control, roughly averse versus not-averse, and not a graded dial, which matters for anyone planning to use natural-language risk profiles as a continuous parameter. It is one observation on one setup and it is not measured formally, so I would treat it as a flag to test rather than an established result.

#### Evaluation

Three separate layers, which is more evaluation infrastructure than most papers in this space bother with even though the final numbers are thin.

For the fundamental and sentiment agents, whose work is mostly RAG and summarisation, [Arize Phoenix](https://github.com/arize-ai/phoenix) supplies retrieval and relevance evaluation with faithfulness and relevance scores per retrieval. The valuation agent is harder, the paper says plainly there is no easy ground truth for numerical analytics, so the mitigation is the calculator tool plus Phoenix monitoring to confirm the agent used it rather than answering from context. Debates are reviewed by humans for logical coherence and soundness, with all discussion histories logged so a reviewer can inspect and override.

Back-testing is then used as the downstream metric. Portfolios are built for the valuation agent, the fundamental agent and the multi-agent system, plus the equal-weight benchmark, all initialised 1 February 2024 and monitored for four months. The sentiment agent gets no standalone portfolio because of insufficient news coverage. Risk-adjusted return uses the Sharpe ratio, $S = (R_p - R_f)/\sigma_p$, with the one-month treasury as the risk-free rate, and performance is tracked dynamically with a rolling Sharpe ratio over a window $w$,

$$S_{\text{rolling}}(t) = \frac{\overline{R_{p,t-w+1:t}} - R_f}{\sigma_{p,t-w+1:t}}$$

where the mean portfolio return over the window is

$$\overline{R_{p,t-w+1:t}} = \frac{1}{w}\sum_{i=t-w+1}^{t} R_{p,i}$$

and the corresponding standard deviation is

$$\sigma_{p,t-w+1:t} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{w-1}\sum_{i=t-w+1}^{t}\left(R_{p,i} - \overline{R_{p,t-w+1:t}}\right)^2}$$

The rolling form is the right instrument for a window this short, since a single Sharpe over four months tells you almost nothing about stability, but the window length $w$ is never stated, which makes the curves hard to interpret and impossible to reproduce.

#### Findings

- **In the risk-neutral setting the multi-agent portfolio beats both single-agent portfolios and the benchmark** on cumulative return and rolling Sharpe over the four-month window. The paper's explanation is a horizon argument, the sentiment and valuation agents work off a one to three month historical window and are better at short-term forecasting, the fundamental agent works off 10-K material and captures long-term potential, and combining them balances short and long-term signal in a way no single agent does. That mechanism is plausible and it is consistent with the selection behaviour described below, but it is asserted rather than isolated, there is no configuration in the experiment that varies horizon while holding everything else fixed.
- **The absolute numbers are worse than the word outperforms suggests, and the figures are where you see it.** In Figure 6 the cumulative return series spend most of the window below zero, bottoming near -0.10, and the rolling Sharpe series are negative across the entire window, roughly -0.10 down to -0.45. So what the risk-neutral result demonstrates is smaller losses than the benchmark over these four months, not positive risk-adjusted return. That is a legitimate finding and relative performance is the right comparison, but reading "the multi-agent portfolio outperforms" without looking at the axis would give entirely the wrong impression of the system's absolute performance, and the paper never states a single Sharpe or return figure in the text.
- **The agents' selection behaviour differs in an interpretable way.** Under a risk-neutral profile the valuation agent reproduces the benchmark portfolio directly, which implies it is not differentiating its picks from the market baseline at all, the fundamental agent expands beyond the benchmark to a broader set suggesting it is weighting diversification and growth signals, and the multi-agent system keeps most of the fundamental agent's picks but curates them down, which the paper reads as coordination refining and consolidating rather than adding names. The valuation agent collapsing onto the benchmark is the most diagnostic single observation here, an agent whose output is the benchmark contributes nothing on its own, and it means the risk-neutral comparison against that agent is really a second comparison against the benchmark.
- **Under a risk-averse profile every agent-selected portfolio underperformed the benchmark.** The stated cause is compositional, the technology sector rallied over the test period, tech stocks are typically more volatile, so the risk-averse agents largely excluded them and gave up the rally. The paper frames this as the classic risk-return tradeoff, in a predominantly bullish market higher risk tolerance wins, and I think that is right as far as it goes but it also means the risk-averse arm of this experiment is testing the sector regime more than it is testing the agents.
- **Within the risk-averse setting the multi-agent portfolio still did relatively better than the valuation and fundamental portfolios**, particularly in the early part of the window, with slightly lower volatility and reduced drawdowns. On this evidence the integration of two conservative strategies balanced risk and return better than either alone, which supports the framework's intended behaviour, though the paper is careful to call the analysis limited in scope and I would hold the claim at that level.
- **Risk-neutral beat risk-averse consistently across all three agent types** in the direct comparison, which is an obvious artifact of a bullish test window rather than a finding about risk profiles, and the paper says so. The more interesting read of the same figure is that the multi-agent portfolio sits between the fundamental and valuation agents and narrows the gap between them, so the debate appears to be doing something like averaging out the risk-tolerance extremes of the individual agents.
- **Structured collaboration is separable from debate in the pipeline but not in the evidence.** The system runs collaboration first to produce the stock analysis report, then debate to produce the recommendation, and both are always on. There is no configuration with collaboration and no debate, so the paper cannot attribute any of the performance specifically to the debate mechanism, which is unfortunate given that debate is the headline contribution.

#### Limitations

- **No numerical results are reported anywhere.** The entire results section is figures, with no table of returns, Sharpe ratios or drawdowns, and no point estimate quoted in the text. That makes every comparison here eyeballed off a chart, makes the work unreproducible in any strict sense, and is a much weaker evidentiary standard than the papers around it, FinCon at least tabulates CR, SR and MDD per configuration even where its own caption overclaims against its numbers.
- **The sample is a single sector, fifteen stocks, one four-month window, one selection date.** No repeated splits, no walk-forward, no significance testing of any kind, and the benchmark is an equal-weight portfolio of the same fifteen candidates. Against the sixteen out-of-sample folds spanning 2003 to 2026 in [[Deep Reinforcement Learning Framework for Diversified Dynamic Portfolio Allocation Across Global Equity Markets]] this is a demonstration rather than an evaluation, and I should cite it as a design reference rather than as performance evidence.
- **Look-ahead risk from the backbone model is not addressed.** GPT-4o's pretraining data plausibly includes material from the February to June 2024 test window, so the agents may have prior knowledge of how these fifteen widely covered technology stocks performed over exactly the period they are being evaluated on. The paper does not raise this or attempt any mitigation such as an older-cutoff model or a pre-cutoff test window. I cannot quantify the effect and it may be small, but with a four-month window on mega-cap tech it is not a risk that can be waved away, and it is the first thing I would expect a reviewer to ask about.
- **No transaction costs, no slippage, no liquidity modelling, no rebalancing policy.** Equal weights assigned at selection, held, and the paper flags confidence-weighted allocation as future work rather than something tested.
- **Homogeneous backbone across all agents.** Every agent is GPT-4o, so there is no evidence separating the contribution of the architecture from the contribution of the model, and, more seriously for the central claim, correlated errors across agents are the exact condition under which debate stops functioning as a hallucination check.
- **Consensus is enforced by the coordinator prompt rather than emerging from the evidence.** The agents are instructed that they must reach consensus and must all be invoked before termination, so the system cannot report persistent disagreement as an outcome, which is arguably the most informative thing a debate could produce. A stock the agents genuinely cannot agree on is indistinguishable in the output from one they agree on easily.
- **Prompt-based risk differentiation breaks down between adjacent profiles**, per the risk-seeking result, and the boundary is not characterised beyond that one observation.
- **No portfolio optimization.** The authors state this plainly in the conclusion, this is stock selection only, weights and sizing are explicitly out of scope. That is an honest scoping decision rather than a flaw, but it does mean the back-test is evaluating a screen dressed as a portfolio.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **The sentiment agent's exclusion is the single most transferable finding for the CSE, and it is evidence, not conjecture.** If fifteen US technology large caps do not carry enough news for a sentiment agent to be evaluated on, then a CSE sentiment agent reading only formal news will be empty for most of the board. That points the design one of two ways, either sentiment comes from sources this paper does not consider, Sinhala and code-mixed retail discussion per [[Enhancing Multilingual Sentiment Analysis with Explainability for Sinhala, English, and Code-Mixed Content]] and the liquidity-conditioned effect in [[Role of Market Liquidity in Sentiment-Based Return Predictions, Evidence from Sri Lanka]], or the sentiment agent needs an explicit abstain path so it reports no signal rather than generating confident text about nothing. The second is the cheaper fix and I want it in the design regardless.
- **Adopt the no-redundancy warning as a design constraint rather than a feature.** Strict data partitioning across agents is clean and it keeps each agent's task narrow, but this paper shows what it costs, a single agent starving takes its whole signal channel with it because nothing else can see that data. On the CSE, where at least one channel will be thin for most tickers, I want either deliberate overlap between agents or an explicit confidence-and-abstain protocol so the aggregator knows which inputs are actually live for a given stock.
- **Take the calculator-tool pattern directly, it is the cheapest reliability win in the paper.** Every quantitative figure is computed outside the LLM and passed in, and the observability layer verifies the tool was called. That is trivially portable, it is what makes the valuation agent auditable despite having no ground truth to evaluate against, and it is where a CSE-specific risk measure enters cleanly, a FIGARCH-derived conditional volatility fed in as a computed input to the agent rather than left to the model to reason about qualitatively. That also fixes the missing risk agent, addresses the gap [[Modeling Long-Term Volatility Memory Dynamics in the Colombo Stock Exchange]] opens, and keeps the risk number itself out of the language model.
- **Build the observability layer in from the start, not after.** Phoenix scoring faithfulness and relevance per retrieval and monitoring tool invocation gives me a per-component evaluation story independent of the back-test, which matters a great deal on the CSE where the back-test will be short, noisy and underpowered by construction. If my portfolio results end up statistically inconclusive, and on CSE data depth they plausibly will, component-level retrieval metrics are the evidence that the system works even when the returns cannot prove it.
- **Keep debate, but make disagreement a reportable outcome.** The consensus-forced stopping rule throws away the most useful thing the mechanism produces. I want the protocol to allow a persistent-disagreement terminal state, logged with the dissenting arguments, because on a thin market a stock the agents cannot agree on is a stock I want flagged rather than resolved by whichever agent is most fluent. That also gives me a cheap proxy for decision confidence, which is the thing AlphaAgents lists as future work for weighting allocations.
- **Do not run every agent on the same backbone if debate is load-bearing.** The hallucination-reduction argument needs agent errors to be at least partly independent, and one model driving all three agents undercuts that. Varying the backbone across agents, or at minimum varying temperature and prompting strategy, is a concrete and testable improvement over what is done here, and it is an ablation this paper leaves open.
- **Settle the debate-versus-hierarchy question with an experiment rather than a citation.** AlphaAgents argues for peer-to-peer debate, FinCon argues hierarchy with no lateral chatter is strictly cheaper and better, and neither runs the comparison. Since both papers are directly in my scope and they disagree, running that comparison on CSE data is a legitimate contribution in itself, and it is cheap because the two protocols share most of their implementation.
- **Use the modular positioning as the template for my own scoping.** AlphaAgents produces a screened list and feeds Mean-Variance Optimization or Black-Litterman downstream, FinCon has the LLM pick direction and a Markowitz solver pick size. Both keep the language model out of the sizing decision, which is the auditable part, and that convergence across two otherwise opposed designs is a strong enough signal to copy. It also gives me the clean insertion point for CSE liquidity and friction constraints, which belong in the optimizer, not in a prompt.
- **On evidence standards, treat this paper as the cautionary example.** Charts without numbers, one window, one sector, no significance testing, and a word like outperforms carrying results where cumulative return and rolling Sharpe are both negative throughout. I need the opposite discipline in my own write-up, quote the actual figures, report the economic result and the formal test side by side, and state the sign of the absolute return rather than only the relative ranking.

[^1]: Form 10-K and Form 10-Q are mandatory reports that public companies file with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). The 10-K is a detailed annual report with audited financial data, while the 10-Q is a shorter quarterly update with unaudited financial statements filed three times a year.
