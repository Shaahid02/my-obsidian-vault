Managerial Finance, Vol. 52 No. 3, pp. 517 to 539, Fernando (University of Oklahoma), Nimal (University of Sri Jayewardenepura) and Abeysekera (Hofstra), received October 2024 and accepted June 2025. A corporate finance paper asking whether firms listed on the Colombo Stock Exchange cut capital investment when their idiosyncratic risk rises, and whether insider and institutional ownership moderate that sensitivity, estimated on <font color="#ffc000">1,226 firm-year observations covering 167 unique CSE firms from 2010 to 2018</font> with firm and year fixed effects. DOI 10.1108/MF-10-2024-0791.

Nothing in this paper is a methodological template for what I'm building, there are no agents, no forecasts and no out-of-sample period anywhere in it, so I'm reading it as market-structure evidence rather than as a design precedent. What makes it worth a full note rather than a paragraph is that it is the most careful treatment of CSE firm-level data I have found, and in the course of being careful it concedes three things about this market that my own design has to absorb: that <font color="#ffc000">weekly returns are used because most CSE stocks are not traded daily</font>, that usable ownership data exists for roughly a third of the firms that have usable accounting data, and that the headline relationship weakens substantially once the sample is extended through 2021. Those are constraints on the technical, volatility and fundamental channels respectively, stated by authors who had no reason to be making a point about data depth, which is exactly why I trust them more than I'd trust the same claim in a methods section arguing for its own contribution.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **The investment-risk sensitivity literature is almost entirely developed-market.** Panousi and Papanikolaou (2012) in the Journal of Finance is the anchor result, risk-averse managers underinvest when firm-specific risk rises, and the follow-on work (Glover and Levine, 2017; Liu and Wang, 2021; Florackis et al., 2019) stays in US and UK data. The authors' claim is not that the mechanism is absent elsewhere, it's that the *sign of the ownership moderation* has no reason to carry over, because the agency environment that produces it is different.
- **The alignment against entrenchment question is genuinely open in emerging markets.** Higher insider ownership can tie a manager's wealth to the firm's growth and push them toward long-horizon investment (the alignment effect, Ang et al., 2000), or it can concentrate their undiversified personal wealth in one firm and make them more risk-averse (the entrenchment effect, Fahlenbrach and Stulz, 2009). Developed-market evidence mostly favours entrenchment. The paper argues that weaker legal protection and weaker formal governance in emerging markets (La Porta et al., 1998; Khanna and Palepu, 2002) raise the marginal value of insider ownership as a substitute governance mechanism, which would tilt the balance toward alignment. That is a testable framing and it is the paper's actual contribution.
- **No South Asian evidence at all, by the authors' own statement.** They claim first coverage of this relationship in the region, which I can't independently verify but which is consistent with what I found when I went looking for CSE firm-level risk studies.

#### Data and setup

The sample is CSE-listed firms from 2010 to 2018, excluding financials and heavily state-regulated sectors (utilities, energy, telecoms), firm-years with missing key variables, and negative book values. Both listed and delisted firms are retained, which is a deliberate survivorship-bias control and more than most of the local literature does. <font color="#ffff00">All accounting and insider ownership data is hand-collected from annual reports published by the CSE</font>, prices and the index come from the CSE data libraries, and Treasury bill rates from the Central Bank of Sri Lanka.

The variable that matters most to me is the risk measure, so it's worth setting out how it is built. For each firm $i$ and each week $t$, weekly excess returns over the risk-free rate are regressed on the Fama and French (1993) three factors, and the annual idiosyncratic risk is the standard deviation of the residuals from that regression over 52 trading weeks:

$$r_{i,t} - r_{f,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_i^{MKT}\,\text{MKT}_t + \beta_i^{SMB}\,\text{SMB}_t + \beta_i^{HML}\,\text{HML}_t + \varepsilon_{i,t}, \qquad \sigma_i^{idi} = \text{sd}\big(\hat{\varepsilon}_{i,t}\big)_{t=1}^{52}$$

where:
- $r_{i,t}$ is the weekly return adjusted for cash dividends, stock splits, scrip dividends, bonus issues and rights issues
- the market proxy is the percentage change in the <font color="#ffc000">All Share Total Return Index (ASTRI)</font>, not the ASPI, which is the right choice since it is total return rather than price only
- $r_{f,t}$ is a weekly rate derived from the yield on 91-day Treasury bills
- weekly returns exceeding $+50\%$ or falling below $-50\%$ are dropped as outliers, following Abeysekera and Nimal (2017)

> [!important]
> **Weekly, not daily, and the paper says why in one sentence: "since most stocks are not traded daily, we use weekly stock returns."** That is a finance journal on my exact market conceding the non-synchronous trading problem in the data section rather than the limitations section. Every frequency decision I make downstream has to answer to it.

Systematic risk enters as a control and is backed out of the decomposition rather than estimated separately, $\sigma^{sys} = \sqrt{\sigma^{tot\,2} - \sigma^{idi\,2}}$ with total risk the standard deviation of weekly returns, which is clean enough that it doesn't need a display equation. Insider ownership is the share of total outstanding shares held by executive directors on the board at year end, institutional ownership is the share held by pension funds, investment banks, mutual funds and companies holding more than 3% (both following Farwis and Azeez, 2019). Controls are cash flow over lagged PPE, firm size as the firm's PPE relative to total sample PPE that year, and leverage as total debt over total assets.

The descriptive statistics carry two numbers I keep coming back to. Mean idiosyncratic risk is <font color="#ffc000">0.221 with a standard deviation of only 0.050 and a full range of 0.117 to 0.315</font>, so the cross-sectional spread in firm-specific risk on this market is narrow, and <font color="#ffc000">insider and institutional ownership correlate at −0.703</font>, which matters a great deal once the design starts splitting firms two ways on both at once. Mean insider ownership is 5.7% against mean institutional ownership of 66.1%, so this is a market of institutionally held firms with thin executive stakes.

The sample accounting is where the data-depth problem becomes visible. The full panel is 1,226 firm-years across 167 firms, but ownership data is only available from 2011, and <font color="#ff0000">the insider ownership sub-sample is 59 firms and the institutional sub-sample 54 firms</font>, which is what produces the 297 and 278 observation counts in the moderation tables.

#### Model and estimation

Three nested fixed-effect panel specifications, all with the investment rate as dependent variable and standard errors clustered at the firm level. Model 1 is the baseline:

$$\left(\frac{I_{i,t}}{K_{i,t-1}}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1\log\left(\sigma_{i,t-1}^{idi}\right) + \beta_2\log\left(\sigma_{i,t-1}^{sys}\right) + \beta_3\left(\frac{CF_t}{K_{t-1}}\right)_{i,t-1} + \beta_4\log\left(\hat{K}_{i,t-1}\right) + \beta_5\log\left(\frac{D_{i,t-1}}{A_{i,t-1}}\right) + \eta_i + g_t + \nu_{i,t}$$

where:
- $I_{i,t}$ is the change in net PPE in year $t$ and $K_{i,t-1}$ is lagged net PPE, so the dependent variable is an investment rate rather than a level
- $\eta_i$ and $g_t$ are firm and year fixed effects
- risk, size and leverage all enter in logs, following Panousi and Papanikolaou (2012)
- $\beta_1$ is the coefficient of interest, and the whole paper is about its sign and what moves it

Model 2 is where the design becomes interesting for my purposes, because rather than interacting risk with continuous ownership it sorts firms each year into two equally sized groups on the previous year's insider ownership relative to the annual median, and interacts risk with each group dummy, omitting the intercept to avoid the dummy trap:

$$\left(\frac{I_{i,t}}{K_{i,t-1}}\right) = \beta_1\log\left(\sigma_{i,t-1}^{idi}\right)\times \text{INSD}_L + \beta_2\log\left(\sigma_{i,t-1}^{idi}\right)\times \text{INSD}_H + \beta_3\log\left(\sigma_{i,t-1}^{sys}\right) + \text{controls} + \text{INSD}_L + \text{INSD}_H + \eta_i + g_t + \nu_{i,t}$$

Model 3 extends the same idea to a $2\times2$ grid, sorting first on institutional ownership and then on insider ownership within each institutional group, which yields four interaction terms and lets the paper ask whether institutional monitoring substitutes for insider alignment. The mechanics are identical to Model 2 so the equation doesn't earn a third display, but the design choice does: splitting at the median annually and reading the *group-specific slope* rather than a single interaction coefficient is a cleaner way of asking a conditioning question than a continuous interaction would be, and it is close to the structure I'd want for ablating state inputs one at a time.

#### Findings

- **Idiosyncratic risk is negatively and significantly related to firm-level investment across every specification, with a coefficient of −0.165\*\*\* in the year-fixed-effects-only column rising to −0.232\*\*\* with full controls and firm fixed effects, and −0.199\*\* once a lagged dependent variable is added.** The economic size is the number worth quoting: <font color="#ffc000">a one standard deviation rise in idiosyncratic risk cuts the investment rate by 2.02 percentage points against a sample mean of 11.60%, a 17.48% decline</font>. Adjusted R² moves from 0.029 to 0.206 across the three columns, so most of the explanatory power is coming from the fixed effects and controls rather than from risk, which is normal in this literature and worth remembering before reading the coefficient as a forecast.

![[Pasted image 20261004140002.png]]

- **The negative sensitivity is concentrated entirely in low-insider-ownership firms, and that is the opposite of the US result.** The interaction of risk with the low insider dummy is −0.435\*\*\* with year effects only and −0.599\*\* with the full specification, while the high insider dummy interaction is statistically indistinguishable from zero throughout (0.035, standard error 0.180). The authors read this as the alignment effect dominating entrenchment, which contradicts Panousi and Papanikolaou (2012) directly. Figure 1 is the cleanest statement of it, the low-ownership line falls from roughly 0.23 to 0.055 across the plotted range of log lagged idiosyncratic risk while the high-ownership line is flat and very slightly positive.

![[Pasted image 20261004140003.png]]

- **Institutional ownership substitutes for insider alignment.** In the $2\times2$ design the only significantly negative cell is low institutional combined with low insider ownership, at −0.566\*\*\* and then −0.985\*\* with full controls, while the high-institutional-low-insider cell is insignificant at −0.392 (standard error 0.276). The reading the authors take is that institutional monitoring picks up the governance work that insider stakes do elsewhere, which is consistent with Shleifer and Vishny (1986) and Gillan and Starks (2000), and I'd hold it a little more loosely than they do because the insignificance could be power rather than absence given the cell counts.

![[Pasted image 20261004140004.png]]

- **The robustness battery is unusually thorough and mostly holds.** Industry fixed effects and industry-by-year fixed effects leave the pattern intact, propensity score matched samples reproduce it, family firms (33% of firm-years, identified by hand from annual reports and executive biographies following Anderson and Reeb, 2003) show no differential sensitivity across five alternative family-ownership measures, and adding independent board fraction and CEO duality changes nothing, which echoes Caixe et al. (2019) finding no governance mitigation in Brazil. For a paper whose headline result contradicts the US literature, that is the right amount of work to do before publishing it.
- **Extending the sample three years to 2021 weakens the baseline result from −0.232\*\*\* to −0.123\*, and the paper calls this "largely consistent".** The ownership results survive better, low insider at −0.407\*\* and low-institutional-low-insider at −0.787\*\*, and the high-institutional-low-insider cell now turns significant at −0.433\*\*. I read the halving of the main coefficient and the loss of two significance stars as the more informative result here, and it is the single most useful thing in the paper for my own period argument.

#### Limitations

- **The moderation results, which are the paper's contribution, rest on very few firms.** 59 unique firms and 297 firm-years for the insider tests, 54 firms and 278 for the institutional tests, and after propensity score matching <font color="#ff0000">64 and 76 observations respectively</font>, all of this carrying firm fixed effects, year fixed effects and category dummies. At those sample sizes firm fixed effects consume a large share of the degrees of freedom and the matched-sample estimates in particular are being asked to do more than the data can support. The authors are not hiding any of this, the counts are in every table, but the write-up treats the matched results as confirmation when at 64 observations they are closer to a consistency check.
- **The −0.703 correlation between insider and institutional ownership makes the $2\times2$ grid structurally unbalanced, and the insignificant cells are where that bites.** Two variables correlating that strongly cannot produce four well-populated quadrants from 278 observations, so the high-insider-high-institutional cell is thin by construction, and every coefficient the paper reads as "no effect" is in a cell that would struggle to detect one. The substitution story the paper tells could be right, but the evidence for it is one significant cell and three insignificant ones, and the design can't separate a genuine null from low power.
- **Non-synchronous trading is handled by moving to weekly data and then not mentioned again.** Weekly returns reduce the problem relative to daily but they do not eliminate it on a market where the paper itself says most stocks don't trade daily, and stale prices bias the residual standard deviation downward for exactly the thinnest counters. There is no Dimson or Scholes-Williams correction, no reported trading-frequency screen, and no check of whether the idiosyncratic risk measure correlates with turnover. That matters because firm size is a control and size correlates with liquidity, so part of what the size coefficient absorbs may be measurement error in the risk variable rather than an investment determinant. The $\pm50\%$ weekly return trim is a sensible outlier rule and it is also a reminder of what the raw series looks like.
- **The investment measure is a net quantity doing the work of a gross one.** The change in net PPE nets out depreciation and disposals, so a firm that holds capex constant while an existing asset base ages records a falling investment rate, and a firm that sells a property records a negative one, which is why the minimum in Table 2 is −0.082. Capex is reported separately in Sri Lankan annual reports and the paper hand-collects everything else, so this is a choice rather than a constraint, inherited from Panousi and Papanikolaou for comparability, and it leaves the dependent variable noisier than the framing suggests.
- **Alignment against entrenchment is inferred from a sign pattern rather than tested.** Nothing in the design observes managerial risk preference, wealth concentration or the project set, so the mechanism is read off the direction of an interaction coefficient. Both effects are present in the theory section with opposite predictions, the data picks one sign, and the paper attributes it to the stronger of the two forces, which is a reasonable inference and not a test. The authors say as much in their conclusion, where they note the underlying mechanisms warrant further exploration.
- **The 2018 cutoff is well-argued and the extension quietly undercuts the argument for it.** SLFRS 16 changing lease recognition in 2019 genuinely contaminates PPE, and that justifies the main sample. But the extended analysis to 2021 then runs straight through SLFRS 16, the Easter attacks and COVID with no adjustment to the PPE measure and no regime split, and reports the result as consistent, so the paper ends up both claiming the break is serious enough to truncate the sample and treating a sample that spans it as corroborating evidence.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **This is my citation for why the technical agent cannot assume a daily price series on this market.** A finance journal, estimating a factor model on CSE firms, moves to weekly data because most stocks don't trade daily, and that is a stronger warrant than anything I could get from the ML literature. It doesn't mean I build at weekly frequency, the index track is liquid enough for daily, it means the per-counter liquidity screen has to come before any cross-sectional feature is computed and counters that fail it have to produce an abstention rather than a stale-price signal. This is the same structural failure [[AlphaAgents, Large Language Model Based Multi-Agents for Equity Portfolio Constructions]] fixed by deleting an agent by hand.
- **The idiosyncratic risk construction is the estimator I'd reuse for the volatility agent's cross-sectional input, with the liquidity correction they skip.** Residual standard deviation from a local factor model over a rolling window is the right shape, the ASTRI and 91-day Treasury bill plumbing is established, and Abeysekera and Nimal (2017) gives me a four-factor local alternative. What I add is the non-synchronous trading correction and a reported correlation between the risk estimate and turnover, because if those two move together on my sample then the volatility agent and the liquidity agent are partly measuring the same thing and the orchestrator's state vector has a collinearity problem I need to know about before I start weighting on it.
- **Mean idiosyncratic risk of 0.221 with a standard deviation of 0.050 sets a realistic prior on how much cross-sectional signal the volatility channel can carry.** That is a narrow spread, and a one standard deviation move being worth 2.02 percentage points of investment is a real-economy effect rather than a trading one, so I should not expect the cross-sectional volatility ranking on CSE counters to separate firms as cleanly as it does on a US sample. Worth pinning down on my own data before the agent's influence weight is allowed to swing much on it.
- **The investment channel is the mechanism-level argument for pairing forecast variance with persistence rather than treating volatility as a point-in-time cap.** If elevated idiosyncratic risk causes firms to cut capital spending by 17.48% of the mean investment rate, then a volatility shock on this market propagates into fundamentals with a lag measured in reporting periods, not days, and the real activity it suppresses takes just as long to come back. That is a fundamental reason to expect slow decay, independent of the statistical long-memory evidence in [[The effect of COVID-19 on long memory in returns and volatility of cryptocurrency and stock markets]] and [[Modeling Long-Term Volatility Memory Dynamics in the Colombo Stock Exchange]], and it is a better sentence for the proposal than another citation of a $d$ estimate.
- **167 firms with usable accounting data and 59 with usable ownership data, all of it hand-collected from annual report PDFs, is the data-depth constraint arriving from a direction I hadn't checked.** My liquidity screen question has been how many counters trade often enough to model, and this adds a second filter underneath it, how many have fundamentals complete enough to extract, with the answer coming from three authors who had institutional access, every incentive to keep the sample large, and who still spent the effort to pull accounting figures, insider stakes, board composition, CEO duality and family-firm status out of CSE filings by hand over nine years. That prices the extraction problem and explains why the retrieval pipeline over those filings is the component most likely to be the bottleneck, which is the case the structure-aware chunking work in [[Financial Report Chunking for Effective Retrieval Augmented Generation]] addresses and reduces rather than removes. It is also why the fundamental agent is being designed for a channel that is frequently absent rather than occasionally imperfect, with abstention as the structural consequence.
- **SLFRS 16 in 2019 is a documented structural break in CSE fundamentals sitting in the middle of my training window.** My index track runs 1 July 2014 to 30 June 2024, so any PPE-derived or lease-sensitive fundamental feature is not comparable across the 2019 boundary, and the fix is either a feature set that avoids the affected line items or an explicit break control with the choice stated in the methodology. I'd rather find this now than discover it in an ablation.
- **The 2021 extension weakening the main coefficient from −0.232\*\*\* to −0.123\* is corroboration that CSE relationships estimated pre-2020 do not survive the crisis window intact.** Taken with [[The Relationship between Macroeconomic Variables, Trading Volume and Stock Returns, Granger Causality Evidence from the Colombo Stock Exchange]] finding the traditional macro linkages broken over 2014 to 2022, that is now two independent CSE results pointing at regime instability across exactly the period a static system would be evaluated on, which is the clearest empirical case I have for an orchestrator that conditions on measured state rather than a fixed combination rule.
- **Mean institutional ownership of 66.1% against insider ownership of 5.7% is a free-float fact with a liquidity consequence.** Two thirds of shares held by institutions means the tradeable float is a fraction of market capitalisation, so order size against available depth is worse than a capitalisation-based estimate would suggest, and the participation-rate sweep in the execution cost work needs to be calibrated against float rather than shares outstanding. This is cheap to check on my own data and I should check it before setting the sweep range.
