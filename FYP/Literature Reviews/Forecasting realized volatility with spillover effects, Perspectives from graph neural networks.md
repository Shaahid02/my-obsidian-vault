International Journal of Forecasting 41(2), 2025, pages 377 to 397, Chao Zhang (HKUST(GZ) and Oxford-Man Institute), Xingyue Pu, Mihai Cucuringu and Xiaowen Dong (Oxford and the Alan Turing Institute), circulated earlier as "Graph Neural Networks for Forecasting Realized Volatility with (Nonlinear) Spillover Effects". The paper proposes <font color="#ffc000">GNNHAR</font>, a nonparametric extension of the HAR family that replaces the linear neighbourhood aggregation in <font color="#ffff00">GHAR</font> with a graph convolutional layer, and then uses that architecture as an instrument to answer three separable questions: whether volatility spillover reaches beyond one-hop neighbours, whether the spillover is nonlinear, and whether the <font color="#ffc000">estimation criterion</font> used to fit the model matters as much as the model itself. The answers are no, yes at short horizons, and yes substantially, in that order, and the third is the one that generalises furthest.

This is the first pure volatility-forecasting paper in the vault that is both graph-based and properly evaluated, so it sits at the intersection of two things I already have separately: the long-memory work in [[The effect of COVID-19 on long memory in returns and volatility of cryptocurrency and stock markets]] and [[Sri Lankan Stock Market Volatility Analysis, An ARMA-GARCH Approach]] on one side, and the cross-sectional graph construction in [[Neo4j Graph-RAG for CSE]] on the other. The distance from my own build is moderate rather than large, since there is no agent, no orchestration and no LLM anywhere in it, but the object it forecasts, next-day realised volatility conditioned on a cross-sectional graph, is exactly what the econometric estimator inside my system would have to produce, and the way the paper reports its results, every number as a ratio to a HAR baseline with a model confidence set attached, is the reporting discipline I want to carry across. What I want from it sits in three places: the QL versus MSE result, which is an argument about loss functions that applies to any heteroskedastic target and costs nothing to adopt, the calm-versus-turbulent stratification, which is a cleaner template for market-state conditioning than [[MacroHFT, Memory Augmented Context-aware Reinforcement Learning On High Frequency Trading]] offers, and the negative multi-hop result, which tells me how much graph machinery is worth building before the returns stop.

> [!NOTE]
> **Realised volatility and the HAR lag structure.** For asset $i$ on day $t$, $RV_{i,t} = \sum_{l=1}^{M} r_{i,t(l)}^2$, the sum of squared intraday log returns over five-minute windows, a consistent estimator of the unobserved integrated variance under the standard diffusion. The HAR model of Corsi (2009) regresses $RV_{i,t}$ on its own daily, weekly and monthly averages, $\boldsymbol{v}_{t-1}$, $\boldsymbol{v}_{t-5:t-2}$ and $\boldsymbol{v}_{t-22:t-6}$, which is the cheapest available way to reproduce the long-memory decay in the RV autocorrelation function without fitting a fractionally integrated model. Everything in this paper is a modification of the cross-sectional term bolted onto that three-lag spine, never a modification of the spine itself.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **The HAR line ignores cross-asset spillover, and the models that capture spillover do not scale.** ARCH, GARCH and stochastic volatility models, and HAR after them, all condition a stock's volatility on its own history alone, which Bollerslev, Hood et al. (2018) identify as the missing channel. The multivariate answers, BEKK-GARCH (Engle and Kroner, 1995), VAR-GARCH (Ling and McAleer, 2003) and the VAR approach of Wilms et al. (2021), hit the curse of dimensionality in exactly the high-dimensional setting where cross-sectional information would be worth having, as Callot et al. (2017) emphasise. Zhang et al. (2022) resolved this with GHAR, augmenting HAR with a neighbourhood-aggregation term over a sparse financial graph, and the present paper is a direct continuation of that line by the same first author, which is worth keeping in view when reading how favourably GHAR is treated throughout.

- **Nobody had checked whether the spillover reaches past one hop.** GHAR aggregates over zero-hop and one-hop neighbours and stops. Whether a shock to a stock two edges away in the graph carries predictive information is a question the cascade literature (Acemoglu et al., 2010) suggests should be answered yes, and the authors state plainly that it has not been examined for volatility modelling. This is the cleanest gap in the paper and, as it turns out, the one where the answer is negative.

  ![[Pasted image 20260926213001.png]]

- **Nonlinear spillover has been documented but only under assumed functional forms.** Choudhry et al. (2016) found significant nonlinear spillover among four major markets using the Bai et al. (2010) causality test, and Wang et al. (2018) captured nonlinearity between equities and crude by imposing asymmetric effects and regime shifts. The authors' position is that these are parametric commitments made in advance, and that a GNN can provide nonparametric evidence for nonlinear cross-sectional mechanisms without assuming asymmetry or regimes in the pairwise interaction. That framing is honest about what the contribution is, evidence rather than a new test.

- **The estimation criterion is a free parameter that the RV literature has effectively fixed at MSE by habit.** GARCH-family models are fitted by quasi-likelihood, HAR-family models by OLS, which is MSE, and the two literatures rarely meet. Cipollini et al. (2020) is the exception the authors cite, finding QL slightly better on linear HAR without a firm theoretical explanation. Since a model fitted by stochastic gradient descent will minimise whatever it is handed, the flexibility to swap the criterion comes free, and the authors are right that this is not specific to GNNs.

#### Data and setup

| Item | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Source | LOBSTER, tick data, five-minute RV following Liu et al. (2015) |
| Universe | 27 DJIA components trading continuously over the full period, plus S&P 100 for robustness |
| Period | 1 July 2007 to 30 June 2021 |
| Out-of-sample | 1 July 2011 to 30 June 2021, ten years |
| Rolling scheme | 36 months training, most recent 12 months validation, next month tested, recalibrated monthly |
| Graph | GLASSO (Friedman et al., 2008) on multivariate daily returns, precision matrix binarised, sparsity by $k$-fold CV |
| Horizons | One day, one week ($h=4$), one month ($h=21$), targets $\boldsymbol{v}_{t:t+h} = \sum_{k=0}^{h}\boldsymbol{v}_{t+k}$ |
| Estimation | HAR and GHAR by OLS when MSE, by Adam when QL; GNNHARs by Adam, lr $10^{-3}$, batch 32 |
| Hidden dimension | Grid search over $\{3, 6, 9, 16, 32\}$, nine selected and held fixed across layer counts |
| Tests | Model confidence set (Hansen et al., 2011) and Diebold-Mariano with the Harvey et al. (1997) correction |

Two rows do more work than the rest. The graph row is where an assumption enters quietly: GLASSO is applied to daily *returns*, so the estimated conditional independence structure is a return-dependence graph, then used as the adjacency for a *volatility* spillover model, a step asserted on the strength of the authors' earlier comparison of graph constructions rather than tested here. The recalibration row is the reason the ten-year out-of-sample result is credible: 120 separate monthly refits on a rolling window, not one train-test split, which is a much higher bar than most of the ML papers in the vault clear.

Appendix A.2 reports the shortest-path-distance distribution and it is the number that decides how much room the multi-hop question ever had. On the DJIA graph, 57.7% of node pairs are one hop apart and 41.8% are two hops, leaving 0.5% at three hops and nothing beyond, so the graph has a diameter of three and is nearly complete at two. On S&P 100 the distribution spreads out, 24.3% at one hop, 61.2% at two, 12.0% at three, 2.2% at four and 0.3% at five, which is why the robustness section extends to four- and five-layer models there.

#### Model architecture and training

GHAR is the immediate baseline and it is worth writing out, because the proposed model is one substitution away from it:

$$\textbf{GHAR}(\boldsymbol{A}): \quad \mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{v}_t|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}) = \boldsymbol{\alpha} + \boldsymbol{V}_{:t-1}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{V}_{:t-1}\boldsymbol{\gamma}$$

where:
- $\boldsymbol{V}_{:t-1} = [\boldsymbol{v}_{t-1}, \boldsymbol{v}_{t-5:t-2}, \boldsymbol{v}_{t-22:t-6}] \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times 3}$ holds the daily, weekly and monthly lagged RV for all $N$ stocks
- $\boldsymbol{W} = \boldsymbol{O}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{O}^{-1/2}$ is the normalised adjacency without self-connections, $\boldsymbol{O} = \operatorname{diag}\{n_1,\dots,n_N\}$ and $n_i = \sum_j \boldsymbol{A}[i,j]$
- $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ carries the stock's own three lags, $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ carries the neighbour-averaged three lags

Setting $\boldsymbol{A} = \boldsymbol{0}$ recovers HAR exactly, and setting all off-diagonal entries to one recovers the global-average model of Bollerslev, Hood et al. (2018), so the whole comparison set is one equation with different adjacency matrices. GNNHAR replaces the $\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{V}_{:t-1}\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ term with a graph convolution:

$$\boldsymbol{H}^{(l+1)} = \operatorname{ReLU}\left(\boldsymbol{O}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{O}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{H}^{(l)}\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(l)}\right), \qquad \textbf{GNNHAR1L}(\boldsymbol{A}): \ \mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{v}_t|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}) = \boldsymbol{\alpha} + \boldsymbol{V}_{:t-1}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{H}^{(1)}\boldsymbol{\gamma}$$

with $\boldsymbol{H}^{(0)} = \boldsymbol{V}_{:t-1}$ and $\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(l)}$ trainable. Two design decisions inside this are worth naming. The propagation rule deliberately omits self-connections, unlike the Kipf and Welling (2017) GCN it is adapted from, so a stock's own past volatility enters only through the linear $\boldsymbol{V}_{:t-1}\boldsymbol{\beta}$ term and every nonlinearity in the model lives in the spillover channel. That is what makes the GHAR-to-GNNHAR1L comparison interpretable as a test of nonlinear spillover rather than a test of nonlinearity in general, and it is a design choice I would copy. The second is that multi-hop is operationalised by stacking layers, so GNNHAR2L and GNNHAR3L reach two and three hops respectively, which conflates reach with depth in a way the limitations section returns to.

The estimation criterion is the other half of the paper. The QL objective is

$$\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{T}_{\text{train}}}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{train}}}\left[\frac{RV_{i,t}}{\widehat{RV}^{(F)}_{i,t}} - \log\left(\frac{RV_{i,t}}{\widehat{RV}^{(F)}_{i,t}}\right) - 1\right]$$

which depends on the forecast *ratio* where MSE depends on the forecast *error*, and is asymmetric around one, penalising under-prediction far more heavily than over-prediction of the same proportional size. That asymmetry is the whole mechanism, and the figure showing the two loss curves makes the argument better than the algebra does.

![[Pasted image 20260926213002.png]]

Proposition 3.1 gives this a precise econometric reading: optimising a HAR model under QL is equivalent to iteratively reweighted least squares with weights $w_{k-1,t} = 1/\widehat{RV}^2_{k-1,t}$ taken from the previous iteration's fitted values, so QL is WLS that down-weights high-volatility observations in proportion to the square of the fitted level. Appendix B connects the same objective to the multiplicative error model of Engle (2002), making QL-trained models the multiplicative counterparts of MSE-trained additive ones, which is the cleanest available explanation for why QL handles heteroskedastic errors better.

#### Findings

- **Graph information helps, nonlinearity helps on top of it, and both effects are largest at the one-day horizon.** Reading the first two columns, GHAR$_M$ cuts one-day MSE to 0.927 of HAR$_M$, GNNHAR1L$_M$ takes it further to 0.907, and switching the criterion to QL improves nearly every row again, with GNNHAR1L$_Q$ reaching 0.867 in MSE and 0.961 in QL, roughly 13% and 4% below the standard HAR benchmark. Both GNNHAR1L$_Q$ and GNNHAR2L$_Q$ sit inside the model confidence set on QL loss, so the one-day ranking is supported by a test and not only by point estimates.

  ![[Pasted image 20260926213003.png]]

  The advantage decays as the horizon lengthens and can reverse. By one month most MSE ratios exceed one, with GNNHAR2L$_Q$ at 1.736 and GNNHAR3L$_Q$ at 1.502, while the QL ratios stay below one and GNNHAR2L$_Q$ at 0.947 remains inside the confidence set. The divergence between the two metrics widens with the horizon, and the explanation offered, that QL is less affected by the extreme observations that occur more often over longer aggregation windows (Patton, 2011), is plausible and is the authors' interpretation rather than something they isolate.

- **More layers make it worse, monotonically, and the mechanism is identified rather than guessed.** One-day MSE ratios run 0.907, 0.967 and 1.210 for one, two and three layers under MSE training, so the three-layer model is beaten by plain HAR. The mean average distance between node representations at the output layer falls with depth, from roughly $e^{-10.5}$ to $e^{-15.5}$ on the log scale, which is over-smoothing in the sense of Li et al. (2018) and Chen et al. (2020) and means the three-layer model's node representations have become close to indistinguishable. The authors' reading is that a suitable level of nonlinearity helps and an excessive one collapses, and the MAD evidence supports that specific mechanism rather than a generic overfitting story.

- **Multi-hop spillover does not clear the bar, on two separate tests, though neither is a test of absence.** The DM comparison of GNNHAR2L against GNNHAR1L gives a cross-sectional $p$-value of 0.75 under MSE training and 0.40 under QL, with AXP the only individual stock where the two-layer model is significantly superior at the 5% level. The cleaner test avoids depth entirely: GHAR2Hop adds an explicit two-hop aggregation term to GHAR without stacking anything, and its DM statistic against GHAR is about $-1$ with a $p$-value near 0.35. The estimated coefficients tell the same story quantitatively, with the daily two-hop coefficient at roughly one-eighth of its one-hop counterpart and the weekly and monthly one-hop and two-hop coefficients turning negative, which the authors attribute to collinearity.

- **Nonlinearity is concentrated in turbulent periods, and the fraction-of-variance-unexplained numbers are the sharpest evidence in the paper.** FVU measures how much of a model's forecast variation the linear HAR cannot explain, so a higher value means the model has departed further from the linear baseline. Every nonlinear model shows a larger FVU on turbulent days than on calm ones, at every horizon, with GNNHAR1L$_M$ at 0.077 calm against 0.165 turbulent for one-day forecasts and GNNHAR3L$_M$ at 0.079 against 0.300.

  ![[Pasted image 20260926213004.png]]

  Set beside the stratified losses, where GNNHAR1L$_Q$ reaches 0.834 of HAR$_M$ on one-day MSE during the top 10% turbulent days against 0.895 for its MSE-trained twin, this is consistent with nonlinear spillover being a turbulence phenomenon, and it is the finding I would most want to re-test on my own data before relying on it.

- **The results survive a smaller validation set and a larger universe.** Re-running with 47 months of training and one month of validation leaves the ordering essentially unchanged, and repeating everything on S&P 100 components reproduces the pattern: GHAR consistently beats HAR, GNNHAR1L improves on GHAR at the one-day horizon, deeper models degrade, and QL training helps throughout. GNNHAR2L$_Q$ posts the best one-day MSE there at 0.915, which is a mild crack in the multi-hop conclusion given that the S&P 100 graph has 12.0% of pairs at three hops against the DJIA's 0.5%.

#### Limitations

- **The multi-hop conclusion is drawn on a graph that barely has a second hop to test.** With 99.5% of DJIA node pairs within two hops and 57.7% within one, the incremental information a second hop can supply is structurally small before any model is fitted, so "two-hop neighbours are not needed once zero-hop and one-hop are included" is at least partly a statement about this graph's density. The GHAR2Hop test is the right design and it returns $p \approx 0.35$, which is a failure to reject rather than evidence of no effect, and the one case where the graph is sparser, the S&P 100 with 12% of pairs at three hops, is also the one case where a two-layer model takes a best-in-column result. <font color="#ff0000">The conclusion I would actually carry forward is narrower than the paper's: on dense graphs of large-cap US equities, multi-hop aggregation does not pay for its parameters.</font>

- **The return-based graph is never compared against a volatility-based one.** GLASSO is estimated on daily returns and the resulting conditional-independence structure is used directly as the adjacency for a volatility spillover model, on the authority of a comparison of graph constructions in the authors' own earlier paper. Since the entire contribution is about what flows along the edges, the identity of the edges is the most load-bearing unexamined choice in the design, and a robustness check swapping in a graph estimated from RV correlations, or from sector membership, or a randomised graph with matched density, would have separated "spillover exists along economically meaningful edges" from "averaging over any sparse neighbourhood helps". The authors flag supply-chain graphs (Herskovic et al., 2020) and analyst co-coverage (Ali and Hirshleifer, 2020) as future work, which is an acknowledgement of the same point.

- **Nonlinearity is established through one architecture and one activation.** The evidence for nonlinear spillover is that adding ReLU and a trainable $\boldsymbol{\Theta}$ to GHAR's aggregation improves forecasts, which is a fair test of the existence of *some* nonlinearity but tells me nothing about its shape. No alternative nonlinear aggregation is tried, no attention-weighted variant, no comparison against a parametric asymmetric specification of the kind Wang et al. (2018) used, so the finding is that a nonlinear model beats a linear one on this data and the interpretation that this reveals a nonlinear spillover mechanism rests on the no-self-connection design rather than on a direct probe. FVU is used as a proxy for the degree of nonlinearity and is well chosen, but it measures divergence from HAR's forecasts, not curvature in the spillover function.

- **The turbulence stratification uses the full-sample distribution to define its own regimes.** Calm and turbulent days are split at the 90% quantile of the S&P 500 ETF RV computed over its entire sample, so the classification of any given test day uses information from after that day. Nothing in model selection or training touches this, so no forecast is contaminated, but every statement about turbulent-period performance is conditioned on an ex-post label that a live system would have to estimate in real time, and the stratified results would look different under a rolling quantile. This matters more for me than for the paper, because I would be using the regime label as a live input rather than as a reporting split.

- **Forecast loss is the only outcome, and the economic question is left open.** Everything is MSE and QL ratios against a benchmark, with no portfolio, no value-at-risk backtest and no transaction-cost-bearing application, so a 13% MSE reduction at the one-day horizon has no translation into a decision anywhere in the paper. The authors cite Goyenko et al. (2024) on economic loss functions and leave it there. Given that the motivation for QL is that it weights errors in a way that matters during turbulence, an application where turbulence-period accuracy has a payoff would have closed the argument. The setting compounds this: five-minute RV on DJIA and S&P 100 constituents assumes continuous trading at a frequency where microstructure noise is manageable, and every parameter choice, the 22-day monthly lag, the five-minute sampling, the 36-month window, is calibrated to that world. This is the same external-validity boundary that [[HLOB – Information persistence and structure in limit order books]] draws around itself, and in both cases the transferable content is the method and the diagnostics, not the numbers.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **Swap the training loss before swapping the model.** The single cheapest result here is that changing the estimation criterion from MSE to QL improved nearly every model in the table, including the linear ones estimated by OLS, and on one-day forecasts a QL-trained GHAR (0.886) beats an MSE-trained GNNHAR1L (0.907), so the criterion bought more than the architecture did. CSE realised volatility will be more heteroskedastic than DJIA volatility, not less, given the thin book and the periodic circuit-breaker-driven quiet spells, so if I fit any volatility forecaster for the risk-control component, QL is the default and MSE is the thing I would need to justify. The IRLS equivalence in Proposition 3.1 also means I can implement this on a linear model without a neural network at all, which matters when the sample is short.

- **Report everything as a ratio to a HAR baseline with a model confidence set attached.** Every number in this paper is normalised against HAR$_M$ and every best-in-column claim carries an MCS asterisk, which is why the results are readable and why the negative findings are as credible as the positive ones. My evaluation protocol currently has no equivalent convention, and adopting this one costs nothing: HAR is trivial to fit on CSE daily data, it is a genuinely hard baseline for volatility, and a ratio above one is an immediately legible admission that a component is not earning its place. The MCS plus cross-sectionally averaged DM pairing is the specific machinery to copy.

- **The calm-versus-turbulent split is a better regime template than MacroHFT's, and it exposes the labelling problem I have to solve anyway.** MacroHFT conditions on trend slope and a realised-volatility tertile and folds everything into one average; this paper splits the out-of-sample period at the 90% quantile of a market-wide RV index and reports both panels separately, which is what reveals that the nonlinear models earn their keep almost entirely in the top decile. That is the reporting shape I want for the orchestrator's evaluation. It also surfaces the gap: the paper's label is computed ex post, and a live orchestrator needs the same label from a rolling estimate, so pinning the minimum window for a usable turbulence indicator on CSE data is now a concrete task rather than an open question, and it is the same estimation-window problem already flagged against the persistence work.

- **Over-smoothing is the volatility-model version of the weight-entropy diagnostic, and I should pre-register the analogue.** The MAD measure catches the three-layer model's representations collapsing towards each other and explains a failure that the loss table alone would leave as "deeper is worse". My orchestrator has the same structural risk one level up, where a learned mixture over heterogeneous agents can collapse to near-one-hot, which is the failure MacroHFT's own Figure 5 shows and which I have already committed to reporting either way. Formalising both as representation-collapse diagnostics, MAD over node representations and entropy over agent weights, gives me one consistent story about capacity that does not pay for itself, and a second instance to cite for it.

- **A CSE cross-sectional volatility graph is buildable but the construction has to be justified locally, not imported.** GLASSO on daily returns is the paper's choice and, as the limitations note, it is never validated against alternatives. On the CSE the same procedure faces a problem that does not arise on the DJIA: a meaningful share of counters post zero returns on a given day because they did not trade, which biases any covariance estimate towards spurious independence and will make the precision matrix unstable. If I build a graph, the honest version includes a density-matched random-graph control and a non-trading filter, and the shortest-path-distance table from Appendix A.2 is the cheap descriptive statistic to compute first, because it tells me immediately whether a CSE graph is dense like the DJIA's or spread out like the S&P 100's, and therefore whether multi-hop is even a question worth asking here. Whatever that answers, one hop plus a nonlinearity is where the performance sits across both universes, which caps how much graph machinery the estimator component needs and frees effort for the orchestration, where my contribution actually lies. That is the same lesson the DRL portfolio work gave about encoder complexity, now on a different task and a different architecture family, which makes it worth holding as a standing prior rather than a one-paper observation.

- **The QL loss ratio is a second candidate for the comparable-confidence problem, and a better-behaved one than $p_\text{T}$.** The open question in the matrices is how an LLM analyst, an econometric estimator and an RL allocator emit confidence on one scale. A rolling QL loss ratio against a fixed benchmark is unit-free, computable for any component that emits a distributional or point forecast of the same target, and interpretable in the same direction regardless of the component's internals, which makes it a rolling competence weight in exactly the way the $p_\text{T}$ metric from HLOB is for directional components. Neither gives a per-decision confidence, both give a backward-looking competence estimate, and having two of them defined on different output types is progress on the problem rather than a solution to it.

- **Five-minute RV is unavailable, so the estimator's input has to be re-derived before any of this transfers.** Everything here assumes intraday tick data supporting five-minute sampling with subsampling corrections. CSE intraday data at that resolution is not something I have established access to, and the retention question on the exchange API is already gating other parts of the design. The realistic substitutes are daily range-based estimators, Parkinson or Garman-Klass, which changes the noise properties of the target and therefore changes what the QL weighting is doing, so the loss-function result is the part that transfers cleanly and the HAR lag structure is the part that needs re-fitting on whatever estimator I end up with. Establishing which RV proxy is feasible is a prerequisite to this whole component, not a detail inside it.
