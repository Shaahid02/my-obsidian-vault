Gu, Kelly and Xiu, Chicago Booth and Yale, November 2018 working version of what became the *Review of Financial Studies* paper that everything in this area now cites. A comparative horse race of thirteen prediction methods, OLS, a three-characteristic OLS benchmark, PLS, PCR, elastic net, a spline generalized linear model with group lasso, random forest, gradient boosted trees, and feed-forward networks at one through five hidden layers, all run on the same problem: predict next month's excess return for every US stock, from nearly 30,000 stocks over 60 years, using 920 features built from 94 stock characteristics, eight macroeconomic series and 74 industry dummies. <font color="#ffc000">The headline is that neural networks win, that the win comes specifically from nonlinear interactions among predictors, that shallow beats deep, and that every method agrees on the same small set of dominant signals: price trends, liquidity, and volatility.</font>

This is not a competitor architecture and I am not reading it as one, there are no agents here and no orchestration problem. It earns a full note for four reasons. It is the best available calibration of what a well-specified return predictor actually achieves, and the number is small enough that it should reset my expectations for the technical agent before I build it. The signal families it ranks at the top are price trend, liquidity and volatility, which is independent large-sample evidence for two of the three state variables my orchestrator conditions on. Its feature construction is the single-model version of conditioning on market state, every characteristic interacted with every macro variable, which is structurally the thing I am proposing to do one level up. And it is a clean, well-documented instance of the failure mode my cost sub-gap exists to address, reporting 113% monthly turnover and an annualised Sharpe of 2.35 in the same table without deducting a basis point.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **Two literatures had accumulated hundreds of predictors and no way to evaluate them jointly.** The cross-sectional strand regresses future returns on a few lagged characteristics, the time series strand regresses aggregate returns on a few macro variables, and the authors note that the profession has accumulated a staggering list of candidates, Green et al. counting 330 stock-level signals, Harvey et al. 316 factors, Welch and Goyal nearly 20 aggregate predictors. The stated problem is how to assess the incremental content of a newly proposed predictor while jointly controlling for the gamut of extant signals, which is the multiple-comparisons problem dressed as an estimation problem.
- **Three specific properties of the setting are argued to suit machine learning, and the argument is careful.** Risk premium measurement is a conditional expectation, so it is a prediction problem rather than an inference problem; the predictor count approaches the observation count and the predictors are close cousins, which is where OLS breaks; and theory gives essentially no guidance on functional form, so the search over specifications has to be mechanical.
- **The framing is explicitly measurement, not mechanism, and the authors say so.** Section 1.6 concedes that improved predictions are only measurements, that they do not identify deep associations between prices and conditioning variables, and that recovering mechanisms requires the economist to impose structure. It is the most honest paragraph in the paper and worth keeping in view, because the variable importance results later on are routinely cited as if they were economic findings.

#### Data and setup

CRSP monthly total returns for every firm on NYSE, AMEX and NASDAQ, March 1957 to December 2016, with the Treasury bill rate used to form excess returns.

| Component | Setting |
| --- | --- |
| Universe | ~30,000 stocks, average >6,200 per month |
| Period | March 1957 to December 2016, 60 years |
| Split | 18 years train (1957–1974), 12 validation (1975–1986), 30 test (1987–2016) |
| Features | 94 characteristics × (8 macro + 1) + 74 industry dummies = 920 |
| Characteristic frequency | 61 annual, 13 quarterly, 20 monthly |
| Target | one-month-ahead individual excess return, pooled panel |
| Refit | recursive, once per year, training window grows by one year each time |
| Validation | fixed-size, rolled forward twelve months per refit |
| Primary metric | $R^2_{oos}$ benchmarked against zero, not the historical mean |

Four choices in that grid matter more than they look. The sample is deliberately unfiltered, stocks below $5, share codes beyond 10 and 11, and financial firms all retained, which I note here because the economic results later depend on the small end of the universe. Publication lags are imposed explicitly, monthly characteristics delayed at most one month, quarterly at least four, annual at least six. Missing characteristics are replaced with the cross-sectional median for that month. And there is no cross-validation anywhere, by design, to preserve temporal ordering.

The over-arching feature construction is the part I care most about. Rather than feeding characteristics and macro variables in side by side, they take the Kronecker product, so every characteristic appears once on its own and once interacted with each of the eight macro state variables:

$$z_{i,t} = x_t \otimes c_{i,t}$$

where:
- $c_{i,t}$ is the $P_c \times 1$ vector of stock $i$'s characteristics at $t$
- $x_t$ is the $P_x \times 1$ vector of macroeconomic state variables, including a constant, common to all stocks

The motivation is the conditional beta-pricing identity $E_t(r_{i,t+1}) = \beta_{i,t}'\lambda_t$. If risk exposures are linear in characteristics, $\beta_{i,t} = \theta_1 c_{i,t}$, and the price of risk is linear in macro state, $\lambda_t = \theta_2 x_t$, then the model collapses exactly onto the interacted feature set, $g^\star(z_{i,t}) = (x_t \otimes c_{i,t})' \text{vec}(\theta_1'\theta_2)$. <font color="#ffc000">The interaction is not a convenience, it is where time variation in the signal's value enters the model.</font>

#### Model architecture and training

Every method shares the same target and the same splits, and the thirteen differ only in how they constrain $g(\cdot)$. PCR and PLS both compress 920 features into $K$ components, PCR's maximising variance among the predictors and PLS's maximising squared covariance with the target, so PLS sacrifices fidelity to $Z$ for predictive relevance. The generalized linear model adds a second-order spline expansion per characteristic under a group lasso penalty, so a characteristic enters with all $K$ of its spline terms or none, and critically it adds <font color="#ffff00">no interactions</font>, which is what makes it the diagnostic model of the paper. Networks are fixed at five architectures following the geometric pyramid rule, NN1 at 32 neurons through NN5 at 32-16-8-4-2, all ReLU, all fully connected, trained by SGD with Adam learning-rate shrinkage, $l_1$ penalisation, early stopping, batch normalisation, and seed ensembling.

Robust losses run throughout. The Huber objective replaces squared loss with absolute loss beyond a tuned threshold $\xi$, and the authors report the Huber variant for OLS, elastic net, GLM and GBRT because it performs better; the Monte Carlo confirms it improves out-of-sample $R^2$ for every method it is applied to.

The evaluation metric carries a decision that changes every number in the paper:

$$R^2_{oos} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{(i,t) \in \mathcal{T}_3} (r_{i,t+1} - \hat{r}_{i,t+1})^2}{\sum_{(i,t) \in \mathcal{T}_3} r_{i,t+1}^2}$$

The denominator is the sum of squared excess returns **without demeaning**, so the benchmark is a forecast of zero rather than the historical mean. The authors justify this on the grounds that the historical mean stock return is so noisy it artificially lowers the bar, and they quantify the difference: benchmarking against historical means raises every method's monthly $R^2$ by roughly three percentage points, with OLS-3 going to 3.74% per month.

For pairwise comparison they adapt Diebold-Mariano, which in its standard form assumes weak error dependence that cross-sectional return data violates. Their fix is to collapse the panel to one time series of cross-sectional average squared-error differences before testing:

$$d_{12,t+1} = \frac{1}{n_3}\sum_{i=1}^{n_3}\left(\left(\hat{e}^{(1)}_{i,t+1}\right)^2 - \left(\hat{e}^{(2)}_{i,t+1}\right)^2\right), \qquad DM_{12} = \bar{d}_{12} / \hat{\sigma}_{\bar{d}_{12}}$$

with $\hat{\sigma}$ a Newey-West standard error. Variable importance is defined as the reduction in predictive $R^2$ from setting one predictor to zero with all other estimates held fixed.

#### Findings

- **The absolute numbers are tiny, and the spread across methods is narrower than the narrative suggests.** Monthly stock-level $R^2_{oos}$: OLS on all 920 features is $-4.60\%$, worse than forecasting zero; OLS-3 recovers to 0.16%; elastic net 0.09%; PLS 0.18%; PCR 0.28%; GLM 0.19%; random forest 0.27%; GBRT 0.30%; and the networks run 0.35% (NN1) to a peak of 0.39% (NN3). So the entire distance from the three-characteristic benchmark to the best model in the paper is 23 basis points of monthly $R^2$. The size split is where the numbers get interesting, NN3 reaching 0.72% on the top 1,000 stocks by market value against 0.46% on the bottom 1,000, and at the annual horizon everything scales up roughly an order of magnitude, NN4 at 3.60% and NN3 at 3.40% across all stocks.

- **The Diebold-Mariano table does not support the method ranking the abstract implies, and the paper is upfront about this itself.** Differences among all the regularized linear models are insignificant, OLS-3, PLS, PCR, elastic net and GLM being statistically indistinguishable from each other. Trees improve on linear models but the improvements are described as at best marginally significant. Neural networks are the only class producing large significant gains over linear and generalized linear models, NN3 against OLS-3 at 3.01, and their improvement over trees is not significant. Among the networks themselves nothing clears, NN3 against NN5 reaching $-1.92$ and NN3 against NN4 only $-1.03$. On this evidence the defensible claim is narrower than "trees and neural nets are the best performing methods": it is that networks beat linear specifications, and that everything else in the ranking is suggestive.
- **Interactions, not nonlinearity, are the mechanism, and the GLM is the clean evidence for it.** The generalized linear model applies spline expansions to every individual characteristic and reaches 0.19%, no better than the linear specifications, despite selecting more features than elastic net. Since the only thing it lacks relative to trees and networks is multi-way interaction, the authors read the gap as attributable to interactions specifically rather than to curvature, and I find this the most convincing single inference in the paper because it is a genuine ablation rather than a comparison across unrelated model families. The marginal-relationship plots show the same thing from another angle, with penalized linear models finding no association at all between returns and either size or volatility while trees and networks find large sensitivity to both, a firm falling from median size to the 20th percentile gaining roughly 2.4% in annualised expected return and a firm whose volatility rises from median to the 80th percentile losing around 3.0% per year under GBRT or NN3.

  ![[Pasted image 20261004120003.png]]
- **Shallow beats deep, and the simulation says why.** Performance peaks at three hidden layers and declines at four and five, random forests settle on trees of one to five layers on average, and GBRT selects fewer than six leaves. The authors attribute this to the comparative dearth of data and the low signal-to-noise ratio relative to settings like computer vision where deep learning thrives. The Monte Carlo makes the trade-off concrete: under a sparse linear data generating process the nonlinear methods lose very little, ENet+H at 4.27% out-of-sample against NN1 at 4.42% and RF at 3.37%, but under a nonlinear interacted process the ordering becomes decisive, GBRT+H and NN2 both at 3.12% against ENet+H at 0.59% and GLM+H at 1.27%, with the oracle at 5.40%. <font color="#ffc000">The asymmetry is the useful part: flexible methods cost almost nothing when the truth is linear and gain a great deal when it is not.</font>
- **Aggregation into portfolios multiplies the measurable signal, and it is where linear methods fail outright.** Bottom-up S&P 500 forecasts built from stock-level predictions give monthly $R^2_{oos}$ of $-0.11\%$ for OLS-3 and $-2.62\%$ for PCR, against 0.86% for GLM and 1.80% for NN3, rising to 15.68% at the annual horizon. The explanation offered is that individual stock returns behave erratically for the smallest and least liquid names, so averaging into portfolios cancels idiosyncratic noise while preserving the signal. Running the Campbell and Thompson mapping from predictive $R^2$ to Sharpe ratio, $SR^\star = \sqrt{(SR^2 + R^2)/(1 - R^2)}$, the S&P 500 buy-and-hold Sharpe of 0.42 becomes 0.63 for an investor timing on NN3 forecasts.
- **Every method converges on the same three signal families, and two of them are the ones I condition on.** Ranking characteristics by summed importance across all eleven models puts short-term reversal, log market equity, twelve-month momentum, momentum change, recent maximum return, industry momentum, return volatility and dollar volume in the top eight. Grouped, that is price trend, liquidity (turnover, turnover volatility, market equity, dollar volume, Amihud illiquidity, zero-trading days, bid-ask spread) and risk (total and idiosyncratic volatility, beta, beta squared), with valuation ratios a distant fourth. Linear and dimension-reduction models concentrate their importance heavily on momentum and reversal, while trees and networks spread it more evenly. On the macro side every method treats aggregate book-to-market as a critical predictor, and it is the single highest-weighted macro variable for the networks and the penalized linear models, 28.57% for NN1 and 43.46% for GLM, while stock variance gets little weight anywhere. The split between families is sharper than the consensus, though, with linear models loading overwhelmingly on default spread and the Treasury rate, 42.13% on default spread for elastic net, while the networks put essentially nothing there, 0.06% for NN3, and load instead on term spread and net equity issuance.

#### Limitations

- **There are no transaction costs anywhere in the paper, and the turnover figures make that decisive rather than cosmetic.** <font color="#ff0000">The best strategy, an equal-weighted decile spread on NN4 forecasts, returns 3.3% per month, 39.0% annualised, at an out-of-sample Sharpe of 2.35, with monthly turnover of 114.08%.</font> Networks run 110.87% to 114.08%, elastic net 143.61% and OLS-3 156.78%, against 22.87% for a size decile spread. The authors note that high turnover is unsurprising given the dominance of price-trend predictors, and then stop. Two things in their own results indicate the size of what is being left out. Value weighting alone, with no costs introduced, drops NN3's Sharpe from 2.32 to 1.25 and OLS-3's from 0.89 to 0.27, so the gap between the paper's two weighting schemes is larger than the gap between most of its methods. And the universe retains sub-$5 stocks by explicit choice. The paper offers the higher top-1,000 $R^2$, 0.72% against 0.46%, as reassurance that machine learning is not merely picking up small-scale inefficiencies driven by illiquidity, but the result carrying the economic claim is an equal-weighted spread over the whole board, and both cannot be load-bearing at once.

  ![[Pasted image 20261004120004.png]]
- **The Monte Carlo summary does not match its own table in the linear case, and that sentence is where the "match the method to the process" reading comes from.** The text states that for the sparse linear model Lasso and ENet deliver the best and almost identical out-of-sample $R^2$, and that RF, NN and GBRT tend to overfit so their performance is slightly worse. Table A.1 shows NN1 at 4.42% and NN2 at 4.40% against ENet+H at 4.27% when $P_c = 50$, and 4.31% and 4.30% against 4.26% when $P_c = 100$, so the networks are ahead in both panels. The trees do underperform as described, and the substantive conclusion about the nonlinear process is untouched, but the specific claim that penalized linear methods win when the truth is linear is contradicted by the numbers printed beneath it.
- **Variable importance by zeroing a predictor is not clean attribution when the predictors are this correlated.** Six of the top eight characteristics are versions of the same price-trend quantity measured over different windows, and setting one of them to zero leaves its correlates free to absorb the signal, which understates each and leaves the group's total importance unallocated. The agreement across models that the paper highlights is therefore partly agreement about the correlation structure of the feature set rather than independent discovery. A second issue is specific to trees, where zeroing a characteristic sends observations to partitions the model never saw during training, so the measured drop mixes the variable's contribution with an off-distribution evaluation. The placebo test with five simulated noise characteristics is a real check and it passes, but it establishes that irrelevant variables rank low, not that the ranking among relevant and correlated variables is meaningful.
- **Complexity is shown to drift over the test period and the drift is explained rather than tested, and no empirical table carries uncertainty.** Figure 3 has elastic net selecting fewer than five features in the first decade of the test sample and 20 to 40 after 2000, PCR using 20 to 40 components, GBRT moving from roughly 20 features to 30, and the explanation offered is a growing cross section and improving data coverage. That is plausible and it is also <font color="#ffc000">asserted rather than tested</font>, and the alternative, that the number of features carrying signal is itself regime-dependent, is the hypothesis my orchestrator rests on and the one this paper was best placed to check. Models are refit once a year with the five architectures fixed throughout, a concession to compute the authors acknowledge, and there are no confidence intervals or seed variation on Tables 1, 2, 5, 7, 8 or 9, only on the 100-repetition simulations. The 60-year sample compounds this, since a model refit annually from 1957 through 2016 averages over an enormous range of market structures, and the conclusion that machine learning is most valuable for larger and more liquid stocks is, read the other way, a statement that it works least well in the conditions a frontier market presents continuously.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **This is the ceiling, and it resets the expectation band for the technical agent.** Thirty thousand stocks, sixty years, 920 engineered features, eleven method families and a thirty-year test window produce 0.39% monthly $R^2_{oos}$ at the stock level. My confirmed data is roughly 300 counters over 374 trading days plus a ten-year daily index track, which is three or four orders of magnitude less information, so <font color="#ff0000">any stock-level monthly $R^2$ much above a few tenths of a percent on CSE data is a prompt to hunt for leakage, not a result.</font> This sits alongside the 0.54 to 0.58 directional-accuracy band I set in [[Extending machine learning prediction capabilities by explainable AI in financial time series prediction]] and the 54.3% out-of-sample figure in [[Deep Learning with Long Short-Term Memory Networks for Financial Market Predictions]], and the three together are now a defensible prior rather than one paper's number.
- **Size the technical agent at NN1 or NN2 scale and stop treating depth as a dial.** Performance peaks at three hidden layers on sixty years of data and declines beyond it, with the authors attributing this directly to data scarcity and low signal-to-noise. On a dataset that is smaller by orders of magnitude the implied architecture is one or two hidden layers, or a regularized tree ensemble, and the depth sweep that would be routine elsewhere is not worth the compute here. The same reasoning applies to feature count, since every method in the paper selected far fewer features than it was offered.
- **Adopt the Huber objective and the publication-lag discipline as defaults, not as refinements.** Huber loss improves out-of-sample performance for every method in the simulation and for four of the methods in the empirical work, and CSE daily returns are heavier-tailed than US monthly returns, so the case is stronger here than there. The lag structure, monthly data delayed one month, quarterly four, annual six, is the specification I should write down for $A_F$ before touching any filing data, since CSE annual reports arrive late and irregularly and a look-ahead bias here would be invisible and fatal.
- **The Kronecker construction is the single-model version of my orchestrator, and saying so sharpens the contribution rather than threatening it.** Gu et al. make every signal's value time-varying by interacting each of 94 characteristics with eight macro state variables inside one estimator, and the beta-pricing derivation is a principled reason to do it. That is conditioning on state, and the fact that it is standard practice in a canonical paper is evidence my premise is sound. What it does not do is the thing I am claiming: the interaction weights are estimated once per annual refit, they are a function of macro variables rather than of any measured reliability of the signal, and no channel in their model can go absent, because a missing characteristic is median-imputed rather than down-weighted. <font color="#ffc000">The difference between imputing a missing input at the cross-sectional median and reducing the weight on the agent that produced it is precisely the orchestration claim</font>, and this paper is the cleanest place to draw that line.
- **Liquidity and volatility are now evidenced as state variables by a 30,000-stock panel, and the macro rejection holds.** Liquidity (turnover, turnover volatility, market equity, dollar volume, Amihud illiquidity, zero-trading days, bid-ask spread) and risk (total volatility, idiosyncratic volatility, beta, beta squared) are two of the three dominant families across every method, which is a far larger evidence base for those two conditioning inputs than the CSE-specific work in [[Role of Market Liquidity in Sentiment-Based Return Predictions, Evidence from Sri Lanka]] or the Granger result at $p = 0.0041$ from volume to ASPI, and it composes with both. Market-level stock variance is given little weight by any method and essentially none by the networks, which is consistent with the thin ARDL result in [[The Long-Term Impact of Macroeconomic Stability Variables on Stock Price Volatility]] that already got the macro block rejected. The one genuine challenge is aggregate book-to-market, which every model treats as critical and which tops the macro ranking for the networks and the penalized linear models, and I should test it against an ASPI-level valuation ratio rather than dismiss it on the strength of a rejection aimed at different variables.
- **Fundamentals being weak at monthly and stronger at annual horizons is a design constraint on the fundamental agent.** Accounting characteristics cluster at the bottom of the importance ranking and the authors attribute this partly to their low update frequency, noting that at the annual horizon price trend variables lose ground to liquidity and risk measures while industry becomes substantially predictive. Read against the abstention argument, this says the fundamental agent is not a timing input and should not be asked for one, it is a slow-moving tilt whose natural horizon is quarters, and an orchestrator that weights it on a daily decision will be weighting noise most of the time. Combined with my working assumption that this channel is unusable more than half the time on the CSE, the honest design is a low baseline weight with an explicit abstention path rather than a weight that happens to fall to zero.
- **Copy the evaluation protocol in full, and state the benchmark every time.** Recursive expanding training window, fixed-size rolling validation, annual refit, no cross-validation, modified Diebold-Mariano on cross-sectionally averaged error differences with Newey-West standard errors, and $R^2_{oos}$ benchmarked against zero with the choice stated. That last point is the one that will bite: the three-percentage-point inflation from benchmarking against the historical mean is larger than any effect I am likely to measure, so a CSE result reported without naming its benchmark is uninterpretable. Reporting the Campbell-Thompson Sharpe mapping alongside the $R^2$ is cheap and converts a statistical number into the economic one the evaluation actually cares about, with the caveat that it assumes costless timing and will therefore be optimistic on a thin book.
- **This is the strongest available illustration of why cost belongs inside the objective rather than after it.** A strategy turning over 113% of its book monthly, concentrated in price-trend signals, on a universe that deliberately includes the smallest and least liquid names, reported at Sharpe 2.35 with no cost deduction and no participation-rate analysis, is the reference case for my cost sub-gap. The value-weighted collapse to 1.25 is itself a partial, unacknowledged cost estimate and I should cite it that way. On the CSE, where Dissanayake and Nanayakkara find unexpected illiquidity shocks depressing returns by $-0.084$ to $-0.097$ and small caps more than large, that turnover is not implementable at any size, which is the concrete argument for sweeping participation rate against daily volume rather than charging a flat spread, as [[Online portfolio selection with state-dependent price estimators and transaction costs]] and [[A reinforcement learning approach to optimal execution]] both do in their different ways.
