NeurIPS 2021, both authors in Stanford statistics, Candès also in mathematics, and this is the paper that introduced <font color="#ffff00">adaptive conformal inference</font> (ACI). The object is narrow and the claim is unusually clean for a methods paper. Standard conformal prediction wraps any black box regressor in a prediction set with a marginal coverage guarantee, but the guarantee rests on the calibration data and the test point being exchangeable, which no financial time series satisfies. ACI drops that assumption by turning miscoverage into a control problem: it carries a single scalar $\alpha_t$, compares the realised coverage of yesterday's interval against the target, and nudges $\alpha_t$ up or down by a fixed step. <font color="#ffc000">The result is that the long-run empirical coverage frequency converges to the target for any data generating process whatsoever, with no assumptions at all, and the bound on the finite-sample deviation is $\left(\max\{\alpha_1, 1-\alpha_1\} + \gamma\right)/(T\gamma)$, which is distribution-free and shrinks as $1/T$.</font> They demonstrate it on daily GARCH volatility prediction for twelve stocks and on county-level election night forecasting.

This is tooling rather than a competitor architecture, there are no agents here, nothing resembling an orchestrator and no trading decision anywhere in it, but I'm giving it a full note because it speaks directly to two things that are currently under-specified in my design. The first is $C_{i,t}$, which the scope revision redefined as a structural property of an agent's output rather than a self-report, and for $A_V$ a calibrated interval width is exactly such a property, measured rather than asked for. The second is the distribution-shift detection item, which sits in my plan as a pre-registered test with no method attached to it. ACI is a candidate method, and it has the useful property of not being a detector at all: $\alpha_t$ moves continuously, so the shift measurement and the response to it are the same quantity, which is a better fit for a weighting function than a binary alarm. It also belongs next to [[Predict the Future from the Past, On the Temporal Data Distribution Shift in Financial Sentiment Classifications]] as the other half of the shift problem, that note measuring how much a fitted model decays over time and this one giving a mechanism that stays calibrated while it does.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **Every conformal method before this one assumes exchangeability, and the authors are precise about what that buys and what it costs.** The split conformal construction fits a score $S(X,Y)$ on a training set, computes its $1-\alpha$ empirical quantile $\hat{Q}(1-\alpha)$ on a held-out calibration set, and returns $\hat{C}_t = \{y : S(X_t, y) \leq \hat{Q}(1-\alpha)\}$. The guarantee follows from the rank of the test score being uniform among the calibration scores, which is true if and only if the points are exchangeable. The specific failure they name in finance is that market behaviour shifts in response to legislation or major world events, which breaks exchangeability in exactly the periods where a coverage statement would matter most.
- **The two prior treatments of shift both handle a single train-to-test transition, not a continuously moving target.** Tibshirani et al. reweight the calibration data by the likelihood ratio $P_1(X)/P_0(X)$, which requires $Y \mid X$ to be constant between training and test and the ratio itself to be known or accurately estimated, neither of which holds for a market regime change. Cauchois et al. form sets valid whenever $D_f(P_1 \| P_0) \leq \rho$ by fixing a conservative $\alpha_\rho$ in advance. The two differences Gibbs and Candès draw are the ones that matter: they estimate the optimal $\alpha^*$ satisfying $M(\alpha^*) = \alpha$ rather than fixing a conservative value, and they work in a fully online setting where the distribution moves at every step.
- **The reframing is that the shift becomes a one-dimensional learning problem.** Rather than modelling how $P(X_t, Y_t)$ changes, which is hopeless, they define the realised miscoverage $M_t(\alpha) := \mathbb{P}(S_t(X_t,Y_t) > \hat{Q}_t(1-\alpha))$ and the optimal $\alpha_t^* := \sup\{\beta \in [0,1] : M_t(\beta) \leq \alpha\}$, then track $\alpha_t^*$ online. Everything about the data generating process is compressed into the movement of a single parameter, which is what makes the distribution-free result possible and is also, as I argue in limitations, what the result is silent about.

The premise doing the quiet work throughout is that correcting the *argument* to the quantile function is an adequate substitute for correcting the quantile function itself. The paper is upfront that this is a modelling choice rather than a theorem, so I deal with it once, in limitations.

#### Data and setup

Two experiments, and the volatility one is the one I'd be reimplementing.

| Component | Setting |
| --- | --- |
| Target | realised volatility $V_t = R_t^2$, $R_t = (P_t - P_{t-1})/P_{t-1}$ |
| Inputs | daily open prices from publicly available WSJ datasets |
| Point predictor | GARCH(1,1), $\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \tau V_{t-1} + \beta\sigma_{t-1}^2$ |
| Refit window | trailing 1250 trading days, roughly 5 years, refit at every $t > 1250$ |
| Target coverage | $1 - \alpha = 0.9$, initialised $\alpha_{1250} = 0.1$ |
| Step size | $\gamma = 0.005$, fixed, chosen for stable trajectories |
| Calibration | the same 1250-day trailing window of conformity scores |
| Comparators | fixed $\alpha_t = \alpha$ on the same rolling window, plus an i.i.d. Bernoulli(0.1) sequence |
| Coverage metric | `localCov`, the average coverage over the most recent 500 days, roughly two years |
| Universe | 12 stocks and indices, 4 in the main figure, 8 more in the appendix |

The second experiment replicates the Washington Post's 2020 election night model, conformalised quantile regression over 3111 matched US counties, predictions starting once 500 counties have reported, each step splitting the observed data 75/25 into training and calibration and refitting a linear quantile regression. The shift is manufactured but realistic: counties are ordered by time zone, east coast first and Hawaii last, reproducing the reporting bias of a real election night and making the covariate distribution drift monotonically rather than jump.

> [!NOTE]
> **Why the Bernoulli comparator is the right benchmark and not a strawman.** A perfectly calibrated method still produces a random coverage sequence, so a local coverage curve that wanders around 0.9 is not evidence of failure. The grey line is the moving average of an i.i.d. Bernoulli(0.1) sequence and shows how much wandering is expected from a method that is correct by construction. The test is therefore whether the adaptive curve stays inside that band, not whether it sits on 0.9, a more honest bar than most calibration plots set.

> [!WARNING]
> **The 1250-day refit window is a hard constraint on transferring this, not a tuning detail.** Five years of daily data per counter is roughly thirteen times what my per-counter archive holds at 374 trading days, and the calibration set is drawn from the same window, so shrinking it degrades the GARCH fit and the empirical quantile simultaneously. The index track at ten years daily clears it comfortably; the per-counter track does not.

#### Model architecture and training

There is no architecture, which is the point. ACI is a wrapper and the algorithm is one line. Define the miscoverage event $\text{err}_t := \mathbb{1}\{Y_t \notin \hat{C}_t(\alpha_t)\}$, then update:

$$\alpha_{t+1} := \alpha_t + \gamma(\alpha - \text{err}_t)$$

where $\alpha$ is the target miscoverage rate, $\text{err}_t$ is the realised miscoverage at step $t$ taking value 0 or 1, and $\gamma > 0$ is the step size. When the interval covered, $\alpha_{t+1}$ rises by $\gamma\alpha$ and the next interval narrows; when it missed, $\alpha_{t+1}$ falls by $\gamma(1-\alpha)$ and the next interval widens. The appendix shows this is online gradient descent on the pinball loss $\rho_\alpha(\beta_t - \alpha_t)$, though they note that standard regret bounds don't capture what they want, because regret is measured against a fixed comparator and $\alpha_t^*$ is a moving target. They also give a weighted variant replacing $\text{err}_t$ with $\sum_s w_s \text{err}_s$ for weights $w_s \propto 0.95^{t-s}$, which produces almost identical trajectories, only smoother.

The guarantee that costs nothing is the one I'd cite. Proposition 4.1 states that with probability one, for all $T$:

$$\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\text{err}_t - \alpha\right| \leq \frac{\max\{\alpha_1, 1-\alpha_1\} + \gamma}{T\gamma}$$

where $\alpha_1$ is the initialisation and the bound follows from Lemma 4.1, that $\alpha_t$ stays in $[-\gamma, 1+\gamma]$ for all $t$. The proof is three lines of rearranging the recursion and places <font color="#ffc000">no constraint whatsoever on the data generating distribution</font>, which is a strong statement and also, as the bound's form makes clear, a statement about the time average rather than about any single $t$.

The conformity score for the volatility experiment is normalised by the predicted variance, and this choice turns out to carry as much weight as the adaptation itself:

$$S_t := \frac{\left|V_t - (\hat{\sigma}_t^t)^2\right|}{(\hat{\sigma}_t^t)^2}$$

where the superscript is the re-fitting index, so $(\hat{\sigma}_t^t)^2$ is the GARCH point prediction from a model fitted using only data in $[t-1250, t)$ and the score at each step has seen nothing after $t-1$.

The sharper results come at a cost in assumptions. Theorems 4.1 and 4.2 model the environment as a hidden Markov chain $\{A_t\}$ with non-zero absolute spectral gap $1 - \eta$, assume $(\alpha_t, A_t)$ is stationary and that $\hat{Q}_t(\cdot)$ and $S_t(\cdot)$ don't depend on $t$, and then give a Bernstein-type concentration bound on $|T^{-1}\sum \text{err}_t - \alpha|$ and an $L$-Lipschitz bound on $\mathbb{E}[(M(\alpha_t|A_t) - \alpha)^2]$ of the form $\frac{L(1+\gamma)}{\gamma}\mathbb{E}[|\alpha^*_{A_{t+1}} - \alpha^*_{A_t}|] + \frac{L}{2}\gamma$. The practically useful fallout is the step-size prescription: that bound is minimised at $\gamma = (2\mathbb{E}[|\alpha^*_{A_{t+1}} - \alpha^*_{A_t}|])^{1/2}$, so $\gamma$ should scale with the square root of the typical one-step movement in the optimal miscoverage level, larger in more volatile environments. An R implementation is at `github.com/isgibbs/AdaptiveConformal`.

#### Findings

- **The non-adaptive method fails visibly and the adaptive one stays inside the Bernoulli band on all twelve names, with the 2008 crisis producing the clearest separation.** In the Fannie Mae panel the fixed-$\alpha$ local coverage falls to roughly 0.83 against a target of 0.9 and stays depressed for most of 2008 and 2009, while the adaptive curve dips and recovers within the range the Bernoulli comparator occupies. BlackBerry shows the same shape in 2008, AMD a fixed-$\alpha$ trough near 0.835 around 2016 and an over-covering excursion to 0.96 in 2004, Nvidia a trough near 0.85 across 2016 and 2017. <font color="#ffc000">The useful reading is that the failure is two-sided, the fixed method over-covers as often as it under-covers, so what's being fixed is calibration drift rather than a conservatism bug.</font> The four stocks shown were selected out of twelve because they showed a clear failure of the non-adaptive method, which the authors state plainly, so the main figure is a demonstration of the failure mode rather than a sample of typical behaviour, and the eight appendix panels are the ones to read for that.

  ![[Pasted image 20261004190001.png]]

- **The adaptation is substantial rather than cosmetic, $\alpha_t$ moving across roughly a fourfold range, and the trajectories read as a volatility-regime series in their own right.** Across the four stocks $\alpha_t$ ranges between about 0.035 and 0.17 against a target of 0.1, so at its extremes the method is targeting 96.5% coverage in some periods and 83% in others to achieve 90% on average. The troughs line up with crises, Fannie Mae and BlackBerry both bottoming out near 0.035 through 2008 and 2009, which is the method widening its intervals hard when the GARCH fit is worst. At $\gamma = 0.005$ a single miss moves $\alpha_t$ by 0.0045, so these swings take hundreds of days to develop, and the series is slow-moving by construction in the same way $d$ is in [[Modeling Long-Term Volatility Memory Dynamics in the Colombo Stock Exchange]].

  ![[Pasted image 20261004190003.png]]

- **The theory splits cleanly into one assumption-free result about time averages and one assumption-heavy result about single time steps, and the authors are careful about which is which.** Proposition 4.1 holds for any data generating process and is shown to be approximately tight, in that replacing $\max\{1-\alpha_1, \alpha_1\}$ with $|\alpha_1 - \alpha^*|$ under a noiseless idealisation recovers nearly the same bound, so it is simultaneously agnostic and close to the best possible given a bad initialisation. Everything sharper needs the stationary HMM. The appendix comparison is the honest part: the naive bound decays as $O(1/(T\sqrt{\epsilon_1}))$ and therefore *increases* as the shift size $\epsilon_1$ shrinks, which they call undesirable, while the HMM-based bound decreases with the shift. Both are reported side by side rather than only the flattering one.
- **The conformity score design controls the result about as much as the adaptation does.** Replacing the normalised score with the unnormalised $\tilde{S}_t := |V_t - \hat{\sigma}_t^2|$ produces much wider swings in local coverage around 0.9 for the adaptive method, visibly outside the Bernoulli band and dropping below 0.6 on AMD and Fannie Mae in the crisis windows, against the normalised version which stays within a few percentage points throughout. The authors' explanation is that normalising by $\hat{\sigma}_t^2$ is what makes the score approximately stationary, and they note the issue can be partially mitigated by a larger $\gamma$. <font color="#ffc000">So "works with any black box" is true of the interface and not of the performance, and the real requirement is a score whose distribution moves slowly.</font>

  ![[Pasted image 20261004190002.png]]

- **The election night experiment shows the method handling a monotone covariate drift that isn't a crisis, which is the more general case.** Ordering roughly 3000 counties by time zone makes the covariate distribution drift steadily rather than jump, and the fixed-$\alpha$ method incurs large troughs in local coverage at the time zone changes, falling to about 0.825 and spending most of the sequence below 0.875, while the adaptive method holds near 0.9 with deviations comparable to the Bernoulli sequence. This matters more to me than the volatility panels do, because the shift is structural and predictable from the data ordering rather than driven by an exogenous shock, which is the shape of a cross-sectional screening problem.

#### Limitations

- **The guarantee is about coverage and says nothing about width, and no width or efficiency number appears anywhere in the paper.** Proposition 4.1 is satisfied by a sequence that alternates between infinite and empty prediction sets in the right proportion, and the mechanism that makes it assumption-free is precisely the one that permits this: when $\alpha_t < 0$ the set is all of $\mathbb{R}$ and $\text{err}_t = 0$ by construction, which is how the proof of Lemma 4.1 gets its contradiction. <font color="#ff0000">There is no table of average interval widths, no comparison of width against the fixed-$\alpha$ method, and no statement of how often $\alpha_t$ goes non-positive in the experiments.</font> The $\alpha_t$ trajectories suggest it doesn't happen on these twelve stocks, the minimum sits near 0.035, but that's inference from a plot rather than a reported quantity, and a method sold on robustness to crises should report what its intervals cost in the crisis. I have not found that evaluation in the literature I have covered.
- **The results that hold at a single time step require a small shift, a stationary chain and a time-invariant quantile function, which is close to the regime where I wouldn't need the method.** Theorem 4.2 bounds the expected squared deviation of marginal coverage by a quantity proportional to $\mathbb{E}[|\alpha^*_{A_{t+1}} - \alpha^*_{A_t}|]$, which the authors interpret as a measurement of shift size, so the bound is informative exactly when the shift is small and vacuous when it is large. The discussion concedes the fixed-$\hat{Q}_t$ assumption directly and says it would be interesting to determine if similar results hold when $\hat{Q}_t$ is fit online on recent data, which is what the volatility experiment actually does. So the headline experiment sits outside the conditions of the sharper theory, and the only guarantee covering it is the long-run average one.
- **$\gamma = 0.005$ is set once, justified in a sentence, and not varied anywhere in the paper.** The stated reason is that it gave relatively stable trajectories while still allowing adaptation, which is a reasonable thing to say and not a sensitivity analysis. The theory does give a prescription, $\gamma \propto \sqrt{\mathbb{E}|\Delta\alpha^*|}$, but that requires knowing the typical size of the shift in the optimal parameter, which is the thing you are using the method to track, and the discussion names adaptive selection of $\gamma$ without prior knowledge as an open problem. For a user with a short sample this is the live question, since the time to recover from a bad initialisation scales as $1/\gamma$.
- **Two domains, both with immediate feedback, and no decision-level evaluation of any kind.** $Y_t$ has to be revealed at every step for the update to run, and the discussion flags delayed or batched responses as outside the method. Quantile loss and local coverage are the only metrics; there is no comparison against a simpler recalibration scheme beyond fixed-$\alpha$ on the same rolling window, no cost attached to an interval that's too wide, and nothing connecting calibration to a downstream decision. That's a fair scope for a statistics paper and a gap for anyone using it inside a trading system, where an interval has a position size attached.
- **The core modelling choice, adjusting the argument to $\hat{Q}_t$ rather than $\hat{Q}_t$ itself, is asserted as adequate rather than tested.** The paper is explicit that in general both $S_t(\cdot)$ and $\hat{Q}_t(\cdot)$ should be re-estimated to track recent observations, and then proceeds to fix the quantile function and correct only $\alpha_t$. The Figure 2 result is the visible cost of that choice, since a score whose distribution shifts faster than $\alpha_t$ can follow produces exactly the wide coverage swings observed. The design burden this pushes onto the user is real and under-acknowledged: picking a score that stays approximately stationary under regime change is a modelling problem of the same difficulty as the one the wrapper was meant to abstract away.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **This gives the distribution-shift item a concrete method, and a better one than a detector, because $\alpha_t$ is a continuous state variable rather than an alarm.** The pre-registered test currently has no method attached. Running ACI over $A_V$'s volatility forecast produces a series whose movement measures how badly the fitted model is tracking the market right now, on the same daily index the orchestrator runs on, and with no threshold to hand-tune. The concrete proposal is to carry $\alpha_t$ as a candidate addition to the conditioning set and test it on the ablation protocol against the existing four terms rather than adopting it, which is also the discipline [[Predicting financial market stress with machine learning]] makes a case for after what 44 predictors on 84 observations did to that paper's out-of-sample performance.
- **For $A_V$, the calibrated interval width is the structural confidence term the scope revision asked for, and it arrives measured rather than self-reported.** The redefinition of $C_{i,t}$ gave a worked example for $A_F$ (does the extracted program parse, does every argument trace to a retrieved figure) and left the other three agents open. The analogue for $A_V$ is now available: wrap the FIGARCH or GJR-GARCH forecast in ACI with the normalised score, and let $C_{V,t}$ be a function of the realised interval width at the target coverage, so a volatility agent whose model has drifted reports wider intervals and is automatically down-weighted. That's a measured property of the output, which is precisely what the redefinition demanded.
- **The abstention channel gets a principled trigger out of this instead of a hand-set threshold.** The design treats abstention as structural because $A_F$ is expected to be unusable more than half the time on CSE filings, and the same machinery works for a price-derived agent: when $\alpha_t$ falls low enough that the interval at the target coverage spans the plausible range of next-day volatility, the agent has nothing to say, and the orchestrator should see that as absence rather than as a weak signal. This is the mechanism AlphaAgents lacked when a human deleted an agent by hand, expressed as a quantity that moves on its own.
- **The 1250-day refit window is the sharpest data-depth constraint I've hit in a review and it splits my two tracks.** Five years of daily data per counter, with the calibration scores drawn from the same window, against 374 trading days per counter in the archive. On the index track at ten years daily the recipe transfers as written; on the per-counter track it does not, and the honest options are a shorter joint window with the calibration degradation reported, or pooling scores across counters, which breaks the per-counter conditioning the method relies on. This belongs in the data-depth discussion as a named requirement with a number attached, not as a general concern.
- **Step size has to be re-derived for a short sample, and the weighted update is the better starting point here.** Their $\gamma = 0.005$ is calibrated to fifteen-plus years of daily data where a multi-hundred-day recovery from a bad initialisation costs nothing. On 374 observations a step that small means $\alpha_t$ barely leaves its initialisation, so the sample would be spent in the transient the theory warns about, and the prescription $\gamma \propto \sqrt{\mathbb{E}|\Delta\alpha^*|}$ points the same way given the CSE's crisis sequence. The experiment is a $\gamma$ sweep on the index track where I have the length to measure it, then carry the chosen value down to the counters.
- **The score-design finding sets the real work, and the thin-book analogue of normalising by $\hat{\sigma}_t^2$ is an open question I should test rather than assume.** Normalising by predicted variance is what made their score approximately stationary; on the CSE, where zero-return days and stale quotes are common, a variance-normalised score may still be non-stationary because the denominator is itself unreliable when the counter didn't trade. The candidate is to normalise by a liquidity-adjusted volatility estimate, using the same Amihud and turnover inputs already being computed for the cost model and for the composite liquidity state, so one measurement serves a third purpose. That's a named experiment, not a design decision.
- **The coverage-without-width gap is a trap I'd fall into in exactly the same way, so the evaluation has to charge for width from the start.** A volatility agent that achieves nominal coverage by widening its intervals during crises is well calibrated and useless for position sizing, which is the same failure as an orchestrator whose weights move impressively in the two or three most volatile CSE months and are inert otherwise. The commitment is that any calibration result reports average interval width alongside coverage, and reports both over quiet sub-periods separately from crisis windows, which is consistent with how the friction-aware evaluation already splits its reporting.
- **Claim this as an adopted component and not as a contribution, and say so before anyone asks.** ACI is an off-the-shelf wrapper with a public R implementation, four years old and widely cited, and there is nothing novel in applying it to a GARCH forecast since that is the paper's own worked example. What would be a candidate technical contribution not identified in the literature I have covered is using the adapted miscoverage level as an input to a weighting function over heterogeneous agents, which is a different claim and a much narrower one, and it needs validating against recent work before it goes anywhere near the novelty statement.
