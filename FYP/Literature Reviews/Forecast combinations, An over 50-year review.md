*International Journal of Forecasting* 39(4), 1518–1547, with the arXiv version (2205.04216v2) dated 26 September 2022. Xiaoqian Wang and Yanfei Kang at Beihang, Rob J Hyndman at Monash, Feng Li at the Central University of Finance and Economics, which is about as authoritative an author list as this particular topic has. It is a narrative review of over fifty years of work on combining multiple forecasts of the same quantity, running from Bates and Granger (1969), and behind that from Galton's 1906 ox-weight competition where the average of 787 estimates came close to the animal's actual weight, through to the feature-based cross-learning methods that placed in the M4 competition. The organising split is point forecast combinations in section 2 and probabilistic forecast combinations in section 3, with a closing section listing eight open problems and a running commentary on which R and Python packages implement what. <font color="#ffc000">The central empirical claim of the whole field, restated here across fifty years of evidence, is that the equally weighted average is extremely hard to beat</font>, and the literature has a name for its own embarrassment about this, the <font color="#ffff00">forecast combination puzzle</font>.

I'm writing a full note on a paper with no architecture in it, no trading decision anywhere, and nothing resembling an agent, because it is the most direct body of prior art on the half of my design I have covered least. My orchestrator computes $w_{i,t} = f(L_t, V_t, P_t, C_{i,t})$ over four heterogeneous agent outputs, and that object is a time-varying, feature-conditioned linear combination, which is a named construct in this literature with five decades of results attached to it, a good number of them inconvenient. The gap I'm defending is that existing multi-agent trading systems have no quantity that could be weighted, and that gap only means something if weighting is worth doing once the quantity exists, which is exactly the question this paper spends forty pages on. It also hands me the hardest available version of my own equal-weight baseline, a vocabulary for positioning the weighting function as a generalisation rather than an invention, and two open problems in its future-directions section that my design happens to sit on top of. This belongs next to [[The Evolution of Reinforcement Learning in Quantitative Finance, A Survey]] as the other half of the prior art: that one establishes that coordination protocols among heterogeneous agents are underexplored in RL, this one establishes what fifty years of statistics already knows about combining their outputs once they exist.

> [!important] What this survey can and cannot be cited for
> The scope statement is explicit and it bounds everything below. The authors take the individual forecasts <font color="#ffc000">as given</font>, do not discuss how they are generated, and restrict attention to forecasts derived from *separate* and *non-interfering* models for a single target series. Two adjacent literatures are named and excluded: manipulating one series into many and combining the results, which Petropoulos and Spiliotis call the wisdom of the data and which covers theta, bagging and temporal aggregation, and forecast reconciliation for hierarchical series following Hyndman et al. (2011). The target is always a realised value of one series and the loss is almost always quadratic. There are no portfolios, no transaction costs, no execution, and no decisions. So this is prior art on the weighting mechanism and on the evidence about weighting, not on anything downstream of it, and the non-interfering assumption in particular is one my design may violate.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **The two standing reviews of this literature are from 1989 and 2006, and the field moved underneath them.** Clemen (1989) and Timmermann (2006) are cited as the extensive earlier reviews, and both predate the entire cross-learning and meta-learning strand, the M4 and M5 competitions, most of the probabilistic combination work, and the open-source tooling that now carries these methods into practice. The authors' own Web of Science count puts combination papers at <font color="#ffc000">13.80% of all published forecasting papers in 2021</font>, up from roughly 1% in 1969, so the case for an update is quantitative rather than rhetorical.

![[Pasted image 20261004190000.png]]

- **Point and probabilistic combination had developed in parallel without being contrasted.** Work after Bates and Granger concentrated on point forecasts; attention moved to probabilistic forecasts more recently, where diversity among the individual forecasts becomes, in the authors' phrasing, more complex and less understood, and where calibration and sharpness enter as considerations that have no analogue in the point case. Setting the two side by side is the structural contribution, and it is what produces the review's most useful result for me, because several intuitions do not survive the crossing.
- **Model averaging and forecast combination get used interchangeably and shouldn't be.** The distinction the paper draws is that model averaging is a general term for handling model uncertainty, chiefly in parameter estimation, with frequentist and Bayesian strands, while forecast combination is narrower, operating on forecasts whose origin is irrelevant, and carrying assumptions on those forecasts intended to keep the combination unbiased or optimal. That is the right frame for my case, because my agents are not models of the same process with different parameters, they are methodologically different producers, and only the second framing accommodates that.

#### Coverage and scope

There is no protocol, no screening count, and no stated inclusion criterion anywhere in the paper, which I take up in limitations. What there is instead is an organised ladder of weighting schemes running from no estimation at all to estimation over a whole dataset of series, and that ladder is the piece I actually need, because it tells me where a state-conditioned weighting function sits relative to everything that already exists.

| Family | What determines the weights | Data needed to fit them | Section |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| Optimal weights | Covariance matrix of the $h$-step forecast errors | Error history per method, all pairs | 2.2 |

| Rank-based | Position in the performance ordering, not the error magnitude | Error history per method | 2.2 |

The last row is the one that matters for me and it is worth stating what it buys. Stacking in the series-by-series mode needs $n$ meta-models for $n$ series, which the authors note is computationally expensive and wastes the training data. The cross-learning mode trains one meta-model across many series instead, and the M4 competition is the evidence: <font color="#ffc000">the top three methods all used information across the whole dataset rather than a single series</font>. <font color="#ffff00">FFORMA</font> (Montero-Manso et al., 2020) is the exemplar, 42 statistical features extracted with **tsfeatures**, an XGBoost meta-model mapping those features to weights over nine traditional models fitted per series, second place in M4. Collopy and Armstrong (1992) is the ancestor, a hand-built rule base of 99 rules over 18 time series features and four statistical models, and the line from there to XGBoost over 42 features is the clearest trajectory in the paper.

#### How the weights get determined

The framing that holds all of section 2 together is a one-dimensional aggregator over an $N$-vector of $h$-step-ahead forecasts, $\tilde{y}_{T+h|T} = C(\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_{T+h|T}; \boldsymbol{w}_{T+h|T})$, which in the linear case is just $\tilde{y}_{T+h|T} = \boldsymbol{w}'_{T+h|T}\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_{T+h|T}$. Everything else in the section is an answer to how $\boldsymbol{w}$ is obtained, and the four answers I'd actually reimplement are below.

The original answer, from Bates and Granger (1969) for pairs and Newbold and Granger (1974) in general, minimises the variance of the combined forecast error, and the first-order condition gives a closed form:

$$\boldsymbol{w}^{\text{opt}}_{T+h|T} = \frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_{T+h|T}\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{1}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_{T+h|T}\mathbf{1}}$$

where $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{T+h|T}$ is the $N \times N$ covariance matrix of the $h$-step forecast errors and $\mathbf{1}$ is a unit vector. If the individual forecasts are unbiased and their error variances are stable, the combination is unbiased too, and its error variance is no greater than the minimum error variance across the individual forecasts, which is the diversification result the whole field rests on. The catch is in the last sentence of that passage: <font color="#ff0000">in practice $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ is unknown and has to be estimated</font>, and that single sentence generates most of the rest of the literature.

Granger and Ramanathan (1984) reframed the problem as a regression of realisations on past forecasts under three restriction regimes: weights summing to one with no intercept, which reproduces the Bates and Granger weights exactly; unconstrained weights with no intercept; and unconstrained weights with an intercept, which they argued is superior regardless of whether the constituents are biased, because the intercept absorbs the bias. De Menezes et al. (2000) push back that the unrestricted version needs stationarity and runs into serial correlation and multicollinearity, and Aksu and Gunter (1992) found empirically that constraining the weights to be nonnegative is at least as robust as the simple average while beating the other regression variants, which is the practical reading I'd take.

The schemes I'd implement first are the performance-based ones, because they need no pairwise estimation at all. Of the five that Bates and Granger proposed and the authors restate in general form, the one with a time dimension is the discounted variant:

$$w^{\text{bg4}}_{T+h|T,i} = \frac{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma^{t}e^{2}_{t|t-h,i}\right)^{-1}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma^{t}e^{2}_{t|t-h,j}\right)^{-1}}, \qquad \gamma \geq 1$$

where $e_{t|t-h,i}$ is the $h$-step error of method $i$ made at $t-h$ and realised at $t$, and $\gamma$ controls how fast older errors are forgotten, with $\gamma = 1$ recovering the plain inverse-MSE weight over the full history. The rolling-window variant replaces the discount with a sum over the most recent $\nu$ observations. Both are adapted to non-stationary relationships between methods, which is precisely the case they exist for, and both, as Timmermann (2006) is quoted saying, increase the variance of the parameter estimates and work quite poorly if the data generating process really is covariance stationary. Stock and Watson (1998) generalise the family by making weights inversely proportional to the $k$th power of MSE, so that $k=0$ is equal weights and larger $k$ concentrates on the best performer, which gives a single interpretable dial between the two extremes.

Time variation enters in a second, more structural way. Diebold and Pauly (1987) use rolling weighted least squares and time-varying parameters that are either deterministic polynomial functions of time or stochastic, Deutsch et al. (1994) let the weights switch abruptly or transition smoothly using switching and smooth transition regression, and Terui and van Dijk (2002) model the coefficients as a random walk in a state space form estimated by the Kalman filter, with Raftery et al. (2010) adding forgetting factors to make the recursive update cheap. <font color="#ffc000">So the literature already contains weights that change over time, and it contains weights that change as a regression function of covariates, and what it does not contain, anywhere in this review, is weights that are a function of measured exogenous market state</font>. The closest thing is the smooth transition specification of Deutsch et al., where the transition variable could in principle be exogenous, and FFORMA, where the features are properties of the series itself computed once rather than a state measured at each step.

#### Probabilistic combination, and where the point-forecast intuitions reverse

The simplest probabilistic scheme is the linear opinion pool, a finite mixture $\tilde{F}(y_{T+h}|I_T) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_{T+h|T,i}F_i(y_{T+h}|I_T)$ with nonnegative weights summing to one. Its first two moments are where the trouble is:

$$\tilde{\mu} = \sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i\mu_i \qquad \text{and} \qquad \tilde{\sigma}^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i\sigma_i^2 + \sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i\left(\mu_i - \tilde{\mu}\right)^2$$

The mean of the pooled distribution is exactly the linear point combination, so nothing changes there. The variance is not: it carries a second term in the *disagreement* between the component means, so the pooled variance exceeds the weighted average of component variances whenever the components disagree about location. <font color="#ff0000">The consequence is that seeking diverse forecasts, which is the standard advice for point combination, harms the probabilistic forecast by destroying sharpness and making the mixture under-confident</font>, and Ranjan and Gneiting (2010) show the pool of individually calibrated forecasts is generally not calibrated. Hora (2004) gives the countervailing case, that pooling helps calibration when the components are overconfident, and Jose et al. (2014) narrow it, that overconfident components with low diversity stay overconfident. Their response is the trimmed opinion pool, where exterior trimming removes components with extreme means to cut variance and address under-confidence, and interior trimming removes the moderate ones to raise variance and address overconfidence.

Quantile combination is the alternative and it is one line, $\tilde{Q}_{T+h|T}(\tau) = \sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{T+h|T,i}Q_{T+h|T,i}(\tau)$ for $0 < \tau \leq 1$, known as Vincentization after Vincent (1912). The geometric statement of the difference is the clearest sentence in section 3: <font color="#ffff00">quantile combination averages the individuals' cdfs horizontally while linear pooling averages them vertically</font>. The properties follow from that. If the components are in the same location-scale family the quantile average stays in it, so averaging normals gives a normal and is always unimodal, where linear pooling can be multimodal. Both share the same mean. And quantile averaging is sharper, because it does not absorb the disagreement in location as extra spread, which is why Lichtendahl et al. (2013) and Busetti (2017) both come down on its side, the former arguing the average probability forecast is generally under-confident while the average quantile forecast is always sharper.

The rest of section 3 is a tour of ways to repair the linear pool's calibration, generalised, spread-adjusted and beta-transformed pools, Bayesian nonparametric beta mixtures, Bayesian model averaging, and dynamic Bayesian predictive synthesis, and the finding that matters is negative and comes from Baran and Lerch (2018), who compared state-of-the-art combination methods in case studies and found <font color="#ffc000">no substantial difference in forecast performance between the simple linear pool and the theoretically superior but cumbersome nonlinear pooling approaches</font>. That is the probabilistic restatement of the puzzle, which makes it two independent appearances of the same pattern in one paper.

#### Findings

- **The forecast combination puzzle is the field's central empirical result and it is fifty years old and still open.** Stock and Watson (2004) named it: theoretically sophisticated weighting schemes should dominate the simple average, and empirically the simple average dominates them. The evidence runs from Makridakis et al. (1982) in the original M-competition through to M4, where <font color="#ffc000">a simple equal-weight combination ranked third for yearly series and a median of four simple models took sixth place overall for point forecasting</font>. Gastinger et al. (2021) is the most recent restatement across M3, M4, M5 and FRED, finding that the winning combination method differs by data source while simple averaging shows, on average, more gain than complex methods. The explanations all centre on estimation error. Timmermann (2006) attributes it to parameter estimation error that simple schemes avoid entirely, Smith and Wallis (2009) to finite-sample estimation effects, Claeskens et al. (2016) prove that treating the weights as random rather than fixed can introduce bias into combinations of unbiased components and inflate the combination's variance past the fixed-weight case, Blanc and Setzer (2020) frame it as a bias-variance tradeoff where equal weights underfit and estimated weights carry estimation variance, and Elliott (2011) bounds the available gain from optimal over equal weights and finds it is usually too small to pay for the estimation.
- **Correlations among forecast errors should be ignored rather than estimated, and this is an empirical finding rather than a convenience.** Bates and Granger argued correlations are poorly estimated in practice and should be left out of the weight calculation, and Newbold and Granger (1974) and Winkler and Makridakis (1983) tested that over more series, more methods and more horizons, concluding that <font color="#ffc000">combinations ignoring correlations are more successful than those attempting to account for them</font>. The mechanism is visible in the optimal-weight formula: the full covariance matrix needs $N(N+1)/2$ parameters from the same short error history that a diagonal treatment needs $N$ from, and with highly correlated forecast errors the sample estimator of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ is unstable. Chan et al. (1999) add the companion result that OLS combination performs very poorly when $N$ is large, which is what pushes the literature toward principal components regression and clustering when there are many forecasts to combine.
- **Accuracy and diversity both constrain what combining can deliver, and a subset usually beats both the full pool and the single best.** Mannes et al. (2014) establish that the mean level of expertise in the pool sets a floor on combination performance, so including poor forecasts degrades the result, and Lichtendahl and Winkler (2020) add that the *variance* of accuracy across series matters as much as the mean and the two have to be traded off when choosing the pool. On the other side, diversity is what produces the error cancellation in the first place, with Atiya (2020) deriving it through the bias-variance decomposition of MSE, and diversity is usually measured as low correlation among forecast errors. The practical consequence is the slogan the paper quotes, *many could be better than all*: there are decreasing returns to adding forecasts, and subset selection, whether by discarding the worst performers, by Kourentzes et al.'s (2019) boxplot-outlier heuristic, by Lichtendahl and Winkler's two screens of removing models worse than Naïve2 and excluding pairs with highly correlated errors, or by Diebold and Shin's (2019) two-step LASSO that zeroes some weights then shrinks the survivors toward equality, beats both using everything and selecting one.
- **Feature-based cross-learning is where the field's momentum is, and the competition results support it.** The reframing is that the meta-model learns a mapping from features of a series to weights over a fixed method pool, trained across many series rather than within one, which sidesteps the short-history problem that makes per-series weight estimation noisy. FFORMA placed second in M4 with 42 features and an XGBoost meta-learner, Montero-Manso et al.'s interval variant placed second for the uncertainty track over 100,000 series by predicting the interval radius as a linear combination of individual radii minimising the MSIS, and the top three M4 methods all drew on cross-dataset information. The feature pool has itself become a research object, with Lemke and Gabrys (2010) adding six features that measure diversity of the *method pool* rather than properties of the series, and Kang et al. (2021) building a combination model from a diversity matrix alone.
- **Diversity reverses sign when the output is a distribution, and nonlinear pooling does not pay for its complexity.** Both covered above, and recorded here as findings because they are the two results in section 3 I would actually cite. The linear pool's variance decomposition makes component disagreement an additive penalty to sharpness, and Baran and Lerch found no substantial performance difference between the simple linear pool and the sophisticated nonlinear alternatives. The one exception where sophistication earned its keep is region-specific: Zischke et al. (2022) report that when forecasting during high-volatility periods, weights optimised under a censored likelihood scoring rule always beat weights optimised under the logarithmic score out of sample, which is a result about choosing *what* to score rather than about how elaborate the pooling function is.
- **Nonlinear combination of point forecasts is a thin literature and the authors say so plainly.** The two forms are a neural network over the individual forecasts (Donaldson and Kamstra, 1996), with purely linear and purely nonlinear specifications as special cases, and an additive pairwise term $\sum_{i<j}\pi_{ij}v_{ij}$ where $v_{ij}$ is a nonlinear function of forecasts $i$ and $j$. The reported results generally dominate simple averaging, OLS weights and performance-based weights, and the authors' own assessment of that evidence is that it rests on <font color="#ff0000">fewer than ten time series, possibly hand-picked</font>, alongside neglect of error correlations, unstable parameter estimation and multicollinearity from overlapping information sets.

#### Limitations

- **It is a narrative review with no protocol, no screening counts and no meta-analysis, so it establishes what exists and not what works.** The Web of Science query is given in full, but only for the Figure 1 publication-share chart, not for the review's own selection. Nothing states how papers were found, how many were screened, or what put one in or out. More consequentially, the authors run no experiment of their own and aggregate no results from the papers they cover, so there is no table anywhere comparing two weighting schemes on shared data. Every performance claim in the paper is a report of a single study's finding, which means I can cite this for the existence and the lineage of a method but never for its relative performance. This is the same structural weakness I recorded against [[The Evolution of Reinforcement Learning in Quantitative Finance, A Survey]], and the two surveys share it for the same reason, that coverage and adjudication are different jobs.
- **The puzzle is reported rather than resolved, and the paper leaves two directly contradictory theoretical results standing side by side.** Claeskens et al. (2016) derive that random combination weights introduce bias and inflate the variance of the combined forecast relative to fixed weights, which is the main theoretical explanation of the puzzle. Zischke et al. (2022) find the opposite, that asymptotically the sampling variability of the combination is driven entirely by the estimation of the constituent models and that weight estimation imparts no bias or variability at all. The authors note the opposition in one clause and move on. Since my whole contribution turns on whether estimated state-conditional weights can beat fixed equal weights, that unresolved disagreement is the piece of theory I most need and least get, and the honest position is that the field does not currently know, which is a better thing to write in the proposal than a confident citation to either side.
- **The non-interfering assumption excludes the systems I am actually positioned against.** The components here are separate models that do not see each other's outputs. Every LLM multi-agent trading system in my reading violates that: [[TradingAgents, Multi-Agents LLM Financial Trading Framework]] has agents debating, [[HedgeAgents, A Balanced-aware Multi-agent Financial Trading System]] runs conferences, and even the ones that do not communicate share the same underlying price history and the same disclosures. So the clean statistical properties derived here, the unbiasedness of the combination and the variance bound, hold under an assumption my pool does not satisfy. My four agents read overlapping data by construction, and the paper's own observation that component forecasts trained on overlapping information sets produce highly correlated errors is the mechanism by which that bites.
- **The target is always a realised value of one series under a symmetric loss, so none of the optimality results transfer to a decision.** The combined forecast is scored against $y_{T+h}$ under MSE or a proper scoring rule. There is no portfolio, no position, no transaction cost, no slippage and no execution anywhere in forty pages. Elliott and Timmermann (2004) and Patton and Timmermann (2007) are cited as the asymmetric-loss extension, and the finding is that optimal weights depend strongly on the degree of asymmetry in the loss and the skewness of the error distribution, which is as far as this literature goes toward my setting. My orchestrator's output is evaluated after costs on risk-adjusted return, and a weighting that minimises forecast MSE is not the same object as one that maximises an after-cost objective, so I can borrow the mechanism and the cautionary evidence but not the optimality.
- **Cross-learning, which is the strand closest to what I'm building, is demonstrated at a scale I cannot reach.** The results come from M4 at 100,000 series, M3, M5 and FRED, and the stated first limitation of the approach is the requirement for a large and diverse time series dataset to produce meaningful training. The workarounds offered are simulating series from assumed data generating processes or generating them with controllable characteristics using **gratis**, both of which hand the researcher the features they then learn from. My per-counter archive is roughly 300 counters over 374 trading days, three orders of magnitude short of M4, and no result in this paper speaks to what a feature-to-weight meta-model does at that scale.
- **Emerging and thin markets are absent from the entire paper.** The applications named are retail, energy, economics, epidemiology and meteorology, and the financial examples are macroeconomic series such as inflation and GDP growth. Nothing addresses a market where the components themselves are estimated on short, illiquid, intermittently traded series, which is the condition under which every estimation-error argument in the puzzle section becomes more severe rather than less. The direction of that bias is at least clear, which is the useful part: everything the paper says about estimation error swamping the gains from optimal weights gets worse in my setting, not better.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **It gives the weighting function a lineage and a precise statement of what is new, which is a stronger framing than novelty.** My $w_{i,t} = f(L_t, V_t, P_t, C_{i,t})$ is a time-varying feature-conditioned linear combination, and this paper contains both halves separately: time-varying weights through the Kalman-filter and smooth-transition line, and feature-conditioned weights through FFORMA. FFORMA is the nearest named ancestor and the difference is one sentence, that <font color="#ffc000">its 42 features are properties of the series itself, computed once from the history, while mine are properties of the market state, measured at each step and exogenous to the agent being weighted</font>. That reads as a generalisation along an identified axis rather than new machinery, which is the framing that worked in the orchestration note and is the one I should reuse. It also tells me what the honest comparison is: FFORMA's structure, a gradient-boosted meta-model mapping features to a weight simplex, is available to me as an implementation choice, and if I use something simpler I should say why rather than let it look like I did not know.
- **The puzzle makes my equal-weight baseline the field's hardest benchmark, and it supplies the falsification condition I should pre-register.** This matters more than anything else in the paper. Fifty years of evidence says the equally weighted combination is hard to beat and that the usual reason sophisticated schemes lose is estimation error, so if my state-conditioned weighting fails to beat equal weights on the CSE, that is the expected outcome under the field's prior and not a surprise, and I should write down before the run what margin and what test would count as beating it. The two practical guidelines the paper derives from the puzzle literature cut in opposite directions for me and the tension between them is effectively the experiment: guideline one says that with limited historical data the simple average or weights ignoring covariances are recommended, which my 374 trading days per counter argues for, while guideline two says structural change impacts sophisticated schemes more than the simple average and that changing weights are a legitimate response to it, which is the entire premise of conditioning on state. Which effect dominates on this data is the question, and the paper is clear that nobody has comprehensive quantitative guidance on when to choose which, so an answer in a thin frontier market is a contribution even if the answer is that equal weights win.
- **Ignoring correlations between agent errors is a design simplification with five decades of backing, not a shortcut.** My weighting function treats each agent's weight as a function of state and of that agent's own confidence, with no pairwise term, and Bates and Granger, Newbold and Granger, and Winkler and Makridakis between them say that is the better choice in practice because $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ estimates are unstable and the full matrix costs $N(N+1)/2$ parameters out of a short history. With four agents that is ten parameters against four, from 374 days, so the argument has real force here. This is worth stating explicitly in the write-up, because a reviewer will ask why there is no interaction term, and the answer is that the literature tried that first and moved away from it.
- **Two cheap baselines sit between equal weights and my learned function, and both belong in the comparison.** The Stock and Watson inverse-$k$th-power family gives a one-parameter dial where $k=0$ is equal weights and larger $k$ concentrates on recent winners, so a sweep over $k$ maps the whole continuum from my baseline to winner-take-all for almost no implementation cost. The discounted inverse-MSE scheme adds a time dimension without adding state, which is the baseline that actually threatens my contribution: if weights that merely react to recent agent performance do as well as weights conditioned on measured market state, then the state conditioning is not earning its place and I need to know that early. The three-way comparison I had planned, equal weights against static learned weights against state-conditioned weights, should become four-way with a discounted performance-weighting arm in it. Related, and worth recording once here rather than in the evaluation plan: the paper's future-directions section flags that <font color="#ffc000">standard errors for a combined forecast are nontrivial to obtain because the weights are random and correlated with the forecasts</font>, which is a direct warning about significance testing on my weighted output and matches the pattern in [[Deep Reinforcement Learning Framework for Diversified Dynamic Portfolio Allocation Across Global Equity Markets]] where the economic result and the formal test diverged.
- **The abstention channel is time-varying subset selection, which is named prior art, and the literature's advice is to shrink rather than zero.** The paper states directly that subset selection is equivalent to assigning zero weights to some individual forecasts, and that time-varying subset selection can be achieved by applying a pre-set threshold to a time-varying weighting scheme, citing Li et al. (2022). That is structurally what the abstention channel does when the fundamental agent has no disclosure to work from, which is expected more than half the time, so I should present it in that vocabulary rather than as a bespoke mechanism. Two things follow. First, the paper's assessment is that subset selection has received scant attention, is mainly trimming on expertise alone, and that most existing algorithms are ad hoc and lack statistical rationale, so a principled selection rule driven by a structural property of the output is defensible ground. Second, Geweke and Amisano's observation that individually poor forecasts may still help in combination, and that one does not want to zero-weight them, is an argument for shrinking the abstaining agent's weight toward a floor rather than driving it to exactly zero, which is a concrete design decision I had not settled.
- **Diversity in my agent pool has to be measured rather than asserted, and this paper gives the measurement.** I describe the four agents as methodologically heterogeneous and that heterogeneity is load-bearing in the gap statement, but the paper's operational definition of diversity is low correlation among forecast errors, with alternatives in Lemke and Gabrys' six diversity features and Thomson et al.'s coherence measure. So the claim is testable on my own pool and I should run it: if the technical agent and the volatility agent produce errors that correlate at 0.8 on CSE data, the pool is substantially less diverse than the architecture diagram implies and the error-cancellation argument weakens accordingly. This is close to free, it uses data I will already have from the individual agent runs, and it is the kind of result that is much better found by me than by an examiner.
- **If the orchestrator ever emits an interval rather than a point allocation, the diversity argument inverts and quantile averaging is the escape.** The linear pool's variance decomposition says component disagreement adds directly to the pooled variance, so a deliberately heterogeneous pool produces an under-confident interval, and that is the one place where my central design choice actively works against me. Quantile averaging avoids it by averaging the cdfs horizontally, preserves the family for location-scale components, and is sharper for the same mean, which makes it the default if I go that way. This connects to [[Adaptive Conformal Inference Under Distribution Shift]] more tightly than I expected, since adaptive conformal inference is a per-agent calibration wrapper and quantile averaging is the aggregation step, and the pair would give me a calibrated interval from a heterogeneous pool without the under-confidence that linear pooling would introduce. That is a route to the confidence term being a measured interval width rather than a self-report, which is what the scope revision asked for.
- **Interpreting combination weights is an open research question in this literature, which upgrades the explainability claim from a feature to a positioned contribution.** One of the eight future directions is, in the authors' words, discussing if, how, and when it is helpful to interpret combination weights, prompted by the observation that some schemes drive poor forecasts to zero asymptotically while individually bad forecasts can still be useful. My orchestrator produces a weight per agent per step as a readable function of named market-state inputs, so the trace of which agent was trusted when, and on what measured conditions, is a direct response to that question rather than a byproduct. The caveat I should carry is the one the layered-explainability reading already established, that a weight being readable is not the same as it being correct, so the claim is that the system exposes the quantity this literature says nobody has examined, not that exposing it is sufficient.
