arXiv:2411.08899v1, Sorouralsadat Fatemi and Yuheng Hu of the University of Illinois at Chicago, 29 October 2024. A multimodal multi-agent LLM framework for daily stock trading built from four modules and an execution agent, a Summarizer that condenses the previous day's news into prose, a Technical Analyst that reads a *rendered image* of a candlestick chart with indicators rather than the numeric series, a two-part Reflection module that scores recent decision effectiveness in text and separately looks at a plotted chart of past buy and sell markers, and a Prediction agent that concatenates all of it and emits BUY, SELL or HOLD with a position size and a written rationale. A Reward agent executes the trade and feeds performance back into the next iteration. Implemented in LangGraph over a shared `AgentState`, with `gpt-4o-mini` at temperature 0.3 for every agent except the Prediction agent, which runs `o1-mini`. Tested on AAPL, AMZN and MSFT from April to December 2023 against buy-and-hold, two rule-based strategies, PPO, DQN and FinAgent.

The note this replaces was a summary with a prompt appendix and three bullets of differences, written before the scope cut, and its two standout observations, the reflection module and the visual analysis, are still the right two things to have picked out. What has changed is that both now have to be read against [[Scope sentiment analysis dropped]] and against what I am actually building. Two things make this worth a full review despite it being the weaker paper next to [[TradingAgents, Multi-Agents LLM Financial Trading Framework]]. It is <font color="#ffc000">the only paper in my set that runs a component ablation across every asset and reports it</font>, which is the experimental shape my weighting comparison has to take, and it is the clearest demonstration I have found of how much of an "LLM agent" can turn out to be a hand-written rule set sitting inside a prompt.

> [!NOTE] What "training period" means here
> The paper splits its nine months into a two-month training period of 42 trading days and a seven-month testing period of 145 days, and presents the short training window as an efficiency contribution against FinAgent's one year. Nothing is fitted in those 42 days. There are no weights and no gradients anywhere in the framework, and the window exists so that the Reflection module has decision history to reflect on and the signal chart has markers to plot. It is a burn-in, not training, and the distinction matters for my own planning because a burn-in comes out of the usable evaluation window rather than out of a separate training set.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **Deep learning and RL trading models flatten heterogeneous inputs into numeric features and lose both information and interpretability.** The specific charges are that news gets compressed into a single sentiment scalar that cannot track evolving events, and that high-dimensional, non-linear, time-dependent indicator series are hard to encode without loss. The sentiment-scalar criticism is the strong half and it is the same objection [[Explainable zero-shot trading using multi-agent LLM architecture, A backtested approach for Bitcoin price]] raises independently, so it is not idiosyncratic.
- **Multimodal LLM agents work but are expensive to set up.** FinAgent is named directly as the prior art that demonstrated the approach and as the thing to improve on, on the grounds that it required a one-year training period with the API costs that implies. This is a cost gap rather than a capability gap and the paper is straightforward about that.
- **Risk management is absent from the comparable LLM trading agents.** The stated fix is that the Prediction agent outputs a position size as a percentage of the portfolio rather than an all-in signal, which is the paper's one genuine addition over its predecessor and the thing that ends up carrying its only defensible result.

The third is a real gap and it is addressed. The first is a motivation for the design rather than a gap the experiments close, since nothing here tests the representation claim, and the limitations section is where that lands.

#### Data and setup

Three stocks, AAPL, AMZN and MSFT, over 1 April to 29 December 2023. Burn-in runs 1 April to 31 May, 42 trading days, and the evaluation runs 1 June to 29 December, 145 trading days. News comes from Yahoo Finance through the eodhd API, and the per-ticker counts are worth having in front of you because they are strange:

| Ticker | Burn-in days | Burn-in articles | Test days | Test articles |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| AAPL | 42 | 1,081 | 145 | 4,886 |
| AMZN | 42 | 1,113 | 145 | 5,556 |
| MSFT | 42 | 1,897 | 145 | 1,249 |

<font color="#ffc000">MSFT collects fewer articles across 145 days than across 42</font>, and lands at roughly a quarter of AMZN's volume over identical dates, which for two mega-cap tech stocks in the same window is not a plausible reflection of news flow. The paper does not remark on it, and it means the news channel's density differs by more than 4× across the three assets whose results are then reported side by side.

Technical inputs are simple moving averages at 10 and 50 days, RSI over 14 days, Bollinger Bands at 20 days and two standard deviations, trading volume and MACD. All of them are computed numerically and then <font color="#ffff00">plotted with Matplotlib into images</font>, one candlestick-and-indicator chart covering the prior 60 days and one trading-signal chart covering the prior 30, and it is the images that reach the agents.

Baselines are buy-and-hold, MACD, KDJ with an RSI filter, PPO, DQN and FinAgent. Only the first five are re-run here; the FinAgent row is taken from FinAgent's own publication. Metrics are annualised return, Sharpe and maximum drawdown. There is no transaction cost model, no slippage and no impact, and every reported number is a single run with no repeats and no dispersion.

#### Model architecture and training

There is no training, in the sense set out in the callout above. Every module is a prompted agent and the only thing that changes between days is the contents of the shared state.

![[Pasted image 20260808181535.png]]

The paper formalises all five agents as the same shape, an agent called on a ticker, some inputs and a prompt. Four of them produce text. The fifth is the one that matters, because it is the entire aggregation mechanism:

$$\hat{A}^{s_t} = \text{agent}_{\text{decision}}\left(s,\ X_1^{s_{t-1}},\ X_2^{s_{t-1}},\ X_3^{s_{t-1}},\ X_4^{s_{t-1}},\ P^{s_{t-1}},\ \text{prompt}_{\text{trading}}\right)$$

where $X_1$ is the news summary, $X_2$ the technical read of the chart image, $X_3$ the textual reflection, $X_4$ the visual reflection over the signal chart, $P$ the portfolio state from the Reward agent, and the output $\hat{A}^{s_t} = (\hat{a}^{s_t}, \hat{p}^{s_t}, \hat{e}^{s_t})$ is an action in BUY, SELL or HOLD, a position size in $[1,10]$ and an explanation. Writing it out makes the structural point without argument, <font color="#ffc000">the four channels are concatenated into one prompt and there is no weight, no confidence and no quantity anywhere that could be weighted</font>, which is the same absence as in TradingAgents but reached with one decision agent instead of a seven-role hierarchy, so it is not an artefact of that paper's organisational framing.

The agent prompts are short and specific, and they are reference material I want to keep because the constraints in them bound what each channel can contribute:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| News Summarizer | Summarise the key points in 5-6 sentences, highlighting information that could impact {ticker}'s price in the short term, identify positive and negative factors, and say whether sentiment is bullish, bearish or neutral. |
| Chart Analyst | Analyze this {ticker} candlestick chart focusing on three trading strategies: 1. MACD Crossover Strategy 2. KDJ with RSI Filter Strategy. For each strategy, provide: one key market trend observation, one potential trading signal. Format: Strategy 1: [Trend] \| [Signal]. **Be concise. Limit each observation and signal to 10 words or less.** |
| Reflection, short and medium term | Focus on: recent recommendations and their outcomes (reward), key factors influencing decisions, cumulative return trend. Provide three insights: decision effectiveness, most impactful key factors, short/medium-term return trend. Format: [Effectiveness] \| [Key Factors] \| [Return Trend]. **Keep each insight under 15 words.** |
| Trading signal chart reflection | Green markers are previous BUY decisions, red SELL, absence HOLD. Relate the recent price trend to previous signals, evaluate their effectiveness against subsequent price movements, identify missed opportunities or mistakes. |

Two things fall out of reading those side by side. The chart analyst is capped at ten words per signal, so a 60-day chart carrying six indicator series arrives at the decision as at most a couple of dozen words, and the reflection insights are capped at fifteen. Whatever the vision model sees, the channel is narrow by construction.

The headline metric needs its definition, because the whole results table is read through it. Annualised return here is a linear rescaling of the realised return, not a compounded one,

$$\text{ARR} = \frac{P_T - P_0}{P_0} \times \frac{C}{T}$$

where $P_0$ and $P_T$ are the initial and final portfolio values, $T$ is the number of trading days in the run and $C$ the number in a year, so the 145-day results are simply multiplied by roughly 1.74. AMZN's headline 43.57 % for buy-and-hold is therefore about a 25 % realised gain over seven months. Nothing is wrong with that as long as everything in the table uses it, which is precisely the problem with the one row that was copied in from elsewhere.

#### Findings

- **On returns it roughly matches passive holding and loses heavily to the one LLM it is compared against, and the claim that survives is risk-adjusted instead.** ARR of 14.79 on AAPL, 25.57 on MSFT and 42.14 on AMZN against buy-and-hold at 13.56, 22.27 and 43.57, so it clears passive by 1.23 and 3.30 points on two stocks and <font color="#ffc000">falls below it on the third</font>, while FinAgent posts 31.89, 44.74 and 65.10 and is ahead by 17 to 23 points everywhere. Sharpe runs the other way, 1.20, 1.41 and 1.72 against 0.67, 1.01 and 1.37, with AMZN's 1.72 the best figure in the whole table including FinAgent's 1.61, and drawdown there of 12.09 % against the market's 17.45 %. Scoped to this sample, what the framework does is convert a return stream similar to passive into a smoother one. Position sizing is the plausible mechanism, since the agent moves in 1 to 5 % slices rather than all-in, but nothing isolates it and the authors do not claim it as a tested result.

  ![[Pasted image 20260926003001.png]]

- **The reflection ablation moves the same way on every stock, and that consistency is worth more than any single margin.** Removing the reflection module drops ARR from 14.79 to 8.84, from 25.57 to 16.99 and from 42.14 to 37.64, and Sharpe from 1.20 to 0.62 and 1.41 to 1.04. This is the experimental shape I want and the reason the paper earns a full review. The scoping caveat is that these are single runs with no dispersion, and on AMZN the Sharpe barely moves at all, 1.72 to 1.68, so the consistent signal is in the returns rather than uniformly across both metrics.
- **The abstract attributes that gain to the *visual* reflection module, and no experiment in the paper separates it.** The ablation removes reflection as a single block, and reflection is two mechanisms, a textual score of recent decision effectiveness and a vision-model read of the plotted signal chart. One row cannot distinguish them. <font color="#ff0000">"The visual reflection module plays a crucial role" is a claim about a component that was never ablated on its own</font>, and since the textual half is both cheaper and more conventional, the attribution runs toward the more novel component rather than toward the evidence. The conclusion section is more careful and says only that the reflection component contributes, which is what the table supports.
- **The RL baselines post negative Sharpe on every stock while returning positive ARR, which says more about their training budget than about RL.** PPO at −0.42, −0.73 and −0.59 and DQN at −0.90, −0.26 and −0.46, alongside returns as high as 22.07. Those policies were given the same 42-day window the framework uses as a burn-in, which is not a budget any RL practitioner would accept, and the paper's claim of substantially higher performance than RL is bought at least partly by that. I would not cite this paper as evidence about reinforcement learning in either direction, and [[MacroHFT, Memory Augmented Context-aware Reinforcement Learning On High Frequency Trading]] and [[The Evolution of Reinforcement Learning in Quantitative Finance, A Survey]] are where that question actually gets addressed.
- **The decision log is the framework's best artefact and is also where its limits are most visible.** The sample response cites an unrealised profit 5.63 % above average purchase price, four named indicators pointing in conflicting directions, a specific news event, and a reflection that past sell signals were timed too early, then sizes the trade at 3 % of the portfolio.

  ![[Pasted image 20260926003002.png]]

  It is readable, specific and short enough that someone would actually read it. It also ends by stating that the strategy "aligns with the provided trading rules", which is the agent naming the rule set it was handed, and is the cleanest possible lead into the limitation below.

#### Limitations

- **The trading policy is written into the Prediction agent's prompt, so this is a rule-based strategy with an LLM applying it rather than a system that learns one.** The prompt specifies: maintain at least 10 % cash; BUY on a strong upward trend with cash above 10 % of portfolio, or on a price dip within an upward trend with cash above 20 %; SELL on signs of significant trend reversal, or on gains above 5 % from average purchase price for a partial sell, or on consistent gains for three or more consecutive days; size at 5 to 10 % in strong upward trends and 1 to 5 % on dips, with partial sells of 3 to 5 %. Every threshold is hand-chosen and none is reported as tuned. Two consequences follow. The comparison against MACD and KDJ&RSI is not a language model against rules, it is one rule set with market context against another rule set without it, and the paper's stated risk-management contribution is a position-size range specified in the prompt rather than a quantity the system determines. <font color="#ff0000">There is no learned component anywhere in this framework</font>, and the word "agent" is doing a lot of work to obscure that.
- **Three stocks, 145 test days, one strongly bullish regime, one run each.** No seeds, no dispersion, no significance test, and every cell in the results table is a single number with nothing attached to it. The paper concedes the regime issue itself, noting that the strong tech uptrend over the test window inherently favours passive strategies, which is honest and also means the two stocks where it beats buy-and-hold are the two where passive did least well. Against Jung and Lee's twenty seeds with standard deviations, a 1.23-point margin on AAPL is not a margin I could defend.
- **The only LLM baseline is copied from another paper rather than re-run.** FinAgent's row is retrieved from its own publication, so it comes from a different evaluation window and possibly a different universe, and given that annualised return here is a linear rescaling by $C/T$, quite possibly a different definition of the headline column. Comparing a copied row against re-run rows inside one table is not a controlled comparison. The direction matters less than the fact that the 17 to 23 point gap, which is the paper's own framing of where it stands, cannot be read off the table with confidence in either direction.
- **No execution friction, on the one claim the paper has left.** Frequent partial rebalancing in 1 to 5 % slices is exactly the pattern whose risk-adjusted advantage a realistic cost model erodes first, because the smoothing comes from trading more often rather than from taking better positions. So the absence of costs does not merely inflate a return figure here, it sits directly underneath <font color="#ffc000">the only result this paper can defend</font>, and the Sharpe advantage over buy-and-hold is measured between a strategy that makes two trades and one that makes many, on the assumption that both are free.
- **The representation claim that motivates the "multi-modal" in the title is never tested.** Indicators are computed numerically, plotted to pixels, and read back by a vision model capped at ten words per signal. The paper's argument is that high-dimensional indicator series are hard to encode without loss, which is a real argument worth engaging rather than dismissing. But no experiment compares chart input against feeding the same indicator values as numbers into the same prompt, which would have been cheaper to run than the ablation that was. On this evidence I cannot tell whether the vision channel helps, hurts or is neutral.
- **Two things that make the setup hard to trust at the margin.** The backbones are `gpt-4o-mini` and `o1-mini` and the test window is June to December 2023, so the models' pre-training covers the period they are evaluated on, and the framework's look-ahead control governs the data pipeline rather than what the model already knows; this is a standing issue across every LLM trading paper in my set rather than a fault specific to this one. And the news counts in the dataset table are unexplained, with MSFT collecting fewer articles over 145 days than over 42 while AMZN collects four and a half times as many over the identical window, which is either a collection failure or a filtering rule that is never described.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **This is the experimental shape my weighting comparison has to take, and it also shows that design's failure mode.** Full system against system-minus-one-named-component, same metrics, every asset, and my three-way comparison of equal, static learned and dynamic state-aware weighting plus the four state-input ablations is the same design with more arms. Two things to take from seeing one executed. <font color="#ffff00">Consistency of sign across assets is the thing to report</font> rather than the size of any single margin, because it survives the absence of significance testing better than a point estimate does. And bundling two mechanisms into one ablatable block, as reflection does here, leaves the paper free to attribute the result to whichever half it prefers, so my liquidity, volatility, persistence and confidence inputs have to be independently removable from the start. That is an implementation constraint on the orchestrator, not something to retrofit at evaluation time, and the four ablation arms belong in the design before the weighting function is written.
- **The clearest warning in my set about where a policy can hide.** If $w_{i,t} = f(L_t, V_t, P_t, C_{i,t})$ ends up specified in a prompt rather than fitted, then "dynamic state-aware weighting" is a hand-written rule set and the contribution disappears, and nothing about this paper suggests its authors intended that outcome. The commitment that follows is concrete, $f$ has to be a fitted function whose parameters I can report and inspect, and the baseline it has to clear is the static learned weight, which is exactly the baseline FinVision has no version of. The supervisor's point from 20 September that beating equal weighting proves very little now has a worked example attached to it.
- **A design decision for $A_T$, numbers rather than pictures, which I should state and justify rather than leave implicit.** Computing indicators and then rendering them to an image for a vision model to read back is lossy by construction, and the ten-word cap makes the channel narrower still. For the CSE the case is weaker again, since thin and intermittent trading makes candlestick patterns sparse and the visual regularities that a chart-reading model would key on less reliable. The honest scoping is that the evidence here is absent rather than against, no experiment compares the two, so this is a decision I am making on architectural grounds and should defend that way.
- **The reflection module is the closest thing in the literature to a mechanism for making agent confidence comparable, and the way it falls short is a proposed revision to my conditioning set.** Reflection scores past decision effectiveness against realised outcomes, which is the right quantity for $C_{i,t}$ to be tracking, an agent's recent track record rather than an elicited number it has no basis for. But it is a single system-level reflection over the framework's own trades, so nothing attributes a good or bad day to the technical channel rather than the news channel. Making it per-agent is a small change to this design and gives a concrete candidate definition, <font color="#ffc000">confidence as a rolling per-agent hit rate against realised next-period outcomes</font>, which sits alongside Jung and Lee's pairwise conflict scoring as the second workable answer I have found to a problem the rest of the literature does not address at all. I would put both to the supervisor as a refinement of $C_{i,t}$ rather than leaving it as an unspecified input.
- **The 42-day burn-in is a real constraint on a twelve-month CSE dataset and it comes out of the wrong end.** Any confidence estimate defined as a rolling track record needs history before it produces anything, exactly as reflection does here, and that history accumulates inside the evaluation window rather than in a separate training set. On the confirmed February 2025 to February 2026 data a 42-day equivalent is roughly 17 % of the run. The decision is to fix the burn-in length before the experiment, report the usable test window net of it, and treat that net length rather than the raw dataset length as the number the design has to justify.
- **An undertrained baseline is worse than no baseline, and the trap has an exact analogue in my design.** PPO and DQN on 42 days of training tell me nothing about reinforcement learning, and their presence makes the paper's comparison look stronger than it is. The equivalent failure for me is a carelessly fitted static learned weight, which is the one baseline my contribution has to clear, and a weak version of it would hollow out the entire result while appearing to support it. Baseline 2 gets the same tuning budget and the same reporting as the proposed method, and I should say so in the experimental design rather than deciding it later.
- **The decision log is the format for my explainability artefact, and it is better suited than TradingAgents' for being shorter.** One structured page with numbered rationale sections, the specific figures that drove the call and an explicit position size is something an examiner will read; sixteen pages of raw transcript is not. The shape to adopt is this one, with the addition my design makes possible, the orchestrator's weight vector printed beside the rationale so the log shows not only what each agent said but how much each one counted, which is the single thing this framework's log cannot show because there is no weight in it.
