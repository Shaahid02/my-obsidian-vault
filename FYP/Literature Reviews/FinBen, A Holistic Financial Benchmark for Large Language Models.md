arXiv:2402.12659v2, 19 June 2024, preprint under review. Qianqian Xie, Jimin Huang and Min Peng again, now with roughly thirty authors across The Fin AI, Wuhan, Manchester, Florida, Columbia, CUHK-Shenzhen, Stevens, Stony Brook and others, with Alejandro Lopez-Lira and Sophia Ananiadou on the science leadership list. <font color="#ffff00">FinBen</font> is the successor to FLARE: 36 datasets across 24 tasks, organised into seven aspects, information extraction, textual analysis, question answering, text generation, risk management, forecasting and decision-making, evaluated zero-shot against fifteen LLMs including GPT-4, ChatGPT and Gemini Pro. Three datasets are new, <font color="#ffc000">EDTSum</font> for news summarisation, <font color="#ffc000">Regulations</font> for long-form OTC-derivatives QA under EMIR, and <font color="#ffc000">FinTrade</font> for stock trading, together 10.32% of the total data. The claimed firsts are stock trading evaluation, agent-based evaluation and RAG-based evaluation.

This sits directly on top of [[PIXIU, A Comprehensive Benchmark, Instruction Dataset and Large Language Model for Finance]], same lead authors, same repository, and it should be read as PIXIU's evaluation half scaled up with the instruction-tuning half dropped. That makes the comparison between the two notes more useful than either alone, because the same research group re-ran a wider version of the same question eight months later with frontier models, and most of the failure modes survived. For my project it speaks to all four channels at once rather than to one, and the three things I actually want out of it are whether retrieval gets evaluated anywhere in the financial-LLM benchmark literature, what the honest prior is for an LLM making directional calls, and whether the trading result that everybody cites survives being read carefully. Per [[Scope sentiment analysis dropped]] the eight textual-analysis classification tasks are read as evidence about evaluation, not as a component to build.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **Existing financial benchmarks cover a handful of NLP tasks and stop there.** Table 1 is the positioning argument and it is the cleanest thing in the paper: CFBenchmark 8 datasets and 7 tasks, Fin-Eva 1 and 1, PIXIU 15 and 8, FinanceBench 1 and 1, BizBench 8 and 5, against FinBen's 36 and 24. More to the point is the distribution across the seven aspects, where PIXIU is the only prior benchmark with any entry in forecasting and none of them has one in decision-making. The charge is that a benchmark of sentiment and entity extraction tells you nothing about whether a model can be used for the decisions the domain makes, which is the same ecological-validity move [[FinanceBench, A New Benchmark for Financial Question Answering]] makes about retrieval and PIXIU makes about prediction, pushed one step further into trading.

![[Pasted image 20260926170001.png]]

- **No benchmark evaluates a financial LLM as an agent rather than as a text-in text-out function.** The decision-making aspect exists to fix this, and the mechanism is that FinTrade is run through [[FinMe, A Performance-Enhanced LLM Trading Agent With Layered Memory and Character Design]]'s framework rather than through a bare prompt, so the model is scored on cumulative return, Sharpe ratio, volatility and drawdown instead of on classification accuracy. This is the part of the paper I care about most and it is also the part where the measurement turns out to be weakest.
- **Regulatory and legal reasoning is untested.** Regulations is 254 long-form questions built from EMIR and related directives with updates from 2013 to 2021, and the authors describe it as the first dataset requiring both financial and legal knowledge. The motivation is real; the metric chosen for it is not, which is the limitations section's problem rather than this one's.

#### Data and setup

FinBen is overwhelmingly an assembly rather than a construction. Of the 36 datasets, the authors state that newly built data is 10.32% of the total, and the three new sets are EDTSum at 2,000 news items with manually selected ground-truth headlines-as-summaries cleaned from Zhou et al. 2021, Regulations at 254 items, and FinTrade at 3,384 records covering 10 US stocks with prices, news and sentiment. Everything else comes from prior open datasets or straight out of PIXIU already in instruction format, which is why the licence column in Table 2 is the usual patchwork of CC BY-SA 3.0, CC BY-NC 4.0, MIT and public.

The aspect breakdown, with the task counts from Table 1 and the datasets from Table 2:

| Aspect | Tasks | Representative datasets | Metrics |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Information extraction | 6 | NER, FiNER-ORD, FinRED, SC, CD, FNXL, FSRL | Entity F1, F1, EM accuracy |
| Textual analysis | 8 | FPB, FiQA-SA, TSA, Headlines, FOMC, FinArg, MultiFin, MA, MLESG | Accuracy, F1 |
| Question answering | 3 | FinQA, TATQA, ConvFinQA, Regulations | EM accuracy, ROUGE, BERTScore |
| Text generation | 1 | ECTSUM, EDTSum | ROUGE, BERTScore, BARTScore |
| Risk management | 4 | German, Australian, LendingClub, ccf, ccfraud, polish, taiwan, PortoSeguro, travelinsurance | F1, MCC |
| Forecasting | 1 | BigData22, ACL18, CIKM18 | Accuracy, MCC |
| Decision-making | 1 | FinTrade | CR, SR, DV, AV, MD |

The thing worth noticing in that table is how lopsided it is. Fourteen of the 24 tasks are information extraction and textual analysis, which are the two aspects every prior benchmark already covered, and the three aspects that justify the paper, text generation, forecasting and decision-making, carry one task each. Breadth in the dataset count is not breadth in the capability count.

Evaluation is zero-shot from the authors' own runs, with few-shot figures imported from earlier papers marked with an asterisk in the results table. Maximum generation length 1024 tokens, batch size 20,000, <font color="#ffc000">16 NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs for approximately 600 hours, total cost including GPT-4 API charges approximately $51,000</font>. That figure is the one I would quote in the proposal whenever the question of building a benchmark comes up.

> [!info] The prompt tables are the reusable artefact
> Table 7 and Table 8 in the appendix give the full instruction text for every dataset, and unlike the rest of the paper they transfer directly. The FinTrade prompt is the interesting one: it asks for short-term, mid-term, long-term and reflection-term information, requires the model to consider momentum, instructs it to behave as risk-seeking when cumulative return is positive and risk-averse when negative, forbids "hold" unless the call is genuinely hard, and constrains the output to a JSON object with `investment_decision`, `summary_reason` and four memory indices. That is a usable contract for a trading agent, written down, and it is the closest thing in this paper to something I can lift.

#### Findings

- **Most of the headline results grid is zero, and the zeros are output-format failures rather than graded capability.** FinQA exact-match accuracy is 0.00 for nine of the twelve models in Table 3 including Gemini, ConvFinQA is 0.00 for nine, FinRED F1 is 0.00 or 0.02 for all twelve, FNXL and FSRL are 0.00 or 0.01 almost everywhere, and ECTSUM's BARTScore is identical at -5.18 across eight models. Eight models producing a byte-identical score is a degenerate-output signature, not eight measurements, and it is the same ECTSUM column that behaved this way in PIXIU eight months earlier, so the benchmark has reproduced the artefact rather than fixed it. Read as capability the grid says open models cannot do financial reasoning; read as measurement it says a large fraction of these cells never contained a parseable answer and the benchmark does not distinguish the two.

  ![[Pasted image 20260926170002.png]]

- **Every model is at chance on directional forecasting once MCC is read instead of accuracy, and the non-LLM baselines the paper buries in Appendix G beat all of them.** Across BigData22, ACL18 and CIKM18 the twelve models cluster at accuracy 0.41 to 0.57 with MCC between -0.07 and 0.08, GPT-4 at 0.02, 0.02 and 0.02, Gemini at 0.03, 0.04 and 0.02, and the best single cell anywhere is FinMA 7B's 0.08 on CIKM18. Table 14 then reports SLOT, a 2022 self-supervised sequence model, at MCC 0.10, 0.21 and 0.09, and DTML at 0.07, 0.18 and -0.00. So a purpose-built non-LLM from two years earlier is the best model on the task and the paper's own conclusion section does not say so. The authors do state that all LLMs "fail to meet expected outcomes and lag behind traditional methodologies", which is honest, but placing the evidence in an appendix while the abstract leads on holistic coverage is a presentational choice with consequences for how the paper gets cited.

  ![[Pasted image 20260926170004.png]]

- **Risk management is the clearest case of a high F1 concealing a model that ignores its input.** These nine datasets are tabular credit and fraud records serialised into prompts, all heavily imbalanced, and the pattern is F1 in the 0.5 to 0.95 range against MCC pinned at or just below zero: Mistral 7B on polish at F1 0.92 and MCC 0.00, GPT-4 on taiwan at 0.55 and -0.02, GPT-4 on portoseguro at 0.55 and -0.02, LLaMA2-70B on ccf at F1 1.00 and MCC 0.00. <font color="#ff0000">F1 near 0.9 with MCC exactly 0.00 is a model predicting the majority class for every row</font>. GPT-4's MCC is -0.02 on eight of the nine, which is a single behaviour repeated, not eight results. No gradient-boosted-tree baseline appears anywhere, which is the comparison these datasets would normally be scored against, so the section measures how badly LLMs handle serialised tables without establishing what the alternative achieves.
- **Trading is the one aspect where the LLMs look good, and the effect is large in point-estimate terms.** GPT-4 reaches cumulative return 28.19% against Buy & Hold's -4.00%, Sharpe 1.51 against 0.02, and maximum drawdown 18.34% against 30.67%, with Gemini second at 14.95% and Sharpe 1.03. Table 5 puts A2C, PPO and DQN at cumulative returns of -4.22%, -0.56% and -2.99% with Sharpe ratios of -0.26, 0.01 and -0.17, so on this window the RL baselines lose money and the frontier LLMs do not. Against that, the ordering inside the LLM set does not behave like a capability ranking: <font color="#ffc000">GPT-4o returns -5.54% at Sharpe -0.19 and llama3-70B returns -2.57% at Sharpe -0.04</font>, both newer than the models above them, and llama2-70B at 4.02% beats llama3-70B. A capability story that has GPT-4 at the top and GPT-4o below Buy & Hold needs an explanation the paper does not offer.

  ![[Pasted image 20260926170003.png]]

- **Financial instruction tuning helps exactly where PIXIU said it helps and nowhere else, and tuning in the wrong language actively hurts.** FinMA 7B posts the best scores in the table on FPB at 0.88 and FiQA-SA at 0.88 and Headlines at 0.97, beating GPT-4's 0.78, 0.76 and 0.86, and then scores 0.04 on FinQA, 0.00 on TATQA and 0.00 on ConvFinQA. CFGPT sft-7B-Full, fine-tuned on Chinese financial data from an InternLM 7B base, falls below its own backbone on MultiFin at 0.05 against 0.37 and on FOMC at 0.21 against 0.35, which the authors read as cross-lingual adaptation damaging English performance. That reading is plausible and it is an observation from two checkpoints rather than an ablation.
- **The shared-task result the abstract leads on is not what the appendix shows.** The abstract says the FinLLM Challenge teams' "novel solutions outperformed GPT-4". Appendix F.3 says the top three teams' Task 1 F1 scores were "comparable to LLaMA3-8B, although slightly inferior to GPT-4 and LLaMA2-70B", and Table 13 gives the winning team's Sharpe ratio on single stock trading as 0.9268 against GPT-4's 1.51 in Table 4. The challenge itself is real, 35 teams registered and 11 submitted system papers, and the honest summary is that participants built competitive small models, which is a different and less quotable claim.

#### Limitations

- **The RAG evaluation claimed as a headline innovation does not exist in the paper.** The abstract lists "novel agent and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) evaluation" among the key innovations and the introduction repeats that FinBen "is the first benchmark to include agent-based evaluation and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) based evaluation". There is no retrieval dataset in Table 2, no retrieval condition in Section 3, no retrieval results in Section 4 and nothing in the appendices. Every task feeds its context directly into the prompt. The agent half of that sentence is delivered through FinMem on FinTrade; the RAG half is asserted and never operationalised. <font color="#ff0000">This is the claim I most wanted the paper to support and it is the one that is missing entirely</font>, which means that as of mid-2024 no financial LLM benchmark evaluates retrieval as a variable, and [[FinanceBench, A New Benchmark for Financial Question Answering]]'s 19% to 50% under realistic retrieval remains the only number in the reading list that measures it.
- **Several headline metrics are measuring output length and format rather than capability, and the paper's prose contradicts its own table.** On Regulations, LLaMA2-7B-chat scores ROUGE-1 0.24 against GPT-4's 0.11 and ChatGPT's 0.12, so a 7B chat model is the best long-form regulatory QA system in the benchmark, which is what ROUGE-1 does when one model writes longer answers. The body text nonetheless says GPT-4 "demonstrates its broad knowledge coverage effectively" on this dataset. On QA, GPT-4's TATQA exact-match accuracy is 0.13 against 0.63 on FinQA, and [[TAT-QA, A Question Answering Benchmark on a Hybrid of Tabular and Textual Content in Finance]] is not five times harder than [[FinQA, A Dataset of Numerical Reasoning over Financial Data]], so that gap is an answer-normalisation artefact, particularly since TAT-QA's answers include spans, counts and multi-span lists that exact match handles badly.
- **The trading confidence intervals cannot be reproduced from the paper's own per-stock table and the paper never says how they were computed.** Table 4 reports means with 95% intervals across 10 stocks and Table 9 gives the per-stock figures, so the interval should be recoverable as
  $$\bar{x} \pm t_{0.975,\,n-1}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}, \qquad n = 10$$
  where $\bar{x}$ and $s$ are the mean and sample standard deviation across the ten tickers. Recomputing GPT-4 from Table 9 gives mean cumulative return 26.80% and mean Sharpe 1.45 against the reported 28.19% and 1.51, and a cross-sectional interval of roughly ±10.5 on cumulative return against the reported ±25.27; the reported interval is not $1.96s$ either, which would be ±33.2, and the ratio of reported half-width to cross-stock standard deviation is 1.49 for GPT-4 and 0.93 for Buy & Hold, so it is not a fixed multiple of anything in Table 9. Whatever the intervals are, they are not the cross-sectional spread of the ten results the paper prints, and no significance test against Buy & Hold is reported anywhere. Add that the evaluation window in Figures 2 to 11 runs roughly October 2022 to April 2023, one regime on ten US names with no transaction costs modelled, and the strongest defensible statement is that GPT-4 beat Buy & Hold on this window on these ten stocks.
- **Own zero-shot runs and imported few-shot numbers share the same cells, and the trading table uses a different model set from the benchmark.** The asterisked entries in Table 3 come from previous papers under few-shot prompting while the unmarked ones are the authors' three-run zero-shot averages, so FPB's ChatGPT 0.78* and Gemini's 0.77 are not the same measurement, and the QA row that carries the most weight, GPT-4's 0.63 on FinQA and 0.76 on ConvFinQA, is entirely imported. Separately, Table 4 evaluates GPT-4o and llama3-70B, neither of which appears in the Section 3 list of fifteen models, while omitting most of the models that do. The direction of the closed-versus-open gap is safe because it is large and repeated, the magnitudes are not comparable across columns, and this is the same problem PIXIU had, unaddressed in the successor.
- **"Holistic" describes coverage and not measurement, because nothing aggregates.** There is no composite score, no per-aspect score and no weighting across the 36 datasets, so the benchmark cannot rank two models overall or say that one aspect is better served than another except by eyeballing a grid where many cells are degenerate. Combined with 89.68% of the data being repackaged from prior benchmarks, mostly PIXIU's own FLARE, the delta over the predecessor is breadth of coverage plus FinTrade, which is a real contribution and a narrower one than the framing suggests.
- **The leakage machinery exists and is pointed at the wrong models.** Appendix F.2 introduces a Data Leakage Test, applied to the top three shared-task teams per task, which I would write as
  $$\Delta_{\text{DLT}} = \mathrm{PPL}_{M}(\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}) - \mathrm{PPL}_{M}(\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}})$$
  where $\mathrm{PPL}_{M}$ is the submitted model's perplexity on each split, and a small $\Delta$ means the test text is as familiar to the model as the training text, suggesting exposure. That formalisation is mine from their prose; the paper gives no threshold, reports no values, and never applies the test to the fifteen LLMs whose scores make up the benchmark, even though ACL18 covers 2014 to 2016, the credit datasets are long-public, and the trading window sits inside the pre-training corpus of llama3 and GPT-4o. No chronological split and no per-period breakdown appears anywhere, and [[Predict the Future from the Past, On the Temporal Data Distribution Shift in Financial Sentiment Classifications]] shows financial performance degrading measurably as the train-test gap widens, so an average over undated conditions is averaging over regimes that behave differently.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **Retrieval quality is still unbenchmarked in the financial LLM literature, and that is now a positive finding rather than a hole in my reading.** The most-cited holistic financial benchmark claims RAG evaluation in its abstract and delivers none, which means there is no external yardstick I can borrow for $A_F$ and no risk that a reviewer points at FinBen as prior coverage of it. The evaluation for the fundamental channel has to be built from [[FinanceBench, A New Benchmark for Financial Question Answering]]'s retrieval-conditioned design and [[Financial Report Chunking for Effective Retrieval Augmented Generation]]'s element-type chunking, scored on a hand-checked CSE probe set, and the absence of a comparable benchmark is worth one sentence in the proposal.
- **The prior for an LLM directional caller on the CSE is at or below chance, and this is now the second independent benchmark saying so.** Twelve models at MCC between -0.07 and 0.08 on US large caps with dense tweet and price history, beaten by a 2022 sequence model at 0.21, is the same picture PIXIU reported with a different model set. On a thinner market with sparser news the honest expectation is lower still, so $A_T$ is an econometric or RL component with an LLM nowhere in the directional path, and <font color="#ffc000">the orchestrator has to be able to drive a channel's weight to near zero</font>, which is what the pre-registered weight-entropy diagnostic exists to detect.
- **Report MCC beside F1 for every classification output and treat a near-zero MCC as a hard failure, not a weak result.** The risk-management block is nine datasets where a model that predicts one class for every row scores F1 up to 1.00, and if I report F1 alone for any component that produces labels I will reproduce exactly that. The rule for my own component evaluation is both metrics in the same table with a no-skill reference line, and the same discipline applied to the confidence scores the weighting function consumes.
- **Adopt the per-asset results table and an explicit interval definition in my own backtest, because this paper shows how quickly a headline breaks under recomputation.** What saved the trading claim from being unfalsifiable is Table 9, and what sank it is that Table 9 and Table 4 disagree. For the CSE backtest that means per-ticker cumulative return, Sharpe and drawdown printed in full, the interval construction stated in the caption, costs modelled rather than assumed away, and a stated test against Buy & Hold rather than a comparison of point estimates. The friction requirement in the execution sub-gap makes this stricter for me than for them, since a 28% cumulative return on ten US megacaps at zero cost would not survive CSE spreads.
- **The seven-aspect taxonomy is a coverage checklist for my agent set, not a scoring scheme.** Information extraction, textual analysis, QA, generation, risk management, forecasting and decision-making map onto what my four channels and the orchestrator have to do between them, and running my design against that list is a cheap way to find a capability nobody is responsible for. What I should not borrow is the aggregation, since FinBen has none, and my ablation needs a single comparable number per configuration to rank the three weighting schemes.
- **The FinTrade prompt contract is a template for the agent output schema, and the memory indices in it point back at FinMem.** Forcing a JSON object with a decision, a reason string and short, mid, long and reflection memory indices gives me a concrete starting shape for what each agent returns to the orchestrator, and it makes the explanation a required field rather than an afterthought, which is what the layered explainability requirement needs. Worth reading beside [[FinMe, A Performance-Enhanced LLM Trading Agent With Layered Memory and Character Design]], since that is the framework FinTrade is actually run through.
- **Budget for unparseable output as a first-class outcome and for a leakage check on CSE data.** The all-zero columns here are the same fourth outcome state I adopted from PIXIU, correct, incorrect, abstained and no parseable answer, confirmed at much larger scale, so the harness needs strict output validation and a logged failure bucket that feeds the weighting function a hard zero. The perplexity-difference test is worth running once on whatever CSE text corpus I assemble, with the caveat that the paper supplies no threshold, because Sri Lankan filings and news are thin enough that a frontier model having seen them is a real possibility I currently have no evidence either way on.
- **The cost line closes the question of scale for good.** 16 A100 80GB GPUs, roughly 600 hours and about $51,000 to evaluate fifteen models on 36 datasets, with three new datasets contributing 10.32% of the data. A CSE equivalent is not a side contribution of this project at any scale I can fund, and the defensible output is a stratified probe set on the order of what FinanceBench released, plus the evaluation protocol written down, rather than a benchmark.
