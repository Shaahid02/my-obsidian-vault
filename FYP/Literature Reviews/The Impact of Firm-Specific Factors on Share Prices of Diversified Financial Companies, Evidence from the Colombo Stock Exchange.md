South Asian Journal of Finance, Vol. 5 No. 1, 2025, pp. 67 to 83, Jayarangi and Buddhika, Department of Finance, Faculty of Commerce and Management Studies, University of Kelaniya, received November 2024 and accepted June 2025. A panel regression asking whether five firm-specific accounting ratios, return on equity, dividend per share, earnings per share, the price to earnings ratio and debt to total assets, explain the share prices of Diversified Financial companies listed on the Colombo Stock Exchange, estimated on <font color="#ffc000">200 firm-year observations covering the 20 largest of 43 sector firms from 2013 to 2023</font> with a random effects specification in STATA. DOI 10.4038/sajf.v5i1.113.

This is not a methodological template, there is no forecast, no holdout and no agent anywhere in it, and I'm reading it the same way I read the other local empirical work, as evidence about what the fundamental channel on this market can and cannot carry. Its value to me is specific and narrow. It is the most recent published statement of which accounting ratios associate with price on CSE financials, it covers a window that overlaps mine almost exactly, and in its own model summary table it reports the one number that decides how I should wire the fundamental agent into the orchestrator: <font color="#ffc000">an R² within of 0.090 against an R² between of 0.709</font>. The paper quotes neither, it quotes the overall 0.576 and calls it 58%, and that choice is the difference between a result about which counter is dearer than another and a result about when a counter moves. The first is what the data supports and it is useful to me. The second is what the conclusion claims.

> [!WARNING]
> **Two of this paper's own diagnostic tables contradict the text that reports them.** The normality section states that the Skewness/Kurtosis p-values are above 0.05 when the table it points at shows 0.000 in every cell, and the Hausman test is reported with a chi-square statistic of −1489.934, which is not a value the statistic can take. The random effects choice that produces every coefficient below rests on that second one. Scope everything in the findings accordingly, and see the limitations.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **The firm-specific determinants literature is developed-market and the Sri Lankan slice of it is thin and dated.** The authors anchor on Kumar (2017) for Indian autos, Nasif and Sharif (2010) for Amman commercial banks, Iqbal et al. (2016) for Kenyan energy and Sharif et al. (2014) for Bahrain, then argue that South Asian coverage generally and Sri Lankan coverage in particular is sparse, citing Dissanayake and Herath (2016) and Pandigamage and Ranaweera (2023) as near enough the whole local set.
- **The sector has not been looked at since the macroeconomic break.** Their sharpest framing point is that the only prior study of firm-specific factors on CSE diversified financials is a 2016 Central Bank staff study reporting percentage increments only, and that <font color="#ffc000">its results may no longer hold after the political and economic changes from 2020</font>. I'd put this more strongly than they do, since their own sample runs straight through the same break and is estimated as one pooled regime.
- **Diversified Financials is picked for growth, not for theory.** The sector is justified by earnings growing 43% and revenues 31% annually over 2021 to 2023 and by the sector being roughly 20% of total market capitalisation, per the Central Bank's 2023 industry analysis. That is a sampling rationale rather than a hypothesis, and it means the sector was selected on the outcome variable's recent behaviour, which is worth holding onto when reading the results.

#### Data and setup

Twenty companies, selected as the highest market capitalisation 20 of the 43 Diversified Financial firms listed on the CSE, LOLC Holdings at LKR 203.3b down to Alliance Finance at LKR 3.3b, over eleven years from 2013 to 2023. Data is hand-collected from the CSE website and from company annual reports. Share price is measured as the average share price of the month, return on equity as net income over average shareholders' equity, dividend per share as annual dividend over the weighted average share count, earnings per share as net profit over the same denominator, price to earnings as market value per share over earnings per share, debt to total assets as total liabilities over total assets, and firm size, the sole control, as the natural logarithm of total assets. The operationalisation table sources each measure to prior local work, Biyiri and Dissanayaka (2017), Irsath et al. (2015), Sritharan (2018) and Rajapaksha and Weerawickrama (2020).

Eleven years across twenty firms is 220 firm-years and every table reports 200, which is not explained anywhere in the paper.

The descriptive statistics are where the measurement problem becomes visible, and two columns in particular are worth looking at directly rather than taking the paper's summary of them.

Share price has a mean of 262.078 against a standard deviation of 458.181 and a range of Rs. 0.50 to Rs. 1,950, so the dependent variable is in nominal rupee levels and is dominated by a handful of high-priced counters. The price to earnings column is worse, <font color="#ff0000">a mean of −20.048 with a standard deviation of 1069.92 and a range from −1471.22 to 2292.82</font>, which is what happens when a ratio with earnings in the denominator is computed on firm-years where earnings are near zero or negative, and the EPS minimum of −21.65 confirms loss-making years are in the sample. Neither variable is winsorised, trimmed or transformed. Earnings per share averages 17.239 with a standard deviation of 53.818, dividend per share 4.66, return on equity 14.237%, debt to total assets 57.277% and log total assets 20.041.

The correlation matrix carries the other half of the problem, and the two cells I keep returning to are not in the share price row.

Against price, the univariate associations are dividend per share 0.535, earnings per share 0.509, price to earnings 0.319, debt to assets −0.502, firm size −0.404 and return on equity −0.160. Among the regressors, <font color="#ffc000">dividend per share and earnings per share correlate at 0.757 and debt to assets correlates with firm size at 0.726</font>, which is the structure that decides how the insignificant coefficients below should be read. Mean VIF is 2.23, maximum 2.81, and the paper treats that as settling the question.

#### Model and estimation

One pooled panel specification, estimated by random effects after a Hausman test, with share price in levels as the dependent variable:

$$SP_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 ROE_{it} + \beta_2 DPS_{it} + \beta_3 EPS_{it} + \beta_4 PE_{it} + \beta_5 DTA_{it} + \beta_6 \log(SIZE_{it}) + \varepsilon_{it}$$

where:
- $SP_{it}$ is the average monthly share price of firm $i$ in year $t$, in nominal rupees, undifferenced and untransformed
- $PE_{it}$ is the price to earnings ratio and $DTA_{it}$ is total liabilities over total assets
- $\log(SIZE_{it})$ is the natural logarithm of total assets, the only control, and the only variable entering in logs
- there are no year effects, no industry effects and no lagged dependent variable, and no standard error clustering is reported

The fourth regressor is the one that needs its own line, because the price to earnings ratio is defined by the paper itself as market value per share over earnings per share, which puts the dependent variable on both sides of the equation:

$$PE_{it} = \frac{SP_{it}}{EPS_{it}} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad SP_{it} = \beta_0 + \dots + \beta_4 \frac{SP_{it}}{EPS_{it}} + \dots + \varepsilon_{it}$$

This is not an omitted control or a specification preference, it is an accounting identity relating two of the variables in the regression, and nothing in the paper acknowledges it. Whatever $\beta_4$ is estimating, it is not the independent contribution of a valuation multiple to price.

The last piece of the setup worth stating precisely is what the two reported R² figures mean, since the gap between them is the single most useful number in the paper. For a panel estimator, the within figure is the fit against variation inside each firm's own time series and the between figure is the fit against the firm means:

$$R^2_{\text{within}} = \text{corr}^2\!\big(\hat{y}_{it} - \bar{\hat{y}}_i,\; y_{it} - \bar{y}_i\big), \qquad R^2_{\text{between}} = \text{corr}^2\!\big(\bar{\hat{y}}_i,\; \bar{y}_i\big)$$

A model that separates expensive firms from cheap ones but says nothing about a given firm's path through time produces exactly the pattern reported here, high between and near-zero within.

#### Findings

- **Earnings per share is the strongest result in the paper and survives every reading of it, at a coefficient of 1.887 with a standard error of 0.501, t = 3.76 and p = 0.000.** It is the only regressor whose univariate correlation with price (0.509), significance and sign all point the same way, and it is consistent with Sharif et al. (2015), Su (2002) and Zhu (2003), which the discussion section works through at length. The magnitude is the part the paper misreads: it describes this as the effect of a 1% increase, but nothing except firm size is in logs, so the coefficient is the effect of one more rupee of EPS, which is Rs. 1.887 of price. <font color="#ffc000">That is an implied marginal multiple of 1.887 against a sample where mean price over mean EPS is about 15.2</font>, so the levels regression is attributing almost all of the cross-sectional price level to something other than earnings, and reading the coefficient as a valuation multiple would understate the market's actual pricing by an order of magnitude.

![[Pasted image 20261004150004.png]]

- **Debt to total assets is negative and significant at −0.530, standard error 0.167, p = 0.002, and it is the result most entangled with the control.** One percentage point more leverage associates with Rs. 0.53 less price, which the authors read as investors discounting financial risk. The complication they don't raise is that debt to assets correlates 0.726 with firm size, firm size enters negatively at −1.136 and is insignificant at p = 0.262, and both have sizeable negative univariate correlations with price, −0.502 and −0.404. On this sample the leverage coefficient and the size coefficient are competing to explain the same cross-sectional variation and the design has no way to separate them.
- **The price to earnings coefficient is positive and significant at 0.673, p = 0.022, and it is the one I would not cite.** Given the identity above and a regressor whose standard deviation is 1069.92, I read this as a mechanical artefact rather than evidence about valuation multiples, and the fact that it reaches significance while dividend per share does not is a feature of the arithmetic rather than of the market.
- **The two variables the paper's own theory section is built around produce nothing.** Return on equity comes in at −0.142 with p = 0.575 and dividend per share at 2.102 with p = 0.248, so H1 and H2 are rejected, after a literature review spending four subsections on bird-in-hand, residual dividend policy, agency cost theory and dividend irrelevance. The authors handle the ROE null gracefully, placing it alongside Badruzaman (2020) and Zaman (2021) who also find negative or negligible ROE effects on price. The dividend null is left largely unexplained, which matters because DPS has the highest univariate correlation with price in the whole matrix at 0.535 and correlates 0.757 with EPS, so what the regression shows is that dividends add nothing *beyond earnings in this specification*, not that dividends are unrelated to price. The conclusion states the second.
- **The model summary is where the paper's headline claim and its evidence separate, and the table is unambiguous about it.** Overall R² is 0.576 and chi-square 64.394 with Prob > chi2 = 0.000, which the text reports as "the R square value lies at 58%" and "the overall model is significant". The same table reports R² within of 0.090 and R² between of 0.709. <font color="#ffc000">Nine percent of the year-to-year movement in a given company's share price is explained, against seventy-one percent of the differences between companies</font>, so the model is a cross-sectional pricing description that has very little to say about time.

#### Limitations

- **The normality section asserts the opposite of the table it cites, and the underlying data makes the assertion implausible anyway.** The text claims all six variables are normally distributed "because the p-values for the Skewness and Kurtosis tests are greater than 0.05", and Table 4 reports 0.000 for skewness and kurtosis on every variable and adjusted chi-square p-values of 0.000 or 0.001 throughout, which rejects normality for all of them. The same paragraph then says the chi-square p-values being below 0.05 confirms the assumption is met, so the two sentences contradict each other as well as the table. This is not a close call that the authors got wrong at the margin, a dependent variable ranging from Rs. 0.50 to Rs. 1,950 with a standard deviation 1.75 times its mean is visibly non-normal, and no transform is applied in response.
- **The Hausman statistic is negative, which means the test did not run, and the random effects choice rests entirely on it.** A chi-square test value of −1489.934 with a p-value of 1 is the standard symptom of the asymptotic assumptions failing, and STATA's own guidance is to re-run under a common variance assumption or use an overidentification test rather than to read the p-value. The paper reads the p-value, concludes random effects, and reports every coefficient from that estimator. Random effects additionally requires the firm effects to be uncorrelated with the regressors, which is hard to believe when two of the regressors are leverage and firm size, and the untested alternative is precisely the fixed effects specification whose within R² of 0.090 the model summary already hints at.
- **The sample is the twenty largest firms as of the end of the window, applied backwards over the whole window.** Selecting on terminal market capitalisation and then estimating on 2013 onwards keeps every firm that grew into the top twenty and drops every firm that fell out of it or delisted, which is survivorship combined with look-ahead selection, and it interacts badly with a dependent variable measured in price levels rather than returns. The missing twenty firm-years out of the expected 220 are never accounted for, and the model summary's mean dependent variable of 83.107 with a standard deviation of 63.883 cannot be reconciled with the share price mean of 262.078 and standard deviation of 458.181 in the descriptives, so the two tables appear to come from different runs or different transformations of the dependent variable.
- **Eleven years of nominal rupee prices are pooled as one regime across a currency collapse.** The window contains the 2018 constitutional crisis, the 2019 Easter attacks, COVID, the 2022 sovereign default and inflation peaking above 50%, and the dependent variable is an unadjusted nominal price with no year effects, no inflation adjustment and no regime split. A rupee of EPS in 2014 and a rupee of EPS in 2023 are not the same quantity, so part of what the coefficients are fitting is the unit of measurement changing underneath the panel. The paper's stated limitation is that macroeconomic variables are excluded, which frames this as an omitted-variable question when the sharper problem is comparability of the dependent variable across the sample.
- **Nothing in the design is evaluated out of sample, and the paper's recommendations are forecasting recommendations.** There is no train and test split, no holdout year, no rolling estimation and no forecast error reported anywhere, yet the abstract says the factors "can be used to make accurate future share price estimates" and the recommendations tell investors to monitor these ratios to "facilitate accurate forecasts of future share prices". An in-sample R² of 0.576 on a cross-section, with a within R² of 0.090, supports neither sentence. This is the gap I actually care about rather than a complaint about this paper specifically, since it is the standard shape of the local empirical literature.
- **The write-up has enough slips that the reported numbers need checking before any of them are quoted.** The variable definitions list renders dividend per share as "Dep_osit" and earnings per share as "Esp_rit", every marginal effect is described as the response to a 1% change when only firm size is logged, the conclusion mentions a panel unit root test finding "at least one panel had a unit root" that appears nowhere in the results, and Table 10 duplicates the p-values already in Table 9. None of these change a coefficient, and together they mean I should treat the figures as needing verification rather than as a clean reference.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **Earnings per share, leverage and firm size are the fundamental features with measured CSE association, and this is my citation for selecting them, scoped to cross-section.** On this sample, over this window, in this sector, EPS at p = 0.000 and debt to assets at p = 0.002 are the two that clear, and both are extractable from the income statement and balance sheet of any annual report. That is enough to justify the fundamental agent's feature set to a supervisor without my having to derive it from first principles, provided I say in the same sentence what the evidence is cross-sectional about.
- **The within R² of 0.090 is the number that decides the fundamental agent's role in the architecture.** Fundamentals on this market separate counters from each other far better than they explain any one counter's path, so the fundamental channel should produce a slowly-updating relative view across the universe rather than a timing signal, with the technical channel carrying the short horizon and the orchestrator capping how much the fundamental weight is allowed to move between reporting dates. Building it the other way round means asking a channel with nine percent within-firm explanatory power to drive entry and exit.
- **The price to earnings identity is a direct warning for my own feature construction.** Any ratio with price in the numerator fed into a model whose target is price or return carries a mechanical link to the label, and on a thin market where the denominator goes near zero it also carries a variance of the kind that produced a standard deviation of 1069.92 here. The fundamental agent's inputs should be restricted to quantities computed from the filings alone, with price-derived multiples either excluded or constructed against a lagged price and reported as such.
- **Universe construction has to be point-in-time, and this paper is the concrete example of what happens otherwise.** Selecting the top twenty by 2023 capitalisation and backfilling to 2013 is the exact failure my own screen could reproduce if the liquidity filter is computed once over the full history rather than as of each rebalance date. Taken with the delisting control in [[Relationship between firm-level investment, idiosyncratic risk and ownership, an emerging market perspective]], which does retain delisted firms, I now have both the right and the wrong version of this decision documented on the same market.
- **The nominal-price problem across 2020 to 2023 is a reason to target returns and to condition on regime, not merely to add controls.** My index track runs 1 July 2014 to 30 June 2024 and contains the same break, so any fundamental feature in rupee units needs either deflating or expressing as a ratio, and the orchestrator's case for conditioning on measured state gets another supporting instance. This is now the third CSE result pointing the same way, alongside the macro linkages breaking over 2014 to 2022 in [[The Relationship between Macroeconomic Variables, Trading Volume and Stock Returns, Granger Causality Evidence from the Colombo Stock Exchange]] and the 2021 extension halving the investment-risk coefficient in the Fernando note.
- **Return on equity and dividend per share failing to clear is an argument for abstention rather than for dropping them.** The honest statement is that neither adds anything beyond earnings in a six-regressor levels specification on 200 observations, which is not the same as neither mattering, and a fundamental agent that must emit a directional view on every counter at every step will manufacture signal out of exactly these variables. Letting the channel return no view when its inputs are collinear or missing is the design consequence, and it is the same abstention argument the liquidity screen produces for the technical channel.
- **Twenty firms over eleven years, hand-collected from annual reports, is another data point on what fundamental extraction costs on this market.** Two authors with institutional access pulled six variables for 200 firm-years by hand, which is the same message the Fernando note delivers from 167 firms, and it is why the retrieval pipeline over CSE filings is the component I expect to be the bottleneck rather than any model. Structure-aware chunking of the kind in [[Financial Report Chunking for Effective Retrieval Augmented Generation]] reduces that cost without removing it.
- **This is a usable published baseline for the fundamental channel, and a weak one, which is what makes it useful.** A linear six-variable model on CSE diversified financials with an in-sample overall R² of 0.576 and a within R² of 0.090 is a reference point my fundamental agent should beat out of sample, and since the paper reports no out-of-sample result at all, establishing one on a comparable universe is a contribution I can state plainly in the proposal rather than having to argue for.
