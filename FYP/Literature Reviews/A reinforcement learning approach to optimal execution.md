Quantitative Finance 22(6), 2022, 1051-1069, Moallemi and Wang, both at Columbia Graduate School of Business, received May 2021 and published online March 2022. The paper takes the <font color="#ffc000">child-order execution problem</font>, the last atomic step after a parent order has already been sliced, and formulates it as an <font color="#ffff00">optimal stopping problem</font>: the trader holds one indivisible share, has a horizon of $T$ seconds, and at each second chooses CONTINUE or STOP. Two data-driven solutions are built on one neural architecture, a supervised learning method that forecasts the entire future price change trajectory and acts on it, and a reinforcement learning method that learns the continuation value directly. Both are trained with a family of temporal difference updates, TD($m$-step), with one-step TD at one end and empirical Monte Carlo at the other, so the paper is really four algorithm families rather than two. Everything is tested on NASDAQ ITCH level III data covering 50 stocks over all of 2013. No code release is mentioned.

This is the first paper in the vault that is purely about execution rather than allocation, and it sits directly underneath everything else I have read. [[MacroHFT, Memory Augmented Context-aware Reinforcement Learning On High Frequency Trading]], [[FreQuant, A Reinforcement-Learning based Adaptive Portfolio Optimization with Multi-frequency Decomposition]] and [[Online portfolio selection with state-dependent price estimators and transaction costs]] all emit a target position and then charge a flat percentage for arriving at it, and this paper is the layer that flat percentage is standing in for. The distance from my own build is large and worth stating plainly: this is a single agent making a single binary decision over sixty seconds in the most liquid equity market in the world, with no portfolio, no allocation, no orchestration and no heterogeneous components. What I want from it is narrower than its subject: the evaluation unit, the cross-asset training protocol, the microstructure feature list, and a citation for the fact that even the most careful execution-timing work in the literature assumes market impact away.

> [!NOTE]
> **The unit of measurement is the half-spread, and this matters more than it looks.** Every result in the paper is a price gain expressed as a percentage of the stock's half-spread, where the half-spread is taken as the time-averaged difference between best bid and best ask over the previous 5 trading days, halved. A gain of 100% means the trade executed at the mid-price, so the trader crossed no spread at all and traded frictionlessly. The reported 25.47% therefore means roughly a quarter of the half-spread was recovered relative to executing immediately at $t=0$, which is the implicit zero baseline. The normalization is what makes 50 stocks with average spreads ranging from 1 tick to more than 54 ticks comparable at all, and it is also what makes the headline number market-agnostic in a way the underlying cash amount is not.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **The optimal execution literature treats the child order as solved.** Almgren and Chriss (2000) and Bertsimas and Lo (1998) set up the parent-order problem, trading off market impact against price risk over hours or days, and the papers following them inherit that framing. The authors' position is that once the parent order has been sliced, each child order is a separate problem on a separate timescale, seconds to minutes rather than hours to days, where impact is negligible because the order is small and the only question left is when within the window to fire. That reframing is the paper's main move and I find it convincing as a decomposition, though it is a modelling choice rather than a result, and the paper never tests whether the two problems can be separated without loss.
- **Classical execution models assume future prices are unpredictable, and the empirical evidence says otherwise.** The paper leans on Sirignano (2019), who predicts the direction of the next price move from limit order book state with a neural network and reports significant predictability, to argue that a model assuming a martingale price is leaving the entire timing decision on the table. If the price is predictable at all at the one-second scale, then a seller who expects the price to rise has a reason to wait, and the classical framework has no mechanism to express that.
- **The nearest prior RL work solves the wrong sub-problem.** Nevmyvaka, Feng and Kearns (2006) is the closest predecessor, but it applies RL to the parent-order scheduling problem with tabular state representation, which forces discretization of the state variables. Working on the child order lets the authors reduce the problem to optimal stopping and use continuous state variables through a neural network, which is a fair delta, though it is a difference in scope and representation rather than a demonstration that the earlier approach fails.
- **The optimal stopping machinery the paper borrows from was built for a different shape of problem.** Longstaff and Schwartz (2001) and Tsitsiklis and Van Roy (2001), developed for American option pricing, estimate the continuation value by backward induction with a new linear regression at each time step, so the number of models grows with the horizon and nothing is shared across it, while the deep stopping line of Becker et al. (2019, 2020, 2021) parameterizes the stopping rule directly as a sequence of binary choices rather than learning a value. The paper's position is one nonlinear network covering all horizons at once, learning the value and inducing the policy from it, which is the architectural claim and the part I would actually cite. That both alternatives are described and neither is run means the superiority of this route over either is asserted rather than tested.

#### Data and setup

| Item | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Source | NASDAQ ITCH 4.1 level III, all events, LOB reconstructed from messages |
| Period | Full year 2013, 252 trading days |
| Universe | 50 high-liquidity stocks, spreads from 1 to 54.36 ticks |
| Episodes | 100 per stock per day, start uniform between 10:00 and 15:30 NY |
| Horizon | 60 one-second intervals, so $T = 60$ |
| Split | 60 / 20 / 20 train / validation / test, randomized at episode level |
| Regimes | Stock-specific, universal (all 50 pooled), take-one-out (train on 49, test on the 50th) |

What to look for in that table is the split row and the regime row. The randomization is at the episode level rather than by date, so episodes from one day land in different folds and future days can be used to predict past days. The authors justify this by arguing that with a one-minute horizon and microstructure features, cross-day predictability is negligible, and they are explicit that within-day episodes never straddle folds so no look-ahead occurs inside a day. The justification is reasonable and it is still an assumption they state rather than a check they run.

The state at time $t$ is not a single snapshot: ten sets of features are collected, one at the current second and one at each of the previous nine, so $x_t = (s_{t-9}, \ldots, s_t)$. The features are grouped as general information (time of day, price normalized by average spread), spread normalized by return and price volatility, depth at the top 5 levels with queue imbalance, trading flow counts in the last second, and exponentially decaying intensity measures for trades and price changes. Everything is defined relative to near-side and far-side rather than bid and ask, so a buy and a sell episode look identical to the network. Queue imbalance is the one feature I would port directly and its sign convention is easy to get wrong:

$$QI = \frac{\text{near depth} - \text{far depth}}{\text{near depth} + \text{far depth}}$$

where near depth is resting size on the side the execution needs to hit and far depth is the opposite side, so $QI \in [-1, 1]$ and it is computed separately at each of the top 5 price levels and also on aggregated depth.

The network is an RNN with 10 LSTM units of dimension 64, one per feature set, feeding 5 fully connected layers with batch normalization into an output layer of dimension 60 matching the horizon. Learning rate $3 \times 10^{-5}$, batch size 1024, target network copied every 1000 gradient steps, TD step size $m = 15$ for the headline results, 50,000 gradient steps, 10 random starts per configuration. A single TensorFlow implementation is printed in the appendix.

#### Model architecture and training

The SL method trains the network to output the price change trajectory $[\Delta p_1, \ldots, \Delta p_T]$ from the current state under MSE loss, then induces a policy by summing predicted changes forward and continuing only while the best achievable cumulative rise is strictly positive. The RL method replaces the prediction target with the <font color="#ffc000">continuation value</font>, the expected best remaining gain conditional on not stopping now, which satisfies

$$C_{t:T}(x) = \mathbb{E}\big[\Delta p_{t+1} + \max\{0,\ C_{t+1:T}(x_{t+1})\}\ \big|\ x_t = x\big]$$

where:
- $C_{t:T}(x)$ is the continuation value at time $t$ in state $x$ with the horizon ending at $T$, and $C_{T:T} \triangleq 0$
- $\Delta p_{t+1} = p_{t+1} - p_t$ is the next one-second price change
- the inner $\max\{0, \cdot\}$ is where the policy enters the value, since a trader who can stop never accepts a negative continuation

The policy is then CONTINUE while $C_{t:T}(x) > 0$ and STOP otherwise. Why this differs from SL is set out in a two-scenario counterexample that is the clearest paragraph in the paper: with price change trajectories $\Delta P_A = [+1, -4]$ and $\Delta P_B = [-2, +3]$ occurring with equal probability, the ex-post optimal execution earns $+1$ under either scenario by stopping at different times, while the MSE-minimizing prediction is the average $[-0.5, -0.5]$, which tells the trader to sell immediately for a gain of $0$. SL averages trajectories and then acts, RL averages over the actions the trader would actually take, and the gap between those two is the entire case for the RL method.

The architectural contribution is making the network respect a known property of the value function. Because a longer horizon can only help a stopping problem, the continuation value increments are non-negative:

$$\delta_i(x) \triangleq C_{t:t+i}(x) - C_{t:t+i-1}(x) \geq 0 \ \ (i > 1), \qquad \hat C^{\phi}_{t:T}(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{T-t} u_i^{\phi}(x)$$

so the 60 output neurons predict the increments rather than the values, softplus is applied to all of them except the first, and the continuation value for any horizon is read off as a partial sum of the same output vector. One network covers all 60 horizons, monotonicity is enforced by construction rather than learned, and only the first increment is allowed to be negative because a one-step-ahead expected price change genuinely can be.

Training targets come from the TD($m$-step) family. The empirical continuation value takes $m$ real price changes, gated by whether the current policy would still be holding, and then substitutes the target network's own estimate for everything beyond:

$$\tilde C_{t:T}(x_t) = \Delta p_{t+1} + \sum_{i=2}^{m}\Delta p_{t+i}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathbb{1}\{\hat C^{\phi}_{t+j:T}(x_{t+j}) > 0\} + \hat C^{\phi'}_{t+m:T}(x_{t+m})\prod_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{1}\{\hat C^{\phi}_{t+j:T}(x_{t+j}) > 0\}$$

where $\phi$ is the train-net and $\phi'$ the target-net. Setting $m = T$ recovers Monte Carlo and $m = 1$ recovers one-step TD, so the whole spectrum is one hyperparameter. The indicator uses the train-net while the accumulated value comes from the target-net, which is the double Q-learning construction of van Hasselt (2010), there to stop the maximization from systematically overestimating the continuation value. One update needs only a $(2m+1)$-tuple, which is why a single 60-second episode supplies many training samples.

The discussion section brackets the target: the SL policy is a model predictive control relaxation that replaces the random future with its mean and so underestimates the true continuation value, $C^{MPC}_{t:T} \leq C_{t:T}$, while an information relaxation giving perfect foresight overestimates it, $C_{t:T} \leq C^{IR}_{t:T}$, and the RL method is aiming at the quantity in between.

#### Findings

- **RL beats SL under every regime, and the margin is stable at around 15%.** Stock-specific gives 21.40 (0.15) for SL against 24.82 (0.16) for RL, universal gives 22.34 (0.15) against 25.47 (0.16), so the improvements are 16% and 14% respectively. With standard errors of 0.15 to 0.16 on differences of roughly 3 percentage points the gap is many standard errors wide and I am comfortable treating it as real on this sample, scoped to what is being compared, which is RL against the paper's own SL variant with both measured from an immediate-execution baseline of zero.

  ![[Pasted image 20260923232304.png]]

- **Training across stocks beats training on the stock itself, and a completely held-out stock does nearly as well.** The universal model improves on the stock-specific one by 4.4% for SL and 2.6% for RL, and the take-one-out regime, where the model never sees a single episode from the test stock, returns 24.85 (0.16) against the stock-specific 24.82 (0.16). On this universe the experience transfers essentially without loss. The authors read this as evidence of universality in the price formation process, and I would scope that more tightly: these are 50 high-liquidity NASDAQ names in one year, and the half-spread normalization is doing some of the work by removing the largest cross-sectional difference between them before the comparison is made. The result is consistent with universality and is not a test of it against a market where the microstructure genuinely differs.

- **RL converges in far more gradient steps than SL, and that ordering reverses entirely once measured in wall-clock time.** In gradient steps SL is at its plateau of roughly 20 to 22 within about 10,000 steps for every $m$ except $m=1$, while RL takes appreciably longer and RL-TD(1-step) is the slowest of all.

  ![[Pasted image 20260923232301.png]]

  Measured in seconds the picture inverts: RL-TD(1-step) reaches roughly 20 within about 400 seconds while RL-TD(30-step) is still near 5 at 800 seconds and RL-TD(45-step) and RL-TD(60-step) have not left their starting level at all. The mechanism is in the loss functions, since SL-TD($m$-step) needs two network evaluations regardless of $m$ while RL-TD($m$-step) needs $m$ of them, so RL gradient step time scales roughly proportionally with $m$ while SL gradient step time stays flat. Given a fixed time budget, TD(1) is the best RL configuration in this experiment by a wide margin.

  ![[Pasted image 20260923232302.png]]

- **TD(1) is also the most data-efficient configuration for both methods, which is the finding most relevant to a short-history market.** Counting a time instance as accessed when either its price or its state variable is used, TD($m$-step) consumes $m$ instances per gradient step, so on a fixed archive TD(1) takes $m$ times as many steps, and the paper's price-gain-against-data-accessed curves put it clearly ahead for both SL and RL across the full 50,000 range. The authors draw the implication for finance explicitly, that when non-stationarity limits usable history to a recent window, the more data-efficient algorithm supports a more complex model on the same span of time, and that argument transfers directly to any market where the archive is short rather than the regime unstable.

- **The bias-variance tradeoff across the TD spectrum is measured rather than asserted, and larger steps overfit.** Using an empirical decomposition over 100 training datasets, prediction standard deviation rises from 23.1 at $m=1$ to 34.9 at $m=60$ while noise standard deviation squared bias falls from 26.9 to 18.0, with total RMSE roughly flat from 35.5 to 39.3, so a larger TD step buys lower bias at the cost of higher variance. This is the pattern Kearns and Singh (2000) predict and the paper confirms it in its own setting.

  ![[Pasted image 20260923232303.png]]

  The overfitting consequence is shown separately on a small training set, where TD(60-step) drives in-sample RMSE down steadily while its out-of-sample RMSE climbs, and TD(1-step) and TD(30-step) do not separate nearly as much. The regularization reading the paper offers, that TD constrains predictions to satisfy a martingale property in the SL case and the Bellman equation in the RL case, is a clean explanation for why the small-step variants generalize better here.

- **The paper's own summary refuses to name a winner, and I think that is the correct read of its evidence.** Maximum performance favours universal RL, running time favours SL with Monte Carlo updates and RL with TD(1), data efficiency favours TD(1) for both, and overfitting resistance favours small TD steps. Four criteria, three different answers, and the honest conclusion is that the algorithm choice is a function of which resource binds.

#### Limitations

- **The zero-impact assumption is stated as a modelling convenience and is the thing that does not survive transport to a thin book.** The paper is explicit that the order is infinitesimal and that any action of the trader has no effect on the price process, the states or the transition kernel, which is what makes the problem a pure stopping problem rather than a control problem. On 50 high-liquidity NASDAQ names with a one-share order that assumption is close to harmless. In a market where a single child order can be a visible fraction of resting depth at the touch, the act of stopping changes the state that the next decision would have been conditioned on, and <font color="#ff0000">the entire optimal stopping formulation stops being the right object</font>. This is prior art for the problem I want to work on rather than a method I can lift, and it is the single most important thing about the paper for my purposes.

- **No order type, no queue position, no partial fills, no fees.** STOP is assumed to produce an immediate fill at the observed price, which implicitly means a marketable order crossing the spread, and that is why the half-spread is the natural unit. There is no treatment of posting passively and losing queue priority, no partial execution, no cancellation, and no commission or exchange fee anywhere in the objective. The paper builds the limit order book in full from ITCH messages and then uses it only as a feature source, never as a fill simulator, so the reported gains are upper bounds on what the same policy would achieve against a book that has to be interacted with rather than observed.

- **There is no non-neural baseline anywhere in the paper.** The comparison set is SL against RL across five TD step sizes, and the implicit floor is immediate execution at $t=0$, which scores zero by construction. There is no threshold rule on queue imbalance, no momentum heuristic, no fixed-delay rule, no random stopping control and no TWAP-style reference, so nothing establishes that the neural machinery is necessary rather than merely sufficient. A queue-imbalance threshold is a one-line policy over a feature the paper already computes, and its absence is the gap between "an RNN trained this way recovers a quarter of the half-spread" and "this problem needs an RNN", which is the question I would have to answer before spending the same effort on a much smaller market.

- **One year, one exchange, one liquidity tier, no walk-forward, and a universality claim demonstrated inside a set selected for homogeneity.** All results come from 2013, split randomly at the episode level within that year, with no out-of-period test and no rolling retrain, so whatever was learned about 2013 microstructure is never tested on 2014 even though the paper's own data-efficiency argument rests on markets being non-stationary over months and years. The same narrowness limits what the transfer result means: the 50 stocks were chosen for high liquidity, they trade on one venue under one set of matching rules, and the metric normalizes away the spread differences between them before any comparison happens, so the take-one-out figure of 24.85 against 24.82 is evidence of transfer within a tight band rather than evidence that a model trained on NASDAQ megacaps carries to a market with different tick rules, hours, participant mix and spreads measured in percent rather than ticks.

- **The training protocol has no stopping criterion and no reported search, so the headline numbers carry an unmeasured selection component.** Because the RL target values depend on the induced policy, which changes as the network trains, the MSE loss is not a meaningful convergence signal, so progress is monitored by applying the current policy to held-out episodes and reading off the price gain. That is an honest workaround, and it leaves the 50,000-step budget as a choice rather than a result. Tuning is described only as a few architectures and a few values per hyperparameter with the winners reported, learning rate $3 \times 10^{-5}$, batch 1024, target copy every 1000 steps and $m = 15$, all selected on one validation set with no grid or configuration count given. The standard errors in the headline table also describe variation across episodes and stocks rather than across training runs, even though 10 random starts were used for the convergence figures, so nothing in the paper quantifies run-to-run spread in the reported gain.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **This is the paper that names the layer my cost model is currently a placeholder for, and its assumption is exactly the one my execution-friction sub-gap has to break.** Every portfolio paper in the vault, and my own design so far, converts a target weight into a fill at a flat percentage. Moallemi and Wang show what the layer underneath actually contains, and they solve it cleanly by assuming the order is small enough not to move the book. In a thin frontier market that assumption is precisely what fails, so the paper is a strong citation for the framing that careful execution work exists but is built for a liquidity regime the CSE does not occupy. That is a better argument for the sub-gap than the absence of literature would be, because it identifies what is there and why it does not transfer.

- **Adopt the half-spread as the reporting unit for execution quality, and report the cash figure beside it.** Expressing execution against mid-price in units of the half-spread is the cleanest solution I have seen to comparing execution across counters with wildly different spreads, and the CSE spread dispersion is worse than the 1-to-54-tick range here. The caveat that comes with it is the mirror image of the paper's: because a CSE half-spread is a large number in basis points, a gain of 20% of it is economically far more significant there than the same percentage on AAPL, so the normalized figure and the basis-point figure have to appear together or the result will be read wrongly in either direction.

- **The take-one-out regime is the experimental protocol I should be using, and it answers the short-history problem directly.** With few liquid counters and possibly only twelve to eighteen months of usable history, per-counter training is not viable. Training across the cross-section and testing on a completely held-out counter is a protocol that both uses the data efficiently and produces a defensible generalization claim, and this paper shows it losing essentially nothing against per-asset training on its own universe. Whether that holds on a market where the counters are genuinely heterogeneous is an open question and it is a cheap one to test, so this goes into the evaluation design alongside the hard-router baseline from the SOPS note.

- **Use TD(1) wherever data rather than compute is the binding constraint, which for me is everywhere.** The data-efficiency result is the most portable finding in the paper and it has a mechanism behind it rather than being an empirical accident: a one-step update consumes one time instance and therefore extracts more gradient steps from a fixed archive. My constraint is a short history, not a slow machine, so the default should be one-step TD in any temporal-difference component I build, with larger steps treated as something to justify rather than the other way round.

- **Encode known structure in the output layer rather than hoping the network learns it.** The monotone-increment construction with softplus, where 60 outputs are increments and any horizon's value is a partial sum, gets one network covering the whole horizon and gets a property that must hold for free. The analogous move in my orchestrator is on the weight vector: a simplex constraint, non-negativity and any monotonicity I can establish belong in the architecture rather than in the loss as a penalty term. This is the same lesson as the log-barrier discussion in the SOPS note approached from the network side.

- **The LOB feature list is a ready-made specification, and it is gated on data I have not confirmed exists.** Queue imbalance at the top 5 levels, near and far depth normalized by trailing 21-day volume, trade and price-change counts in the last second, and exponentially decaying intensity measures are a compact and well-motivated set, and the near-side and far-side convention makes a buy and a sell episode look identical to the model, which halves what has to be learned. All of it needs level-III message data. <font color="#ff0000">Whether the CSE exposes anything below top-of-book is unknown</font>, and `cse.lk/api/chartData` almost certainly does not, so this belongs in the data-access letter to CSE R&D as a specific ask rather than sitting as an assumption in my design.

- **An execution-timing agent is a cheap addition to the orchestrator, and the SL counterexample is the reason it should be value-based.** A network making one binary decision over a short window is small next to a full RL allocator, so adding execution timing as a component is affordable if the data arrives. The two-scenario example is the thing to carry into that design: a component that predicts prices and then acts on the prediction will average away exactly the scenarios a trader could have avoided, so the execution component should learn the value of waiting rather than forecast the price and threshold it. The same argument applies to how the orchestrator itself is trained, since averaging over predicted states and averaging over the actions taken in those states are different objects.

- **The MPC and information-relaxation bracketing gives me a sanity check that costs almost nothing to compute.** The deterministic-future policy underestimates the true continuation value and the perfect-foresight policy overestimates it, so any learned value sitting outside that interval is a bug rather than a result. Both bounds are computable from the same backtest data I would already have, and a bracketing diagnostic of this kind is worth more than another performance table when the thing being validated is a learned value function with no ground truth.
