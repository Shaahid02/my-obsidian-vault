Sri Lankan Journal of Banking and Finance, Volume 8 Issue 01, June 2025, pp. 84 to 102, Somathilaka (University of Vavuniya) and Hewamana (Wayamba University of Sri Lanka). An ARDL bounds-testing study of whether five macroeconomic stability variables, GDP, inflation, money supply, interest rate and exchange rate, drive what the authors call stock price volatility on the Colombo Stock Exchange, using 120 monthly observations from January 2010 to December 2019. DOI 10.4038/sljbf.v8i1.68.

This is the most complete single-framework macro study of the CSE I have, five variables in one model rather than the two or three that the earlier local impact literature usually runs, and the review section is the best consolidated map of Sri Lankan macro-market work I've found in one place, which is most of what I'm taking from it. The empirical half is a different matter. <font color="#ffc000">The dependent variable is not volatility</font>, it is the monthly change in the ASPI, which is a return, and once that's clear every substantive interpretation in the paper has to be re-read as a statement about returns wearing the word volatility. I read it as a useful survey and a cautionary specification rather than a result I can cite for a volatility claim, which puts it in the same bracket as [[Machine Learning-Driven Sri Lankan Stock Market Prediction, Harnessing Economic Indicators and Sentiment Analysis]], strong on the local input side, unusable on the protocol side.

> [!WARNING]
> **The proxy and the construct never meet.** Table 1 defines stock price volatility as "upward and downward movements of the stock market prices" and measures it as monthly change in ASPI. A signed one-period change has no dispersion in it at all, so the paper's finding that higher interest rates reduce "volatility" is, on its own measurement, the conventional and unsurprising finding that higher rates reduce returns. Every volatility claim in the abstract and conclusion is scoped to this.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **The Sri Lankan macro literature runs variables in twos and threes, not as one system.** The review covers Menike (2006) on inflation, Treasury bills and exchange rate, Gunasekarage et al. (2004) on money supply and CPI, Wickremasinghe (2011) on variance decomposition, Shafana (2014) on sectoral interest rate exposure, Hemamala and Jameel (2016) on inflation under bivariate versus multivariate specifications, and Badullahewage (2018) on combined macro effects, and the authors' reading is that almost none of them put all five stability variables into a single research framework, which is fair on the evidence they present.
- **Local work targets performance rather than volatility.** The paper is explicit that most CSE research examines stock market performance rather than volatility directly, and that volatility is the risk quantity investors actually need. The framing is right even though the execution collapses the distinction the framing depends on.
- **Long-horizon dynamics specifically.** Fernando (2017) used VECM and EGARCH and Hewamana et al. (2022) used EGARCH on asymmetric information, both short-run conditional variance machinery, so the claimed contribution is the long-run cointegrating question, which is what the ARDL bounds procedure is there to answer.

#### Data and setup

Monthly series from January 2010 to December 2019, <font color="#ffc000">120 observations per variable, 117 after lags</font>, no missing values reported. The decade is chosen deliberately to exclude COVID-19, which the authors present as avoiding extreme events.

- **Dependent**: monthly change in the All Share Price Index, from CSE market data.
- **Independent**, all from CBSL: GDP growth rate, inflation via the CCPI, the Weighted Average Deposit Rate as the interest rate, broad money M2, and the USD/LKR exchange rate.
- **GDP is quarterly** and was converted to monthly with the <font color="#ffff00">Quadratic Interpolation Method</font> in EViews, citing Ju et al. (2014).

Stationarity was checked with ADF, Phillips-Perron and KPSS at level, first difference and second difference. The exchange rate comes out non-stationary at first difference under KPSS, and the interest rate is non-stationary at level under PP and again at second difference under KPSS, so the orders of integration are mixed and inconsistent across tests. That inconsistency is the paper's stated reason for choosing ARDL over OLS, which is half right, ARDL does tolerate a mix of I(0) and I(1), and it is exactly that second-difference result that should have stopped the procedure rather than justified it.

#### Model and estimation

The Pesaran et al. (2001) bounds procedure estimates a conditional error-correction form and then tests whether the level terms are jointly zero, which is what makes it possible to ask about a long-run relationship without first committing to a single order of integration:

$$\Delta y_t = \alpha_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\beta_i\,\Delta y_{t-i} + \sum_{j=1}^{5}\sum_{i=0}^{q_j}\gamma_{ji}\,\Delta x_{j,t-i} + \delta_0\,y_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{5}\delta_j\,x_{j,t-1} + \varepsilon_t$$

where:
- $y_t$ is the ASPI monthly change and $x_{j,t}$ are the five macro variables, all in logs
- $\delta_0,\dots,\delta_5$ are the level coefficients, and the bounds F-test is on $H_0:\delta_0=\delta_1=\cdots=\delta_5=0$
- $p$ and the $q_j$ are the lag orders, which the paper never states and never reports a selection criterion for, although the fitted table implies something like ARDL(1,2,0,0,1,0)

The F-statistic comes out at <font color="#ffc000">14.36187 with k = 5</font>, against an upper I(1) bound of 4.15 asymptotically and 4.587 in the finite-sample (n = 80) critical values the output also prints, so cointegration is confirmed comfortably at 1% on either set.

![[Pasted image 20260925120002.png]]

The long-run relation recovered from that error-correction term is where the paper's own conclusion starts to come apart, so it's worth writing out:

$$\text{STOCKV} = 0.7673\,\text{EX} + 0.0574\,\text{GDPMN} + 2.5493\,\text{INF} + 0.0368\,\text{INT} - 0.3864\,\text{MS} - 0.0002$$

Inflation carries by far the largest long-run coefficient at 2.549274 with p = 0.05, the exchange rate is next at 0.767264 with p = 0.0734, and interest rate, money supply and GDP are nowhere, at p = 0.9168, 0.7497 and 0.287.

#### Findings

- **The bounds test confirms a long-run relationship and the paper then rejects one.** F = 14.36187 clears the 1% upper bound by a factor of three, which is the procedure saying the five variables and the index move together in levels, yet the discussion rejects H1 and the abstract states that CSE price volatility "does not show a long-term impact from macroeconomic stability variables". <font color="#ff0000">Those two statements are about the same estimation and they contradict each other</font>, and the paper never reconciles them.
- **The exchange rate at a two-month lag is the strongest single result, 0.583956 with p = 0.0015.** A 1% depreciation of the rupee is followed two months later by a 0.58% rise in the dependent variable, which the authors tie to the flow-oriented model of Dornbusch and Fischer (1980) working through trade balances and corporate earnings, and to Nouzad (2009) on exchange rate volatility feeding return volatility. The two-month delay is read as information diffusion lag in an emerging market, which is plausible and untested here.
- **The interest rate at a one-month lag is the only other significant term, −13.8453 with p = 0.0122.** In a log-log specification that coefficient is an elasticity, so it reads as a 1% rise in the WADR being followed by roughly a 13.8% fall in the dependent variable, which is an implausible magnitude for a monthly series, and the paper instead describes it as a "13.84-unit decrease", a reading the log form doesn't support.
- **GDP, inflation and money supply all fail in the fitted equation**, at p = 0.0716, 0.1948 and 0.9152 respectively, so three of the five stability variables contribute nothing at conventional levels, and the authors conclude their roles may be limited or mediated by other factors.
- **The lagged dependent variable is flat, 0.015096 with p = 0.8698**, and the Durbin-Watson statistic is 1.944936, which the paper reads as the absence of autocorrelation and treats as a clean result.
- **The model explains very little, R² = 0.300153 falling to an adjusted 0.189286 on 117 observations.** The authors are upfront about this and read the residual as evidence for information asymmetry, investor sentiment and irrational market behaviour in the CSE, which is the most interesting sentence in the paper and also the one with no test behind it.

#### Limitations

- **The construct and the measurement are different quantities, and this propagates into every interpretation.** Monthly change in the ASPI is a signed return, and the dispersion measure the paper's own definitions describe is something else entirely:

  $$\text{STOCKV}_t = \frac{\text{ASPI}_t - \text{ASPI}_{t-1}}{\text{ASPI}_{t-1}} \qquad\text{against}\qquad \hat{\sigma}_t = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_t-1}\sum_{d=1}^{n_t}\left(r_{t,d}-\bar{r}_t\right)^2}$$

  where $r_{t,d}$ is the daily return on day $d$ of month $t$ and $n_t$ the number of trading days. The flat lagged dependent variable is the diagnostic, because a genuine monthly volatility series on the CSE would show strong positive autocorrelation, that being what volatility clustering means and what [[Modeling Long-Term Volatility Memory Dynamics in the Colombo Stock Exchange]] finds persists on ASPI across all three regimes it tests. A near-zero AR(1) term with p = 0.8698 is consistent with a return series and inconsistent with a volatility series, so the paper's cleanest internal evidence is against its own dependent variable. There's a second unresolved question underneath it, since monthly index changes are frequently negative and the specification is in logs, and the log of a negative number is undefined, so something was done to the series, absolute values or a rebase, that the paper never states. The reported mean of the dependent variable, −0.93762, doesn't fit a percentage-change series either.
- **The short-run and long-run coefficients are used interchangeably.** The exchange rate and interest rate coefficients are lagged terms in the conditional equation, so they are short-run dynamics, and the discussion presents them as establishing "a statistically significant long-term relationship", while the abstract calls the same two variables short-term effects. Meanwhile the actual long-run vector puts inflation at 2.549274 with p = 0.05 as the only term with a claim to significance, and inflation is not discussed in the results at all. On this evidence the paper's headline conclusion doesn't follow from the table it's drawn from, regardless of what the correct conclusion would be.
- **If any variable is genuinely I(2) the bounds F-distribution is invalid, and the paper's evidence points that way.** Pesaran's critical values are derived for regressors integrated of order zero or one, and the unit root section reports results at second difference and describes the interest rate as non-stationary there under KPSS, so the possibility that the procedure was applied outside its assumptions is raised by the paper's own table and then used as the argument for applying it. No test of the I(2) case appears.
- **Diagnostics stop at Durbin-Watson.** There's no CUSUM or CUSUMSQ stability test, which is standard practice for ARDL over a decade that contains a constitutional crisis and the 2019 Easter attacks, no Breusch-Godfrey test for higher-order serial correlation, no heteroskedasticity or normality test on the residuals, no reported error-correction term speed of adjustment, and no lag selection criterion. DW alone on a specification with a lagged dependent variable is also the one test that is known to be biased toward 2 in exactly that setting.
- **Interpolating GDP inflates the apparent sample.** Quarterly GDP spread across months by quadratic interpolation produces 120 monthly figures out of 40 independent observations, and the interpolation imposes smoothness by construction, so the standard errors on LGDPMN are computed as though there were three times more GDP information than there is. That the GDP term sits at p = 0.0716, just outside significance, is the coefficient most exposed to this.
- **The sample is defined to exclude exactly the regimes that matter now.** Ending in December 2019 to avoid COVID-19 removes the pandemic, the 2022 forex and sovereign crisis, the trading halts and the price band interventions, which is to say it removes every period in which Sri Lankan macro instability was actually severe, and a study of macroeconomic *stability* variables that drops the instability is answering a narrower question than the title claims. It's also index-level throughout, with no counters, sectors, volumes or spreads anywhere.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **This is my evidence for how little a monthly macro block buys, and therefore for what the orchestrator should condition on instead.** Five macro variables across ten years on this market get to an adjusted R² of 0.189286 in-sample, so the conditioning set I build around liquidity state and volatility persistence is not passing over a stronger macro alternative, it's choosing state variables that move at the frequency decisions are actually taken at. If a macro block goes in at all it's as a slow-moving context input, not as a driver, and I cite this number when I say so.
- **The exchange rate lead is the one prior worth carrying forward, re-estimated properly.** A two-month lag at 0.583956 with p = 0.0015 lines up with the four-to-six-month monetary leads that [[Machine Learning-Driven Sri Lankan Stock Market Prediction, Harnessing Economic Indicators and Sentiment Analysis]] finds on the ASPI, so two independent CSE studies now put USD/LKR ahead of the index by months rather than days, and that's a usable starting grid provided I re-estimate on differenced series inside the training window and report the lag as selected rather than tested.
- **The residual argument is a citable warrant for the sentiment component, and only that.** A finance journal's conclusion that CSE price movements are driven by information asymmetry, investor sentiment and irrational market behaviour rather than macro fundamentals is exactly the motivating sentence I want in the proposal, coming from the domain literature rather than from an ML venue, and it sits alongside Hewamana et al. (2022) on asymmetric information and conditional volatility. It's an inference from an unexplained 81%, not a test, so it motivates the sentiment agent and never evidences it.
- **I state my volatility estimator explicitly and early, because this is the failure mode.** Realised volatility from daily returns within the month, or the conditional variance from a fitted ARMA-FIGARCH as in [[Modeling Long-Term Volatility Memory Dynamics in the Colombo Stock Exchange]], named in the methodology with the formula next to it, so that no reader has to reconstruct from a table what quantity the risk layer is actually consuming. Pairing the two notes in the review chapter is the cleanest way to make that point, one paper measures persistence in a properly specified variance process, the other calls a signed return volatility and reaches the opposite conclusion about how much it clusters.
- **The excluded decade is my coverage argument.** Dropping 2020 onward to avoid extreme events means the local macro literature's most complete framework has nothing to say about the regimes my system has to survive, and since my evaluation spans COVID-19 and the 2022 crisis deliberately, this paper is the concrete citation for why period coverage is a contribution rather than a data-availability accident.
- **Sectoral heterogeneity goes into the evaluation design.** Shafana (2014), surfaced through this paper's review, found the Treasury bill rate negatively affecting every sector except Information Technology and Telecommunications, which together with the ASPI against S&P SL20 divergence in the two other CSE notes means index-level aggregates hide the variation my per-counter agents are supposed to exploit, so results are reported per counter and grouped by sector rather than as one market-wide number.
- **In-sample fit is not a result, and my results table says so by construction.** There is no out-of-sample period anywhere in this paper, no baseline, and no forecast, so R² of 0.30 is doing the work a backtest should, which is the third CSE-facing paper in my set where an unbenchmarked in-sample figure carries the conclusion. Persistence and buy-and-hold stay as fixed columns, and every number I report comes from a window the model never saw.
