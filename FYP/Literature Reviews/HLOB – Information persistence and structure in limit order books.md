Expert Systems With Applications 266, 2025, article 126078, Briola, Bartolucci and Aste, UCL Computer Science and the LSE Systemic Risk Centre, published open access on 12 December 2024. The paper introduces <font color="#ffc000">HLOB</font>, a deep learning architecture for forecasting the direction of <font color="#ffc000">mid-price changes</font> in a Limit Order Book, and its one real idea is to stop treating the LOB as an image. Instead of convolving over price levels in the order they happen to be stored, the authors first learn a dependency structure among the volume levels using an <font color="#ffff00">Information Filtering Network</font>, specifically the <font color="#ffff00">Triangulated Maximally Filtered Graph</font>, then feed the resulting topological objects, tetrahedra, triangles and edges, into a <font color="#ffff00">Homological Convolutional Neural Network</font> extended with an LSTM for temporal dynamics. It is tested against 9 SOTA alternatives on 15 NASDAQ stocks over 3 years at 3 prediction horizons, with code released as part of the LOBFrame pipeline.

This is the second microstructure paper in the vault after [[A reinforcement learning approach to optimal execution]], and the two sit on the same layer from opposite directions: that one takes the book as given and asks when to fire a child order, this one asks what the book's spatial structure actually is and whether a model can exploit it. The distance from my own build is larger than for anything else I have read, since there is no portfolio here, no agent, no orchestration, no LLM and no sentiment, and the venue is the most liquid equity market in the world at nanosecond resolution, roughly the inverse of the CSE. What I want from it sits in three places: the tick-size classification, which is a cheap diagnostic I can compute on CSE counters before committing to any microstructural design at all, the $p_\text{T}$ metric, which scores a model on round-trip feasibility rather than classification accuracy, and the information-persistence result, which is a principled argument about how far ahead a microstructure signal is worth predicting.

> [!NOTE]
> **The tick-size classification is the paper's organising variable and it is defined relative to the spread, not the price.** With $\theta$ the exchange tick size, fixed at \$0.01 on NASDAQ, and $\langle\sigma\rangle$ the stock's average bid-ask spread, the three groups are *small-tick* ($\langle\sigma\rangle \gtrsim 3\theta$), *medium-tick* ($1.5\theta \lesssim \langle\sigma\rangle \lesssim 3\theta$) and *large-tick* ($\langle\sigma\rangle \lesssim 1.5\theta$). The naming is counterintuitive: a *large-tick* stock is one where the tick is large **relative to its spread**, so the spread is pinned near one tick, the book is dense and consecutive levels are genuinely adjacent in price. A *small-tick* stock has a spread spanning many ticks, so the ten quoted levels scatter across a wide and irregular price range and 'level 3' stops referring to any fixed distance from the touch. Everything in the results follows this split rather than sector, capitalization or volatility.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **The dominant LOB architectures assume a spatial adjacency the data does not supply.** DeepLOB (Zhang, Zohren and Roberts, 2019) and the CNN line before it lay the book out as a $T \times 4L$ array and convolve over consecutive entries, encoding the assumption that price level $\ell$ and level $\ell+1$ are neighbours in a meaningful sense. The authors' position, following Bouchaud et al. (2018) and Sirignano and Cont (2021), is that there is no guarantee of homogeneous spatial separation between consecutive price levels, so the convolution models a storage artifact rather than a market mechanism, and that this heterogeneity is what degrades micro-alpha discovery in CNN-style models. The framing is convincing and it is an argument about representation, established by construction rather than by an experiment isolating it.
- **Attention-based models learn the dependencies instead, and pay for it in interpretability and determinism.** BinBTabl and BinCTabl (Tran et al., 2021) apply dual attention across the input's spatial and temporal dimensions and the transformer variants do something similar, so the dependency structure is discovered per-sample and never inspected. The counter-proposal is a structure computed once, deterministically, from mutual information, which can be drawn and read. The authors are unusually honest in the conclusion that this trade runs against them at longer horizons, which is a result I come back to below.
- **Information Filtering Networks exist in the econophysics literature but had not been pointed at a limit order book, and the paper's own predecessor had already shown why one was needed.** The MST (West et al., 2001), PMFG (Aste and Di Matteo, 2006) and TMFG (Massara, Di Matteo and Aste, 2017) line was built for correlation structures among asset returns, and the Homological CNN that consumes them (Briola, Wang, Bartolucci and Aste, 2023; Wang, Briola and Aste, 2023) was demonstrated on tabular data, so the gap closed here is an application gap rather than a theoretical one, with the adaptation, an LSTM added so the architecture handles temporal as well as spatial dependencies, as the specific technical contribution. The motivation comes from Briola et al. (2024), which demonstrated that small-tick stocks impose sparser LOB structures that are harder for deep learning architectures to model while large-tick stocks impose compact ones, and a fair amount of the present paper is spent re-establishing that tick-size effect with a new model rather than testing something independent of it.

#### Data and setup

| Item | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Source | LOBSTER, tick-by-tick NASDAQ, $L = 10$ price and volume levels per side |
| Universe | 15 stocks, 6 sectors, 13 industries, split into 6 small-tick, 3 medium-tick, 6 large-tick |
| Period | 3 separate years, 2017, 2018 and 2019, treated independently |
| Split per year | 40 training days, 5 validation days sampled from within the training period, 10 consecutive test days |
| Input window | 100 LOB updates of history |
| Horizons | $H_{\Delta\tau} \in \{10, 50, 100\}$ LOB updates, event time, physical time never used |
| Target | 3 classes, up / stable / down, threshold $\theta = \$0.01$ |
| Normalization | 5-day feature-wise rolling z-score, fitted on past days only |
| Baselines | CNN1, CNN2, DLA, Transformer, iTransformer, LobTransformer, BinBTabl, BinCTabl, DeepLOB |
| Compute | 1,350 experiments, 7,192 GPU hours, 10 distinct GPU models |

Two rows carry most of the experimental design. The horizon row is in event time, so 100 LOB updates is a different amount of wall-clock time for every stock and the comparison across tick-size groups is not a comparison at a fixed frequency. The validation row is the answer to the look-ahead question: the 5 validation days are drawn from inside the training window rather than after it, justified as producing greater robustness and made safe by the rolling standardization, but it does mean model selection is not performed on data that follows the training set in time.

The labelling rule is the one piece I would need verbatim to reproduce anything here, because the threshold is absolute rather than proportional:

$$
\begin{cases}
(m_{\tau + \Delta\tau} - m_{\tau}) \leq -\theta &\rightarrow\ -1\ \rightarrow\ \text{Down}\\
-\theta < (m_{\tau + \Delta\tau} - m_{\tau}) < +\theta &\rightarrow\ \ \ 0\ \rightarrow\ \text{Stable}\\
(m_{\tau + \Delta\tau} - m_{\tau}) \geq +\theta &\rightarrow\ +1\ \rightarrow\ \text{Up}
\end{cases}
$$

where:
- $m_\tau = \frac{p_1^\text{ask}(\tau) + p_1^\text{bid}(\tau)}{2}$ is the mid-price at tick time $\tau$, and the bid-ask spread is $\sigma_\tau = p_1^\text{ask}(\tau) - p_1^\text{bid}(\tau)$
- $\theta = \$0.01$ is the NASDAQ tick size, used here as the significance threshold for a move
- $\Delta\tau$ is counted in LOB updates, so the horizon stretches and compresses with activity

The simple difference in mid-prices is chosen over the usual smoothed log-return target because smoothing tracks trends rather than the immediate fluctuations that matter for a high-frequency strategy, which is defensible and also makes these numbers incomparable to most of the prior literature. Class balance varies sharply across the groups, with large-tick stocks strongly imbalanced towards *stable* at $H_{\Delta\tau} = 10$ and moving towards balance by $H_{\Delta\tau} = 100$, handled in training by balanced samplers drawing 5,000 representatives per class per day where available, and left untouched in the test set.

#### Model architecture and training

The TMFG is built from volume data only, reducing each snapshot from $\mathbb{R}^{4L}$ to $\mathbb{R}^{2L}$, on the reasoning that volumes are the homogeneous part of the record while price levels are not. Volumes are discretized into equally spaced bins, with the bin count tuned once and held constant across stocks, to stop minimum-lot granularity from injecting noise into the estimator. Pairwise mutual information is computed between all volume levels, bootstrapped daily and averaged across training days, giving one $(2L \times 2L)$ similarity matrix per stock. The graph is then chosen by minimizing the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the true density and the one implied by the representation structure, which reduces to a cross-entropy minimization:

$$\mathcal{G}^{*} \Rightarrow \arg\min_{\mathcal{G}} D_{KL}\big(f(\mathbb{L})\ \|\ \tilde{f}(\mathbb{L}|\mathcal{G})\big) \Rightarrow \arg\min_{\mathcal{G}} \mathbb{E}_{f}\big(\log f(\mathbb{L})\big) - \mathbb{E}_{f}\big(\log \tilde{f}(\mathbb{L}|\mathcal{G})\big) \Rightarrow \arg\min_{\mathcal{G}} H(\mathbb{L}|\mathcal{G})$$

where the first term is independent of $\mathcal{G}$ and drops out, leaving the entropy of the system under the candidate representation as the only thing being minimized, which the TMFG algorithm solves incrementally by joining disconnected parts sharing the largest mutual information. The practical consequence is that the structure comes from a criterion with an information-theoretic meaning rather than a threshold on a correlation matrix, and only statistically relevant dependencies survive because the weakest links are pruned.

From the resulting graph the authors extract three simplicial families, the maximal cliques of size 4, 3 and 2, giving a fixed 17 tetrahedra, 52 triangles and 54 edges for a 20-node graph. Price levels are reinserted alongside each volume level and the sets flattened over the 100-update history, producing three input tensors of shape $100 \times 136$, $100 \times 312$ and $100 \times 216$, one per head.

![[Pasted image 20260923233801.png]]

Each head runs three convolutional layers with a deliberate stride design. The first is $1 \times 2$ with stride $1 \times 2$, so it summarizes the price and volume of a single level and, by virtue of the stride, prevents parameter sharing between levels that are geographically but not logically consecutive, which is the whole complaint against DeepLOB expressed as a stride. The second uses stride $1 \times 4$, $1 \times 3$ and $1 \times 2$ respectively so that it only ever mixes components belonging to the same simplex. The third uses a filter of size $1 \times \Omega$ with $\Omega$ the cardinality of the simplicial family, 17, 52 and 54, collapsing each head to a $100 \times 1$ feature map, with dropout 0.35 applied here because relationships at this layer no longer derive directly from the graph. The three maps concatenate into an LSTM of 32 units and a dense layer of 3 outputs. Training is AdamW at $6\times10^{-5}$, $\beta_1 = 0.90$, $\beta_2 = 0.95$, batch 32, 100 epochs maximum, early stopping when validation loss fails to fall by 0.003 over 15 consecutive epochs.

#### Findings

- **The model's advantage is real at short horizons and gone by the longest one, which is the paper's actual subject.** HLOB outperforms the 9 alternatives in 73.3% of the 15 stock cases at $H_{\Delta\tau} = 10$, 60% at $H_{\Delta\tau} = 50$, and 33% at $H_{\Delta\tau} = 100$. The authors read this as the spatial structure extracted by the TMFG carrying short-term information that degrades as the horizon lengthens, and I read it the same way with one qualification: superiority is defined as the sum of three metrics being maximal, a count of wins rather than a tested difference, so the trajectory is clear but the individual cell comparisons are not adjudicated.

- **Tick size, not architecture, is the variable that separates a usable model from an unusable one.** The averages make this stark, and the contrast between the first and last rows is larger than any difference between models anywhere in the paper.

  | | $H_{10}$ | $H_{50}$ | $H_{100}$ |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | Small-tick F1 / MCC / $p_\text{T}$ | 0.42 / 0.16 / 0.11 | 0.36 / 0.09 / 0.07 | 0.32 / 0.05 / 0.05 |
  | Medium-tick F1 / MCC / $p_\text{T}$ | 0.41 / 0.16 / 0.14 | 0.40 / 0.11 / 0.10 | 0.35 / 0.06 / 0.07 |
  | Large-tick F1 / MCC / $p_\text{T}$ | 0.48 / 0.33 / 0.10 | 0.58 / 0.38 / 0.14 | 0.52 / 0.30 / 0.15 |

  An MCC of 0.05 on small-tick stocks at the longest horizon is a model that has essentially stopped predicting, while 0.30 on large-tick stocks at the same horizon is a model still working. Large-tick performance also *improves* from $H_{10}$ to $H_{50}$ on all three metrics, which is the opposite of the aggregate trend and suggests the aggregate degradation story is really a small- and medium-tick story.

- **The mutual information matrices show why, and the difference is visible rather than statistical.** For small-tick stocks the average non-normalized MI runs from 0.26 for GOOG to 0.74 for IBM and the matrices are sparse and weakly organized, with the clearest structure in IBM concentrating on the central levels and NVDA showing almost nothing.

  ![[Pasted image 20260923233802.png]]

  For large-tick stocks the same quantity runs from 0.62 for ORCL to 1.18 for BAC and a clean three-group hierarchy emerges: the top level $v_1$ carries the lowest pairwise MI, levels 2 to 3 an intermediate amount, and levels 4 to 10 the highest concentration, with the pattern holding across all six stocks.

  ![[Pasted image 20260923233803.png]]

  The diagnostic that makes this quantitative is the 'actual depth' measure $\Xi^\text{Bid}$ and $\Xi^\text{Ask}$ from Wu et al. (2021), the number of tick-sized price intervals the ten quoted levels span. Every large-tick stock sits at 9.00 to 9.21 across all three years, so the ten levels occupy ten consecutive ticks and 'level' means what the array says it means, while MCD in 2017 reports a mean ask-side $\Xi$ of 126.97, its ten levels scattered over more than 127 ticks. When $\Xi \gg 9$ the average MI across levels is corrupted by informational drift, because the same index refers to a different distance from the touch at different times.

- **Against DeepLOB, the structure-agnostic architecture that is otherwise its closest analogue, the gains are consistent in sign and small in size.** At $H_{\Delta\tau} = 10$ the average F1 gain is 0.03, 0.02 and 0.003 for small-, medium- and large-tick stocks, with MCC gains of 0.04, 0.04 and 0.02; at $H_{\Delta\tau} = 50$ the F1 gains rise to 0.05, 0.05 and 0.01 and the MCC gains to 0.06, 0.02 and 0.03. The direction is stable across 15 stocks, 3 years and 3 horizons, which is worth something, and the magnitude means the topological prior is a refinement rather than a step change on this universe. The authors' reading, that DeepLOB is limited by acting only on consecutive volume and price levels, is consistent with the pattern but is inferred from the comparison rather than isolated by an ablation.

- **A model can win on classification and lose on tradeability, and the paper measures both.** Plotting $p_\text{T}$ against the total number of executed round-trip transactions places HLOB, BinBTabl and BinCTabl permanently in the upper-right quadrant, effective at round trips but not parsimonious in trading actions, while iTransformer and LobTransformer sit permanently in the lower-left, the worst on practicability regardless of how their F1 reads.

  ![[Pasted image 20260923233804.png]]

  At $H_{\Delta\tau} = 10$ HLOB is more effective than the two attention models while being the most aggressive trader of the three; at $H_{\Delta\tau} \in \{50, 100\}$ it falls slightly behind them. The authors' explanation is that dual attention weights spatial and temporal features jointly and can therefore refine non-linear relationships across time, which the TMFG, being purely spatial and computed once, cannot, and they state plainly that this advantage comes at the price of reduced interpretability.

#### Limitations

- **No statistical testing appears anywhere, and the headline claim is a win count.** A model is declared superior when the sum of its F1, MCC and $p_\text{T}$ is maximal, three metrics on different scales added without weighting, and the 73.3% / 60% / 33% figures count stocks where that sum is highest. There are no confidence intervals, no repeated-seed variance, no significance test against DeepLOB, and year-wise results are averaged into one number per cell so the across-year spread is not visible either. With 1,350 experiments run, the absence is a reporting choice rather than a resource constraint, and it means the 0.003 F1 gain over DeepLOB on large-tick stocks and the 0.06 MCC gain on small-tick stocks are presented with identical evidential weight.

- **Nothing isolates the TMFG, so the central claim about higher-order topological priors rests on a model-versus-model comparison.** The experimental design is HLOB against 9 alternatives. What is missing is HLOB against itself with the graph replaced: a random planar graph on the same nodes, a correlation-based MST, a fully connected input, or the same three-head convolutional stack fed with arbitrary groupings of the same size. Any one of those would separate the contribution of the filtered dependency structure from the contribution of the three-head architecture, the stride design and the LSTM, and <font color="#ff0000">without one, the claim that the mutual-information structure is what produces the gain is the paper's interpretation of a result rather than a result</font>. This is the limitation I would raise first if I were reviewing it.

- **The reported parameter count does not reconcile with the architecture description.** Table 3 lists HLOB at $1.8\times10^4$ trainable parameters, positioning it among the most parsimonious models tested. The per-layer counts in Section 4.2, 96 per head for the first convolution, 12,384, 11,360 and 10,336 for the second, 17,440, 53,280 and 55,328 for the third and 16,640 for the LSTM, sum to roughly $1.8\times10^5$. One of the two is out by an order of magnitude, and since the parsimony argument runs against BinBTabl at $6.6\times10^3$ and LobTransformer at $2.0\times10^6$, it matters which: on the section text HLOB is comparable to DeepLOB at $1.4\times10^5$ rather than an order below it.

- **The label threshold is absolute while the grouping variable is relative, which partially confounds the tick-size finding.** A move of $\theta = \$0.01$ is one tick for every stock, but for a large-tick stock whose spread is already about one tick that is a move of roughly the spread, while for a small-tick stock with a spread of 3 or more ticks it is a fraction of the spread and well inside the noise. The three groups are therefore not solving the same classification problem, and their class distributions differ accordingly, which the paper documents. Some portion of 'large-tick books are more learnable' is a statement about the relationship between the label threshold and the spread rather than about the spatial structure of the book, and the paper does not separate the two, for instance by relabelling with a spread-relative threshold.

- **The structure is static in a paper about persistence, and the authors say so.** The TMFG is computed by averaging mutual information matrices across the 40 training days and then held fixed through validation and test, so a paper whose subject is how long information persists in the LOB never lets its own representation of that information change over time. This is listed as future work, temporally-evolving IFNs capturing the evolving complexity of the LOB, and I think it is more than future work: it is a plausible alternative explanation for the horizon degradation, since a structure estimated as a 40-day average will describe a longer horizon worse for exactly the stocks whose books drift most, which is the small-tick group.

- **Event-time horizons, no execution model, and one venue.** Horizons are counted in LOB updates, so no result is stated at a fixed frequency and cross-stock comparisons implicitly compare different timescales. $p_\text{T}$ is a probability of correctly identifying a round trip, not a P&L: no fees, no queue position, no partial fills, no market impact, which is the same boundary [[A reinforcement learning approach to optimal execution]] draws around itself. And everything is NASDAQ 2017 to 2019 under one tick regime, which the conclusion flags as the main external validity question.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **The tick-size classification is a cheap go/no-go test I should run on CSE counters before committing to any microstructural component.** Computing $\langle\sigma\rangle / \theta$ needs only the best bid, the best ask and the exchange's tick schedule, all of which are top-of-book quantities I have a realistic chance of obtaining, and it tells me which of this paper's two worlds a counter lives in. If the liquid CSE counters turn out to be large-tick in this sense, with spreads pinned near one tick, the paper's evidence says LOB structure is compact and learnable and a microstructure-aware component is worth building. If they are small-tick, with wide spreads spanning many ticks, HLOB's own small-tick results at $H_{100}$, MCC 0.05, are the expected ceiling and I should not spend the effort. This is the rare diagnostic that is both decisive and nearly free, and it goes ahead of the CSE data-depth question rather than behind it.

- **$p_\text{T}$ is a candidate answer to the comparable-confidence problem the orchestrator design has been stuck on, and it belongs in the evaluation protocol either way.** The open question in the matrices is how an LLM analyst, an econometric estimator and an RL allocator produce confidence scores on one scale. $p_\text{T} = \frac{\text{CT}}{\text{PT} + \text{TT} - \text{CT}}$, where PT counts potential round trips in the targets, TT the ones a model's predictions would have executed and CT the ones executed correctly, is a bounded $[0,1]$ quantity defined purely on realized decisions and outcomes, so it can be computed for any component that emits a directional view regardless of how it arrived at one. It is backward-looking, so it would feed the weighting as a rolling competence estimate rather than a live per-decision confidence, but that is closer to a common scale than anything else in the vault. The quadrant figure adds the reporting discipline that goes with it, plotting practicability against the number of actions taken so that a model buying its accuracy with excessive trading is visible immediately, and with CSE-scale frictions the gap between that view and the F1 view will be wider than it is here.

- **The information-persistence result is the argument for choosing a decision frequency, and it has to be re-derived rather than imported.** The paper establishes that structural information degrades over the prediction horizon and that the rate depends on tick class, which is exactly the shape of argument I need to justify how often my system re-decides. What I cannot import is the number, because the horizons are event-time and a CSE counter may take a full session to accumulate 100 book updates. The transferable piece is the method: measure the degradation curve locally and set the decision frequency where it flattens, rather than picking daily rebalancing because the data happens to arrive daily.

- **A static dependency structure is a live weakness in an architecture, and my orchestrator inherits the same risk in a different layer.** HLOB fixes its TMFG as a 40-day average and the authors identify temporally-evolving IFNs as the missing piece. My main gap is about adapting influence over heterogeneous components as market state changes, and this is the same failure mode one level down: a structure estimated once over a training window describes a regime, not a market. It also suggests a concrete construction worth testing, an IFN computed over the *agents'* output signals rather than over LOB levels, which would give the orchestrator a filtered dependency graph among components and a principled way to notice when two supposedly independent agents have started agreeing for the same reason.

- **The mutual-information estimation recipe is reusable and its preprocessing step is the part that is easy to get wrong.** Binning volumes into equally spaced bins with a tuned and then fixed bin count, bootstrapping daily, and averaging across days is a defensible pipeline for any MI estimate I compute, and the reason for the binning, that minimum-lot discreteness injects non-negligible noise into the estimator, applies with more force on a market with smaller and lumpier orders. If I build any dependency structure from CSE data, this is the recipe to follow and the bin count is a hyperparameter to report.

- **The compute figure and the reproducibility note together set my experimental policy.** 1,350 experiments and 7,192 GPU hours across 10 GPU models is what this paper's evidentiary standard costs on a university HPC cluster, and I will not have that, which argues for a narrow grid decided in advance, fewer baselines chosen for what they isolate rather than for coverage, and the ablation this paper skipped in place of the breadth it bought. The authors also record that original code was unavailable for 5 of the 6 literature baselines with a direct reference, forcing reimplementation and, in their words, compromising reproducibility in the field. Alongside the AlphaAgents sentiment-agent case and the FinAgent auxiliary-strategy result already in the matrices, that is a third documented instance of published financial ML results that cannot be checked as published, and it is directly citable in the review chapter as well as an argument for releasing my own pipeline rather than treating that as optional polish.
