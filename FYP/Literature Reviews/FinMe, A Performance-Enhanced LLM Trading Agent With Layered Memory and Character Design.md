arXiv 2311.13743v2, December 2023, Yu, Li, Chen, Jiang, Li, Zhang, Liu, Suchow and Khashanah out of Stevens Institute of Technology. A single LLM trading agent for daily single-stock decisions, built from three modules, a profiling module that fixes the agent's professional background and risk inclination, a memory module split into working memory and a three-layer long-term store with different decay rates per layer, and a decision module that emits Buy, Sell or Hold on one share. Tested on five US tickers against three DRL agents, two LLM agents and buy-and-hold. Code released.

This is the ancestor of two systems already in the vault, [[FinCon, A Synthesized LLM Multi-Agent System with Conceptual Verbal Reinforcement for Enhanced Financial Decision Making]] comes from largely the same Stevens group and benchmarks against it, and [[A Multimodal Foundation Agent for Financial Trading, Tool-Augmented, Diversified, and Generalist]] credits it with the memory mechanism and human-aligned character design before beating it on four of five stocks and losing to it on crypto. Reading it after those two is the right order, since what each builds on top is clearer once the base is in view, and since FinMem is the cleanest statement in my set of the idea I actually want, that <font color="#ffc000">an information source's timeliness should determine how long the agent remembers what it said</font>. The rest of the paper is a wrapper around that.

> [!NOTE]
> **Naming, and what "memory" means here.** The v2 PDF says FinMem in the title and tables and FinMe in a couple of body paragraphs, and the vault note is filed under the latter. Same system. More importantly, memory here is not [[MacroHFT, Memory Augmented Context-aware Reinforcement Learning On High Frequency Trading]]'s episodic memory, a table of state-action-value tuples used as an auxiliary regression target inside a Q-learning loss. FinMem's is a text store of LLM-summarised insights, ranked by score and pasted into a prompt. Same word, no shared mechanism.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **DRL trading agents are opaque and cannot use text.** The two named deficiencies are interpretability, the layers leading to a decision being neither understandable nor transparent, and textual integration, where turning news and filings into embeddings inflates input dimensionality and empirically causes convergence problems alongside numerical indicators. Standard framing, fair as far as it goes.
- **Existing financial LLM systems are QA systems, not agents.** BloombergGPT and FinGPT are positioned as answering questions on demand rather than acting sequentially, the specific charge being that they process information indiscriminately through QA iterations with no ability to memorize influential messages, so they have no daily-updating knowledge base and their handling of timeliness depends on fine-tuning.
- **The general-purpose agent architecture it borrows from cannot tell financial data apart.** Park et al.'s Generative Agents supply the profiling, memory and action skeleton and a retrieval score built from recency, relevancy and importance, and the stated problem is that this framework struggles with financial data of varying timeliness and importance, daily news against quarterly and annual reports, because one undifferentiated memory stream applies one decay rate to everything.

That third point is the real gap and the other two are scene-setting. The contribution is a stratification of one existing retrieval score, so the question to carry into the results is whether the layering earns its keep, and the ablations never ask it: there is no single-layer FinMem anywhere in the paper.

#### Data and setup

Data spans 15 August 2021 to 25 April 2023, daily OHLCV from Yahoo Finance through `yfinance`, news from the Alpaca News API with Benzinga as backend provider, and 10-Q and 10-K filings from SEC EDGAR. Five tickers, TSLA, NFLX, AMZN, MSFT and COIN, selected for having the highest volumes of accessible news text, with TSLA carrying roughly 6,800 articles against NFLX's roughly 1,600. FAISS is the vector store and OpenAI's `text-embedding-ada-002` supplies the embeddings.

The three long-term layers are the design, and the constants are the part I would need to reimplement, so they belong in a grid rather than in the running prose the paper uses:

| Layer | Source | Update | Stability $Q_l$ | Decay base $\alpha_l$ | $v_l^E$ weights $\{p_1,p_2,p_3\}$ | $\theta_l$ hits 5 after |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shallow | Daily market news | Daily | 14 | 0.9 | {0.8, 0.15, 0.05} | 30 days |
| Intermediate | 10-Q insights | Quarterly | 90 | 0.967 | {0.05, 0.8, 0.15} | 90 days |
| Deep | 10-K insights, extended reflections | Yearly, reflections daily | 365 | 0.988 | {0.05, 0.15, 0.8} | 365 days |

What to read down the last three columns is one design idea expressed three times, a news item drawn from an importance distribution concentrated on the low value and forgotten inside a month, a 10-K drawn from one concentrated on the high value and surviving a year, and nothing about either message's content affecting which layer it lands in. Routing is by <font color="#ffc000">source type alone</font>, decided at summarisation time.

Training runs 17 August 2021 to 5 October 2022 and testing 6 October 2022 to 10 April 2023, roughly fourteen months in and six out, chosen so at least one full 10-K or 10-Q publication cycle falls inside training. Backbone is GPT-4-Turbo at temperature 0.7, top-$K$ is 5 events per layer, and the DRL baselines get a roughly ten-year window from 1 January 2012 because they will not converge otherwise. Baselines are buy-and-hold, PPO, DQN and A2C through Stable Baselines 3, plus Park et al.'s Generative Agents adapted to trading and FinGPT. Metrics are Cumulative Return, Sharpe, daily and annualised volatility and Max Drawdown, averaged over five repeated trials, with Wilcoxon signed-rank against the second-best model.

> [!WARNING]
> Section 5.1 states plainly that FinMem was run under each of the three risk inclinations and that **the reported outcome is the setting that achieved the highest cumulative return during the testing period**. So every headline number in the main results table is a maximum over three configurations selected on the test window, while each baseline contributes a single configuration. This is stated openly rather than hidden, and it still means the main comparison is not like-for-like.

#### Model architecture and training

![[Pasted image 20260919223001.png]]

The profiling module writes two things into the system prompt, a professional background covering the ticker's trading sectors and a historical financial performance overview spanning the training period, and one of three risk inclinations. Risk-seeking and risk-averse are static textual instructions. The <font color="#ffff00">self-adaptive</font> character is far simpler than its name suggests, flipping to risk-averse when cumulative return falls below zero within a brief period such as three days and back when it turns positive, so the whole regime-detection apparatus is a sign test on a three-day return.

Working memory does three operations on a daily trading inquiry. Summarization condenses incoming documents into insights and routes each to its layer by source type. Observation retrieves market facts, and the asymmetry between phases matters, during training it reads the next day's adjusted close so a price decrease is labelled Sell and an increase or no change is labelled Buy, while in testing it loses future access and falls back on cumulative return over the last $M$ trading days. Reflection comes in two forms, immediate, producing the day's direction, its rationale and the IDs of the most influential events from each layer, and extended, which reassesses trends over an $M$-day trace and is written into the deep layer.

Retrieval is where the layering actually lives. Each memory event carries one score in one layer, $\gamma_l^E = S^E_{\text{Recency}_l} + S^E_{\text{Relevancy}_l} + S^E_{\text{Importance}_l}$, with any component above 1.0 rescaled into $[0,1]$ before summing, and the top $K$ events per layer are pulled into the prompt. Recency is an exponential forgetting curve whose time constant is the only thing separating the layers:

$$S^E_{\text{Recency}_l} = e^{-\frac{\delta^E}{Q_l}}, \qquad \delta^E = t_{\text{P}} - t_E$$

where:
- $\delta^E$ is the gap in days between the memory event and the arrival of the trading inquiry
- $Q_l$ is the layer's stability constant, 14, 90 and 365 days for shallow, intermediate and deep

Relevancy is plain cosine similarity between the event embedding and the prompt-query embedding, so it is the one component the paper inherits unchanged. Importance is a sampled initial value degraded by a per-layer exponential:

$$\theta_l = (\alpha_l)^{\delta^E}, \qquad S^E_{\text{Importance}_l} = v_l^E \cdot \theta_l$$

where:
- $v_l^E \in \{40, 60, 80\}$ is drawn with layer-specific probabilities $\{p_1, p_2, p_3\}$ from the table above
- $\alpha_l$ is 0.9, 0.967 and 0.988, set so importance falls to a threshold of 5 after 30, 90 and 365 days

Events are purged once recency drops under 0.05 or importance under 5 pre-scaling. Sitting on top is an <font color="#ffff00">access counter</font>, monitored through Guardrails AI, which adds 5 to the importance of any event immediate reflection names as pivotal, promotes it to a deeper layer once it clears the criterion, and resets its recency to 1.0. So layer is assigned by source but revised by use, the one place content affects routing.

There is no gradient training anywhere. The training phase makes no investment decisions at all, since direction is given by the realised price move, and exists only to accumulate hindsight-labelled reflections in the memory store before testing begins.

#### Findings

![[Pasted image 20260919223002.png]]

- **FinMem takes the best Cumulative Return and Sharpe Ratio on all five tickers, and the margin is wildly uneven across them.** Cumulative returns of 61.78%, 36.45%, 4.89%, 23.26% and 34.98% on TSLA, NFLX, AMZN, MSFT and COIN, with Sharpe of 2.68, 2.02, 0.23, 1.44 and 0.72. Against the best non-FinMem row on each ticker the gaps are +28.4 points on TSLA over DQN's 33.34%, +11.2 on AMZN over A2C's -6.36%, +8.5 on MSFT over DQN's 14.74% and +31.5 on COIN over the Generative Agent's 3.46%, but on NFLX it is <font color="#ffc000">+0.94 points over buy-and-hold's 35.51%</font>. The AMZN win is also over a field where every other row is negative, which is a different achievement from beating a rising market, and it is the ticker where FinMem's own return is thinnest.
- **The prose overstates the table in two places, one of them load-bearing.** Every FinMem cumulative return carries a Wilcoxon signed-rank marker against the second-best strategy, including the NFLX cell where the economic gap over buy-and-hold is under one percentage point across six months, and a signed-rank test on paired daily returns clears its threshold on a consistent tiny edge, so the star says the daily return distributions differ, not that 0.94 points of alpha would survive another window. The smaller slip is the claim that on TSLA and NFLX FinMem holds the lowest volatility and drawdown alongside Sharpe above 2.0, which is true throughout on NFLX at 2.2951% daily and 15.8495% drawdown, and wrong on TSLA where the Generative Agent posts 2.8774% daily against FinMem's 2.9522%. Both are the paper's own table qualifying its own sentences, and the first is the one I would not read as validating the headline.
- **The backbone comparison says the architecture does not survive a weaker model.**

  ![[Pasted image 20260919223003.png]]

  On the shortened TSLA ablation window, GPT-4 reaches 62.62% cumulative return and GPT-4-Turbo 54.70% with the best Sharpe at 2.50, GPT-3.5-Turbo trails at 16.15%, and then it falls off a cliff, davinci-003 at 1.63% and Llama2-70b-chat at <font color="#ff0000">-52.72% with a Sharpe of -2.85</font>. The paper's diagnosis is that both weaker models default to Hold during volatile stretches, which davinci-003's 0.2269% daily volatility and 0.8408% drawdown confirm, it barely traded. The one open-weight model tested is the worst in the table by a wide margin and the only one losing money, and the paper attributes this to context window length and message comprehension rather than testing either.
- **The cognitive-span result is the headline contribution and its own table complicates it.**

  ![[Pasted image 20260919223004.png]]

  Across $K \in \{1, 3, 5, 10\}$ the response is not monotonic, Top 3 at 29.44% cumulative return and 1.12 Sharpe sits well below Top 1 at 52.09% and 1.86, then Top 5 reaches 54.70% with 2.50 and Top 10 reaches 79.44% with 2.75. Best return and best Sharpe both belong to Top 10, which is what supports the claim about exceeding the human five-to-nine limit, while the recommended operating point is Top 5 for its lowest volatility at 2.60% daily and lowest drawdown at 12.57%, and Top 5 per layer is inside the human range on any reading. Defensible as stated, evidenced by one configuration on one stock, and the non-monotonicity warns that this is not a curve I can tune by intuition.
- **The self-adaptive character beats both static ones, and risk-averse is the informative failure.** On the same TSLA ablation, self-adaptive returns 54.70% against risk-seeking's -19.41% and risk-averse's -12.47%, with the best drawdown at 12.57% against risk-seeking's 45.00%. Risk-averse posts the lowest volatility in the block while trailing the market baseline, which the paper reads as overcaution limiting trading activity, and self-adaptive is the best setting on four of five tickers, MSFT preferring risk-seeking. A three-day sign test on cumulative return is the cheapest regime signal available, and on this evidence it already beats both fixed stances.

#### Limitations

- **The best-of-three selection happens on the test window, and it is the main table that is affected.** Three risk inclinations are run and the highest test-period cumulative return is reported, so the headline row for each ticker is a maximum over three draws chosen with the answer visible, against baselines contributing one configuration each. Given that risk-seeking and risk-averse both lose money badly on the TSLA ablation, the spread between settings is large enough that this selection is worth real points, and the honest version of the comparison fixes the inclination on a validation window. It is the single thing I would change here.
- **The test window sits inside the backbone's pretraining data.** Testing runs to 10 April 2023 on five of the most heavily covered tickers on the US market, with GPT-4-Turbo as the backbone, and nothing in the paper measures, or mentions, how much of the result comes from the model recognising what TSLA and COIN did over that window rather than from reasoning over the retrieved memories. The profiling module makes it worse and the paper is unclear about it, Figure 1 shows the historical financial performance overview being generated by the LLM as background knowledge while Section 3.1 says it is fetched from a backend database, and those two readings differ exactly on whether pretraining knowledge enters the prompt. I am flagging this as an untested confound rather than claiming it explains the numbers, and it is unfalsifiable from the paper alone, which is the problem.
- **There are no transaction costs, no sizing and no execution model at all.** Cumulative return is a plain sum of daily log returns signed by the action:

  $$\text{Cumulative Return} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \left[\ln\left(\frac{p_{t+1}}{p_t}\right) \cdot \text{action}_t\right]$$

  where $\text{action}_t$ is the direction the agent chose for day $t$ and $p_t$ is the closing price. No cost term, no spread, no slippage, no position size, and the decision is over a single share, so this is a direction-prediction score wearing a trading metric's clothes. MacroHFT charges 2 bps and [[FreQuant, A Reinforcement-Learning based Adaptive Portfolio Optimization with Multi-frequency Decomposition]] solves a proper fee fixed point at 33 to 40 bps, and against those two FinMem charges nothing, which makes its returns the least comparable to a live result in my whole set. It also means the daily Buy-Sell-Hold cadence is free, and turnover is never reported.
- **The layering itself is never ablated.** Three ablations are run, over backbone, character and top-$K$, and none removes a layer, merges the three into one, or varies $Q_l$ and $\alpha_l$. Since stratifying Park et al.'s single retrieval stream is the entire contribution, the absence of a flat-memory FinMem means the paper offers no evidence that its central idea does anything. The constants are unexplained too, 14 days for news and $\{40, 60, 80\}$ as importance values with those probability triples are credited to "experimentation" with no search reported.
- **The ablation tables are internally inconsistent and all three rest on one reference run.** Table 4's volatility rows do not survive arithmetic, its annualised volatility figures (3.8050, 2.5960, 2.9236, 0.9358) are the daily volatilities reported everywhere else, and annualising a 3.9527% daily figure by $\sqrt{252}$ gives roughly 62.7%, not 3.8050%. The rows look transposed or mislabelled. Separately, the GPT-4-Turbo column of Table 3, the self-adaptive column of Table 4 and the Top 5 column of Table 5 are the same numbers, so all three ablations pivot on a single shared run on a single ticker over a shortened window chosen for budgetary efficiency, and none of them reports dispersion even though the main table averages five trials.
- **The evaluation is broad on tickers and narrow on everything else.** One test window, five US large caps plus one crypto exchange listing, all selected for having the most news available, which loads the dice for a method whose whole mechanism is news retrieval. No walk-forward, no second period, no bear-then-bull contrast, and the DRL baselines are trained over a decade ending in the same month, so the claim that FinMem works on limited data is a claim about FinMem, not a controlled comparison.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **Per-source decay is the piece I am taking, and the constants have to be re-derived rather than copied.** Two independent stores, working memory and a layered long-term memory keyed by source timeliness, with one exponential per layer, is a small amount of machinery for a large amount of behaviour, and it is a better fit for the CSE than for the US market because CSE information arrives far more irregularly. That irregularity is also why the numbers cannot transfer: $Q_{\text{shallow}} = 14$ days assumes a daily news flow, and a CSE ticker going three weeks without a story would see its shallow layer purge itself to empty while the deep layer, holding one annual report, does all the work. The decision this pushes me toward is making the layer constant a function of the observed arrival rate for that ticker and source, a defensible small contribution in its own right that costs one estimator.
- **The information-density assumption breaks first, and FinMem shows me exactly where.** Five tickers chosen for carrying 1,600 to 6,800 articles each over twenty months is the same density assumption I flagged in the FinCon note, but the failure localises better here: with too few shallow-layer events, top-$K$ retrieval returns whatever is left regardless of score and the agent reasons confidently over stale news. There is no abstention path, no floor on $\gamma_l^E$ below which a layer contributes nothing to the prompt, and adding one is cheap. Set beside the finding in [[Role of Market Liquidity in Sentiment-Based Return Predictions, Evidence from Sri Lanka]] that CSE sentiment effects are conditional on liquidity, an explicit "insufficient evidence" branch looks like a requirement rather than a refinement.
- **The reported memory IDs give layered explainability for free, and this is the cleanest instance of it in my set.** Immediate reflection returns the direction, the rationale and the IDs of the highest-influence events from each of the three layers, so every decision arrives with citations into a store whose provenance is known by construction, a news item, a 10-Q insight or a prior reflection. That is an attributable explanation with no extra instrumentation and it decomposes by information horizon, which is exactly the shape of explanation an econometric plus LLM plus RL stack needs to produce. It pairs directly with the section-level provenance from [[Financial Report Chunking for Effective Retrieval Augmented Generation]], the difference being that FinMem's provenance survives into the decision rather than stopping at the retrieval step.
- **Six months to a year of daily data is enough for this architecture, and that reorders my build.** The main run trains on roughly fourteen months, and a separate limited-data test trains on under six months, 17 August 2021 to 10 February 2022, then evaluates over the following fourteen, against DRL baselines needing a decade to converge and dropping out of the COIN comparison entirely for lack of history. Since CSE data depth is the open question gating everything, the sensible sequencing is to build the retrieval-and-memory layer first, which is viable at almost any depth, and treat the RL component as conditional on what the archive holds. A working system then exists even in the bad case, which is a materially better risk position than the one I had.
- **The pretraining-contamination worry mostly disappears on the CSE, and it takes something useful with it.** A frontier model asked about TSLA over late 2022 is recalling, not reasoning, and no design in this literature controls for that. A frontier model asked about a Colombo-listed ticker knows close to nothing, so my results are cleaner by default, a genuine methodological argument for the venue rather than a consolation. The cost is that either reading of FinMem's profiling module leaves me building the background myself, a database of sector descriptions and filing summaries, since the model has nothing to recall and would otherwise produce confident fiction.
- **Backbone dependence is a hard blocker to resolve early.** The architecture returns -52.72% on Llama2-70b-chat and 1.63% on davinci-003 against 62.62% on GPT-4, and since Sinhala and code-mixed content is a first-class requirement, the model handling that input is unlikely to be the strongest available reasoner. Two questions follow, whether a current open-weight model clears the bar Llama2-70b failed, which is cheap to test, and whether the pipeline can split roles, a multilingual model for sentiment extraction and a stronger one reasoning over already-normalised English insights. <font color="#ff0000">If neither works, the memory architecture is not portable to my setting at all</font>, which is worth knowing before the design is committed.
- **The three memory designs in the vault now form one comparison, and they are not competing answers.**

  | | FinMem | FinAgent | FinCon |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | Store split by | Source timeliness, 3 layers | Artefact type, 3 collections | Agent, plus manager episodic |
  | Retrieval score | Recency + relevancy + importance | Similarity per retrieval intent | Relevancy + importance |
  | Decay | Per layer, $Q_l$ and $\alpha_l$ | Not the mechanism | Per agent, $v^E \theta^{\delta t}$ |
  | Promotion | Access counter, layer upgrade | None | Access counter, +5 |
  | Learning | None, memory only | Dual-level reflection | Verbal belief update |

  Read across the top row and the three split the same store on three different axes, and nothing stops me using more than one, since source timeliness and retrieval intent are orthogonal. What I would build is FinMem's per-source decay, FinAgent's intent-tagged retrieval keeping query separate from artefact, and FinCon's belief update as the only learning signal, a composition rather than a choice between three papers, and the argument the review chapter should make instead of summarising each in turn.
- **Its regime switch is the floor my volatility work has to clear.** Flipping to risk-averse on a three-day negative cumulative return is the minimum viable regime detector, and it already beats both static characters by 67 and 74 points of cumulative return on the TSLA ablation. That makes it the right baseline against a conditioning signal built from the FIGARCH estimates in [[Modeling Long-Term Volatility Memory Dynamics in the Colombo Stock Exchange]], not a strawman to dismiss: if a persistence-aware volatility state cannot beat a three-day sign test on CSE data, the econometric layer is not earning its place and I need to know that from the ablation rather than argue it from theory.
