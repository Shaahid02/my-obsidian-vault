ICAIF '22, pages 386-393, Chen, Zhu, Liu and Li, the first author at Cornell writing up an IBM Research internship, the rest at MIT-IBM Watson AI Lab, IBM Research and Three Bridges Capital. The paper takes <font color="#ffc000">optimal trade execution</font> of a parent order, liquidating a fixed inventory over a fixed number of steps, and attacks two things at once: it puts the agent in a <font color="#ffff00">queue-reactive virtual market</font> that responds to the agent's own orders rather than replaying a frozen book, and it proposes <font color="#ffff00">Deep Dyna-Double Q-learning (D3Q)</font>, a hybrid of direct RL and planning against a learned market environment model, plus a state-balanced exploration scheme built for the fact that inventory only ever decreases. Seven US stocks, IEX limit order book data from March to December 2020, everything evaluated inside their own simulator. No code release is mentioned.

This is the second execution paper in the vault and it is the mirror image of the first. [[A reinforcement learning approach to optimal execution]] solves the child-order timing problem cleanly by assuming the order is infinitesimal and the book does not react, which I flagged there as the single assumption that does not survive transport to a thin market. Chen et al. take the opposite starting point, that market impact is the whole problem, and the value of the paper to me is almost entirely in that reframing plus the number they attach to it. The distance from my own build is still large: one agent, one stock, one liquidation, sixteen seconds, no portfolio, no orchestration, no heterogeneous components, and a market that exists only as a simulator. What I want from it is the friction-aware evaluation metric, the baseline set, the evidence that ignoring impact is quantifiably expensive even on liquid names, and the sample-efficiency argument, which speaks directly to my short-history blocker.

> [!NOTE]
> **ATC is a regret against the decision price, not a return.** The metric throughout is average trading cost, $\text{ATC} = (\text{average selling price} - p_D)/p_D$, where $p_D$ is the mid-price at the start of the episode, reported in basis points. It is negative by construction when liquidating, because pushing size into the book walks the price down, so a smaller absolute value is better and zero would mean executing the entire eighty units at the price observed before any of it traded. The normalization by $p_D$ is what makes AAPL and HSBC comparable in the same table, and it is the same trick as the half-spread unit in the Moallemi and Wang note, arriving at a number that is market-agnostic in a way the cash figure is not.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **RL for execution is trained against a market that cannot react, and the authors argue that invalidates the policy.** Their reading of the prior deep RL execution work, Daberius et al., Huang, Lin and Beling, Ning et al., is that all of it represents the market as historical limit order books replayed unchanged, so the agent's own order never depletes a queue, never shifts a level and never moves the mid, and the trained policy is therefore optimal against a market that does not exist. Citing Said et al. on impact, they argue every average-sized order changes the book immediately. This is the paper's central move and it is the one thing here that I would cite without qualification, though it is worth noting they establish it by experiment inside their own simulator rather than against live execution.
- **Model-free RL needs more real-market interaction than anyone can afford to pay for.** Direct RL learns a policy purely from agent-environment samples, which is simple but sample-inefficient, and if the environment is an actual market then every exploration step is a real order with a real cost. The authors position planning against a learned model as the way to buy policy improvement without paying market cost for it, and they distinguish themselves from Wei et al., the nearest model-based execution work, on the grounds that it treats environment-model learning and policy learning as two separate steps while D3Q closes the loop under the Dyna framework.
- **The classical $\epsilon$-greedy exploration used everywhere in RL is systematically biased in this problem.** Most RL domains have an action space that does not change shape over the episode. Execution does not: residual inventory is non-increasing, it bounds the action, and sampling uniformly from $[0, \text{rest}\_I_t]$ at each step therefore concentrates the generated experience on schedules that dump early. That is a structural flaw rather than a tuning issue, and identifying it is the part of the paper I find most useful outside its own subject.

#### Data and setup

The table below is the whole experimental frame, and the row to read hardest is the first one, because the market the agent trains and tests in is generated, not replayed.

| Item | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Environment | Queue-reactive virtual market following Huang et al. (2015), queues as continuous-time Markov jump processes |
| Calibration data | IEX limit order book data, March to December 2020 |
| Universe | 7 stocks, AAPL, HAL, HPQ, HSBC, PFE, SBUX, VZ, six sectors, 2 to 115 million shares per day |
| Time step | 1 second, trade unit is level-1 average order size $\text{AOS}_1$ |
| Episode | $I = 80$ units to liquidate, $T = 16$ steps |
| Market state | 20 variables, volumes at 16 LOB levels, reference price $p_R$, decision price $p_D$, volatility, total LOB volume |
| Metric | ATC in basis points, mean and standard deviation over 1,000 test runs |
| Training | 200,000 episodes, $\epsilon = 0.3$, batch 64, Adam at $10^{-4}$, roughly 20 hours on one K40m |

The queue-reactive construction is the piece worth understanding, because it is what makes impact endogenous. Queue length at each level moves as a jump process whose up-rate is the intensity of new limit orders and whose down-rate is the intensity of market orders plus cancellations, with both intensities estimated as functions of current queue size from historical books. The mid-price moves only when the best bid or best ask queue depletes to zero, and levels shift when opposite-side limit orders are inserted. So an agent that sends a market order consumes queue, brings a level closer to depletion, and makes an adverse mid-price move more likely on the next step, which is impact emerging from the mechanism rather than being charged as a fee.

The agent state splits in two, an agent part carrying residual inventory, residual time and the decision price $p_D$, and the market part carrying the twenty LOB variables. Actions are market orders only, $a \in \{0, 1, \ldots, \text{rest}\_I\}$, which the authors justify by saying limit orders bring delay and non-fill complications they choose not to model. The reward is the per-step relative cost against the decision price:

$$R(s,a) = \big(r(s,a) - p_D \cdot a\big)/p_D$$

where $r(s,a)$ is the cash actually received for selling $a$ units into the current book and $p_D \cdot a$ is what those units would have fetched at the decision price. The discount factor is set to $\gamma = 1$ since the horizon is sixteen seconds, so the objective is undiscounted total relative cost with no risk or inventory-variance term anywhere in it.

#### Model architecture and training

Three processes interleave, and Figure 2 is the clearest statement of the loop: direct RL collects real experience from the dynamic market under state-balanced exploration, the environment model is refitted on that real experience, and planning generates simulated experience from the fitted model to give the policy extra updates for free.

![[Pasted image 20260925230001.png]]

The policy is a <font color="#ffff00">dueling double DQN</font>, two three-layer fully connected networks of widths 512, 256, 128 producing $V(s)$ and $A(s,a)$ with the advantage head sized to the inventory, trained with the standard double-Q target where the online network picks the action and the target network evaluates it, and a soft target update $\psi = (1-\tau)\psi + \tau\theta$ at $\tau = 0.01$ rather than periodic copying. None of that is novel and the paper does not claim it is.

The learnable environment model $M_\psi(s,a)$ is deliberately small in scope. Agent-state transitions are deterministic given the action, residual time drops by one, inventory drops by $a$, the decision price is fixed for the episode, so the only thing that has to be learned is the next market state, which makes the model a plain regression from twenty variables plus an action to twenty variables, a three-layer network trained under L2 loss on the same replay buffer. The reward is not predicted at all, it is computed exactly from the current book and the chosen action. Two design choices here are worth keeping: the model predicts only what is genuinely stochastic, and rollouts are strictly one step from a real state rather than a full synthetic episode, which caps error accumulation at a single transition.

The state-balanced exploration scheme is the paper's other contribution and the argument for it is short and convincing. Under uniform random action sampling over the currently feasible range, each step takes on average half of what is left, so

$$\mathbb{E}[a_t] = I/2^{t+1}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\text{rest}\_I_t] = I/2^{t}$$

and by the middle of a sixteen-step episode there is essentially nothing left to explore with, so the agent almost never sees the states a patient liquidation schedule would visit. The fix samples the whole execution plan at once instead of one action at a time. Draw $T$ distinct integers uniformly from $\{1, \ldots, I+T\}$, sort them into $z_1 < \cdots < z_T$, set $z_0 = 0$ and $z_{T+1} = I+T+1$, and take

$$a_t = z_{t+1} - z_t - 1, \qquad t \in \{0, \ldots, T\}, \qquad \sum_{t=0}^{T} a_t = I$$

which by the stars-and-bars bijection makes every valid execution plan equally likely. Proposition 4.1 states exactly that, and the same sampler is used for the one-step action draws inside planning, not only for full episodes. It is a two-line change to any $\epsilon$-greedy loop and it needs no tuning, which is a good ratio of payoff to complexity.

#### Findings

- **Ignoring market impact during training costs 3.04 basis points on average, on seven liquid US names.** Training D3Q against a version of the virtual market that generates the next book without conditioning on the agent's action, then testing it in the reactive market, degrades performance for every one of the seven stocks. This is the paper's headline justification and I treat it as the most portable result here, with the scope stated plainly: it is a comparison between two configurations of the authors' own simulator, so it measures the cost of ignoring impact as their queue-reactive model represents impact, not as a live exchange would.
- **D3Q beats all eight baselines on all seven stocks, and beats TWAP by a wide margin.** On AAPL, D3Q(7) reaches -11.53 ± 0.14 against -13.40 ± 0.14 for DDQN, -15.79 ± 0.12 for SAC, -16.69 ± 0.12 for Equal Split, which is TWAP, and -16.72 ± 0.14 for VWAP, with First All at -26.55 and Last All at -27.81 bracketing the naive extremes. The pattern holds across the table, though the margin varies a lot by stock, from roughly 5 bps on AAPL down to about 0.4 bps over DDQN on SBUX and 0.2 bps on HPQ, so the claim I would make from this is that D3Q dominates on this universe and that the size of the win is stock-dependent in a way the paper does not explain.

  ![[Pasted image 20260925230002.png]]

  Beating TWAP is the part with theoretical bite, since TWAP is provably optimal when the price process is a martingale, so a policy that consistently beats it is either exploiting predictable structure in the book or exploiting the shape of its own impact, and in this setup it has to be the second because the queue dynamics are calibrated to produce impact rather than drift. That makes the win a statement about impact-aware scheduling specifically, which is narrower and more defensible than beating a benchmark usually supports.

- **Planning cuts the real-market interactions needed by roughly a factor of three, and leaves the final policy slightly better.** D3Q(7), seven planning updates per real update, reaches -13 ATC on AAPL within about 30,000 real market interactions where D3Q(0) needs about 100,000 for the same value, and on VZ it is 20,000 against about 70,000 for -17. The end point moves much less than the path to it, since D3Q(7) beats D3Q(0) on all seven names but by 1.36 bps on AAPL and only 0.12 to 0.36 bps everywhere else, so on this evidence I would treat planning as a sample-efficiency mechanism that does not degrade final performance rather than as a performance mechanism in its own right.

  ![[Pasted image 20260925230004.png]]

- **State-balanced exploration produces a visibly different policy shape, patient rather than front-loaded.** Comparing action histograms over 1,000 test episodes on AAPL, the DDQN agent sells roughly half the inventory in the first few steps and has little left to work with afterwards, while the D3Q agent spreads the schedule and still liquidates a meaningful block in the last steps. This is the exploration bias showing up in the learned policy exactly as the analysis predicts, and it is the most legible figure in the paper.

  ![[Pasted image 20260925230003.png]]

- **The event-driven variant is consistently worse, and they report it anyway.** Acting whenever the book changes rather than on a fixed one-second grid sounds strictly more expressive, and it loses to the discrete-time model on ATC across the board. Their explanation is that the number of decisions per episode explodes and varies stochastically, while effective actions with $a > 0$ become very sparse, so the credit assignment problem gets much harder for a finer granularity that buys little. A negative result on the authors' own idea, published, is worth more than most of the positive ones.

#### Limitations

- **The market is a simulator in training and in testing, and nothing in the paper is evaluated outside it.** The queue-reactive model is calibrated on real IEX books, which is the right way to build such a thing, but every number in the paper is generated by it, including the impact result that motivates the whole approach. There is no historical replay comparison, no live or paper execution, and no sensitivity analysis over the calibration, so what is demonstrated is that D3Q handles impact well as Huang et al.'s queue-reactive dynamics define impact. <font color="#ff0000">A policy that beats TWAP inside a model of the book has not yet been shown to beat TWAP in a book</font>, and this is the gap between the paper's claim and its evidence that I would flag if I cited it in a results context rather than a motivation context.
- **Sample efficiency is measured only in real interactions, with no wall-clock or compute accounting.** Every planning update is a forward and backward pass plus a model evaluation, so D3Q(7) does eight times the gradient work per real interaction, and the paper reports about twenty hours of training without breaking it down by configuration. In a real deployment where interactions cost money that trade is obviously worth making, which is the authors' framing, but inside their own experiments the real interactions are simulated and therefore nearly free, so the reported efficiency gain is an argument about a deployment setting they do not actually operate in. The Moallemi and Wang note makes the opposite mistake impossible by plotting both axes, and this paper would be stronger with the same treatment.
- **Market orders only, no queue position, no fees, no partial fills.** Restricting the action space to marketable orders is stated as a way to avoid modelling delay and non-fill, and the consequence is that the policy can only ever pay the spread, never earn it. That removes the entire passive side of execution, which is where a patient liquidator in a thin market would expect to make most of its savings, and it means the reported ATC values are a specific and rather aggressive style of execution rather than the best achievable cost.
- **One inventory size, one horizon, one venue, one regime.** Everything runs at $I = 80$ and $T = 16$, with no sweep over inventory or participation rate even though impact is the subject and impact scales with size, so nothing tells me whether the advantage over TWAP grows, shrinks or inverts as the order gets larger relative to depth. The data is ten months of 2020 from IEX, a venue with a small share of consolidated volume, covering a period of unusually high volatility, and there is no split by regime, no out-of-period test and no retraining protocol.
- **The exploration result is a between-method comparison rather than a clean ablation.** The evidence for state-balanced exploration is D3Q against DDQN, and those two differ in the exploration scheme but also potentially in network architecture, since D3Q is explicitly dueling and the DDQN baseline is described only as following its original configuration. There is no run of D3Q with $\epsilon$-greedy holding everything else fixed, so the size of the exploration effect is not separated from the rest of the implementation, and the action histograms, while persuasive about the mechanism, are a qualitative comparison of two policies rather than a measurement of one design choice.
- **The objective has no risk term at all.** The reward is pure expected relative cost with $\gamma = 1$, so the agent is indifferent between a schedule that reliably costs 15 bps and one that costs 5 or 25 with equal probability, which is exactly the trade-off the Almgren and Chriss framework exists to express and which the paper cites in passing without incorporating. On a sixteen-second horizon in a liquid name the variance term is genuinely small, so the omission is defensible here and would not be on a horizon long enough for price risk to matter.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **This is the citation that closes the argument the optimal execution note opened.** Moallemi and Wang show the execution-timing literature assuming impact away, Chen et al. show what it costs to do so and build the environment where it cannot be assumed away, and together they let me state the execution-friction problem as a gap with evidence on both sides rather than as an absence. For the CSE the framing writes itself: if ignoring impact costs about 3 bps on names trading two to a hundred and fifteen million shares a day, then on a counter where a single order can be a visible fraction of resting depth the same neglect is not a small correction to the backtest, it is capable of reversing the sign of a strategy's reported edge. That is the strongest available argument for the friction-aware evaluation layer being a contribution rather than housekeeping.
- **Adopt ATC as the execution metric, and pair it with the half-spread unit rather than choosing between them.** ATC normalizes by the decision price and reports in basis points, so it travels across counters with wildly different price levels and is directly interpretable against a strategy's gross return, while the half-spread unit from the Moallemi and Wang note says how much of the available friction was recovered. On the CSE, where spreads in basis points are large, those two numbers can tell very different stories about the same execution, and reporting both is the honest version.
- **Take the baseline set wholesale, because it is cheap and it is the right shape.** First All, Last All, Equal Split as TWAP, and volume-weighted VWAP require no learning, they are what a CSE desk would plausibly do, and they make the evaluation legible to a supervisor and an examiner. This is the same discipline as the static learned weight baseline in the orchestration ablation: the informative comparison is against the best simple thing, not against the worst.
- **A learned one-step transition model is a candidate answer to the short-history blocker, with a caveat that has to be stated up front.** With possibly only twelve to eighteen months of usable CSE data, anything that multiplies the number of policy updates per unit of real data is worth looking at, and D3Q's structure is a good template: model only the genuinely stochastic part of the transition, compute the reward analytically instead of predicting it, and roll out exactly one step from real states so model error cannot compound. The caveat is that a model fitted on a short archive can only interpolate within it, so simulated experience does not create information, it reuses what is there more thoroughly, and any claim I make about it has to be phrased that way.
- **The exploration-bias analysis generalizes to my orchestrator and is worth porting before I need it.** Whenever the action space shrinks as an episode progresses, naive random exploration front-loads and the later states go unvisited, which applies to any execution scheduler I add and also, in a different form, to sampling the orchestrator's weight vector, where independent uniform draws per agent concentrate mass in the middle of the simplex rather than covering it. The stars-and-bars sampler is the discrete version of drawing from a flat Dirichlet, and the lesson I am taking is that the exploration distribution over the constrained object should be designed deliberately, not inherited from whatever the RL library does by default.
- **The queue-reactive simulator is almost certainly out of reach on the CSE, and saying why makes the data request sharper.** Calibrating it needs limit order arrival, market order and cancellation intensities as functions of queue size at each level, which means order-level message data or at minimum multi-level depth snapshots. <font color="#ff0000">Whether the CSE exposes anything below top-of-book is still unconfirmed</font>, and if it does not, the realistic fallback is a parametric temporary and permanent impact model calibrated against observed turnover and daily price moves, which is weaker but honest. This is the same ask as the level-III feature list in the optimal execution note, so it goes into the CSE R&D letter as one request rather than two.
- **The same data gap constrains the liquidity agent, and that link is worth making explicit in the design.** The market state here is raw depth at sixteen levels plus volatility and total volume, which is what $A_L$ would ideally consume, and if none of it is observable then the liquidity agent falls back to turnover, traded-value, zero-return frequency and Amihud-style illiquidity measures built from daily bars. The useful observation is that the evaluation layer and the liquidity agent are gated on exactly the same data, so whatever I cannot observe I also cannot simulate, and the depth of the data request determines how strong both components can be. That argues for treating them as one workstream rather than two.
- **The event-driven negative result is evidence for keeping the orchestrator's decision cadence fixed and coarse.** Their finding, scoped tightly to a sixteen-step second-scale liquidation where effective actions are sparse, is that increasing decision granularity hurt because the decision count exploded and the useful actions did not. My rebalancing decisions are sparse in the same way, most days call for no change, and the temptation to trigger reweighting on a volatility or liquidity event instead of a fixed schedule has the same failure mode. I would rather fix the cadence, show it works, and treat event-triggered reweighting as an ablation to run once rather than a design assumption to build on.
