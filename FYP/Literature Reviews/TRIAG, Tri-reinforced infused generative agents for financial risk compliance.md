*Intelligent Systems with Applications* 30 (2026) 200653, Rafsun Sheikh and Shah J. Miah, Newcastle Business School, University of Newcastle, accepted 16 March 2026, open access. A design science paper that builds TRIAG, three LLM agents coordinated inside a multi-agent reinforcement learning training environment over a hybrid dense plus sparse retrieval stack, and deploys the result as a compliance chatbot inside a partner Australian FinTech. Agent Alpha orchestrates, ingests data, delegates tasks, computes rewards and manages a shared memory, Agent Beta interprets regulatory text, Agent Gamma scans external news and social data for emerging risks. The headline numbers are a retrieval F1 of 0.93 on 2500 regulatory queries and roughly a 96 % inference cost reduction against a commercial API, evaluated further through a 90-minute workshop with five compliance staff.

The task is regulatory compliance and not investing, so nothing here is evidence about returns, and I will not be building a compliance agent. What transfers is the orchestrator. Alpha is the closest published thing in my set to what I am calling the adaptive orchestrator that also writes down an explicit reward decomposition, an explicit state vector, and an explicit agent output contract that carries a confidence field, and the paper is the second instance after [[MacroHFT, Memory Augmented Context-aware Reinforcement Learning On High Frequency Trading]] of state-aware coordination over components that are architecturally identical, which sharpens rather than threatens the heterogeneity claim in [[Scope sentiment analysis dropped]]. It also speaks directly to $A_F$, since the retrieval stack under Beta is a hybrid of dense and BM25 retrieval with a reranker, evaluated with an ablation that separates the two paths.

> [!NOTE] What the 0.93 actually measures
> The F1 of 0.93 is the retrieval pipeline's score on a curated set of 2500 regulatory queries, scored as whether the system retrieved the relevant clause and generated an accurate answer. It is not a measure of multi-agent coordination, and none of the three headline numbers separate the contribution of having three agents from the contribution of having a good retriever. I keep returning to this below because it is the hinge on which the paper's central claim rests.

#### Gap it's addressing

The authors' framing is that AI in financial risk compliance is fragmented by risk type, credit card fraud, market volatility, systemic risk, supply chain credit risk, SME default, insurance claims, smart contract vulnerabilities, each handled by a single-purpose model, and fragmented by method, with GNNs, Transformers, classical ML and federated learning each solving one slice. Their Table 1 is a good version of this argument, twelve studies scored against whether they use GenAI, an RL or adaptive loop, multiple agents, and unstructured data, and the pattern it shows is real, the studies that use GenAI over unstructured data do not close an RL feedback loop, and the ones that close an RL loop operate on structured numerical data. TRIAG is positioned in the empty cell, GenAI plus MARL plus multiple agents plus unstructured text.

The theoretical framing is Generative Sociotechnical Systems theory, from which the authors take strong emergence, generative novelty, and systemic inputs and outputs, and map each onto a design construct, respectively the global reward, the exploration parameter, and the memory update gate. The first two mappings are strained, a global reward is a standard credit assignment device rather than an instantiation of emergence, and calling the exploration term generative novelty renames it. The third survives, because the memory gate is a real mechanism with a real threshold, and it is the only one of the three I would cite.

The gap statement I would actually use from this paper is narrower than the one the authors write and more useful to me. Their contribution is not that GenAI and RL had never been combined, it is that the RL loop here governs *coordination and adaptation between agents* rather than the generation itself, which is a different place to put the learning than almost anything else in my reading.

#### Data and setup

The corpus is 115 regulatory documents totalling 3870 pages and 1,305,766 words, drawn from seven Australian and global bodies, processed into 11,086 chunks averaging roughly 119.6 tokens. The distribution across sources is uneven in a way the authors flag themselves.

| Source | Type | Docs | Words | Chunks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| FATF | Global standards | 19 | 547,449 | 4757 |
| BASEL | Banking standards | 10 | 253,694 | 2059 |
| AUSTRAC | AML / CTF | 59 | 229,347 | 1901 |
| APRA | Regulatory / policy | 19 | 143,299 | 1212 |
| IOSCO | Securities | 2 | 96,764 | 876 |
| ATO | Tax compliance | 5 | 20,249 | 156 |
| RBA | Monetary policy | 1 | 14,964 | 125 |

FATF alone is 42 % of the word count, which the authors read as a risk that downstream behaviour is dominated by international guidance unless source weighting is applied, and they do not then apply any. The raw documents are heavy-tailed, mean 20,402.59 words against a median of 9801 and a standard deviation of 24,821.69, longest 107,043 and shortest 580. Chunking flattens this completely, the processed chunks have mean 117.79, median 117.41 and standard deviation 5.0, which is the point of the table and also its problem, since a fixed-width chunker that turns a 24,821-word standard deviation into a 5.0-word one is discarding document structure rather than respecting it.

The backbone is DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, 28 transformer layers, $d_{model} = 1536$, context capped at $L_{seq} = 1024$ tokens. Storage is dual, Qdrant with HNSW indexing for dense vectors and OpenSearch BM25 for sparse lexical retrieval, with Reciprocal Rank Fusion reranking to the top five chunks, served through FastAPI and Ollama on AWS ECS Fargate.

#### Model architecture and training

Three agents, all built on the same base model, specialised by three mechanisms rather than by architecture.

| Agent | Role | Inputs | Specialisation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alpha | Orchestrator and trainer | Full global state | Central critic, holds reward and memory |
| Beta | Regulatory interpretation | Regulatory and legal text | Role-specific DAPT on regulatory corpus |
| Gamma | Horizon scanning | News, social media, enforcement actions | Role-specific DAPT on external corpus |

![[Pasted image 20260926223001.png]]

The training regime is CTDE, centralised training with decentralised execution, formalised as a hierarchical, decentralised, partially observable MDP over $\langle \mathscr{S}, \mathscr{A}, \mathscr{P}, \mathscr{R}, \Omega, \gamma \rangle$. Alpha observes the full global state, Beta and Gamma each receive a local observation $o^i_t = O(\mathscr{S}_t, i)$ containing only their own text segment and Alpha's instruction. The global state is a concatenation of four blocks, and this is the piece I care about most because it is the paper's answer to the same question my conditioning set is trying to answer.

$$\mathscr{S}_t = \left[ h^{reg}_t \oplus h^{ext}_t \oplus m_t \oplus \theta_t \right]$$

where:

- $h^{reg}_t$, $h^{ext}_t$ are latent context embeddings from Beta and Gamma respectively
- $m_t$ is current compliance metrics, for example a non-compliance count
- $\theta_t$ is the current hyperparameter configuration of the agents

The shape is worth noting against my own $w_{i,t} = f(L_t, V_t, P_t, C_{i,t})$. Two of the four blocks are the agents' own latent outputs, one is a task-performance metric, and one is the system's own configuration. There is no environment-state block at all, nothing describing the regulatory landscape independently of what the agents just said about it. In my setting the analogue would be an orchestrator conditioned on the four agents' embeddings and its own recent accuracy, with no liquidity, volatility or persistence input, which is precisely the design I am arguing against, so the contrast is useful.

The reward is a convex combination of a system-wide and an agent-specific term, and this is the mechanism I would most likely reuse.

$$R^i_{total} = \lambda \cdot R_{global} + (1 - \lambda) \cdot R^i_{local}$$

where $R_{global}$ is a Macro-F1 score on compliance classification computed by Alpha, $R^i_{local}$ is the cosine similarity between agent $i$'s output and either ground-truth regulatory standards or high-confidence pseudo-labels, and $\lambda \in [0,1]$ trades cooperation against specialisation. The authors swept $\lambda \in [0.1, 0.6]$ and report roughly 0.6 as optimal, with low values producing agent selfishness, each agent optimising its own local text metric without improving the system, and high values producing what they call lazy agents, Beta and Gamma riding on Alpha's feedback.

Parameter adaptation is not a policy gradient in the usual sense, Beta and Gamma's adaptation is a heuristic that raises the learning rate $\eta$ when the temporal difference error $\delta_t = R^i_{total} + \gamma V(\mathscr{S}_{t+1}) - V(\mathscr{S}_t)$ is positive and lowers it otherwise. The RL control layer driving retrieval is explicitly not PPO either, it is a deterministic logical control layer whose state is a JSON object of `{Current_Query, Retrieval_History, Compliance_Score}` and whose action space is `{Refine Search, Retrieve Clause, Generate Answer, Halt}`, iterated greedily for at most $K = 5$ steps. That is a scripted controller with a scoring function, and calling it reinforcement learning is generous.

Memory is gated rather than continuous, which is the one construct where the sociotechnical framing earns its place.

$$u_t = \mathbb{1}\left( R^t_{global} > \mu_R + k \cdot \sigma_R \right)$$

where $\mu_R$ is the moving average of the last $N$ global rewards, $\sigma_R$ the rolling standard deviation, and $k$ a sensitivity coefficient set to 1.5. Long-term memory is written only when the current global reward clears a moving statistical baseline, so a round that merely does about as well as recent rounds leaves memory untouched. The authors tested $k \in [0.5, 3.0]$ and report that below 1.0 the policy is high-variance and above 2.0 necessary adaptations are blocked.

> [!WARNING] Symbol collision
> $k$ is the memory sensitivity coefficient in Section 4.4 and the retrieval depth in Section 4.5, two unrelated quantities with two different selected values, 1.5 and 5. If I cite both mechanisms I need to rename one.

Communication is mediated. Alpha broadcasts a task vector $\tau_t = \langle \textit{Instruction}, \textit{Data\_Pointer}, \textit{Context} \rangle$, and Beta and Gamma return $y_t = \langle \textit{Output}, \textit{Confidence}, \textit{Metadata} \rangle$. Direct Beta to Gamma communication is prohibited, enforcing Alpha as the sole mediator.

#### Findings

- **The retrieval ablation is the strongest empirical content in the paper, and it argues for the hybrid rather than for the agents.** Against the full system at precision 0.94, recall 0.92 and F1 0.93, removing the dense vector path and running lexical only drops recall to 0.70 and F1 to 0.76, removing the sparse path and running vector only gives 0.89 / 0.81 / 0.85, removing fine-tuning gives 0.82 / 0.79 / 0.80, and removing the iterative RL retrieval loop gives 0.88 / 0.85 / 0.86. The dense path is carrying most of the recall, which is what you would expect for conceptual regulatory queries that do not share vocabulary with the statute, and the 0.07 F1 attributable to the control loop is the only number in the paper that isolates anything resembling the adaptive contribution.

  ![[Pasted image 20260926223003.png]]

- **Retrieval depth has a clear interior optimum and the paper quantifies the noise cost of overshooting it.** F1 rises from 0.68 at $k = 1$ to 0.93 at $k = 5$, then falls monotonically to 0.74 at $k = 20$, driven by precision collapsing from 0.96 to roughly 0.45 while recall saturates near 1.0. The authors call $k = 5$ the context saturation point and fix it for all primary experiments. The concave shape is unsurprising, the useful part is the magnitude, adding fifteen more chunks costs 0.19 F1, which is a direct argument that a retrieval budget is a real hyperparameter and not a "more context is better" knob.

  ![[Pasted image 20260926223002.png]]

- **The domain-adapted 1.5B model beats a 7B general model on the dimensions that depend on the corpus and loses on the one that does not.** Against deepseek-r1 7B scoring 4.062 fluency, 3.941 relevance, 4.021 domain correctness, 4.291 consistency, 4.427 factuality, the TRIAG artefact scores 4.118, 4.612, 4.204, 4.087, 4.652. Relevance and factuality move most, consistency is the one that goes backwards. On this rubric, judged by an LLM-as-a-judge framework with human validation over compliance-specific QA pairs, adaptation on a domain corpus buys grounding at some cost in output stability, at roughly a fifth of the parameters.
- **The cost argument is the paper's most durable claim and it is an engineering result rather than a research one.** Distilling to 1.5B and serving on CPU or small GPU puts inference at roughly $0.20 per million tokens against $5.00 for GPT-4o and $7.50 for a self-hosted Llama-3-70B, with median latency 210 ms against 450 ms and 180 ms respectively. Latency stays stable to about 75 concurrent users and P99 spikes beyond that, which the authors read as the auto-scaling threshold.

  | Architecture | Deployment | Cost / 1M tokens | Latency | Reduction |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | TRIAG (1.5B distill) | Fargate, CPU / small GPU | ~$0.20 | 210 ms | baseline |
  | GPT-4o | Commercial API | ~$5.00 | ~450 ms | 96.0 % |
  | Llama-3-70B | Self-hosted AWS G5 | ~$7.50 | 180 ms | 97.3 % |
  | Compliance analyst | Human labour | ~$500+ | minutes to hours | >99.9 % |

- **The workshop is small and its most informative result is the rating the authors do not foreground.** Five participants from one partner organisation over 90 minutes, rating six factors out of 10. Task completion support scored 8.6, accuracy and relevance 8.2, usability 8.2, workflow integration 7.8, trustworthiness 7.4, and <font color="#ffc000">correctness and usefulness of references scored 6.8, the lowest of the six</font>, with participants noting that a cited chunk was sometimes only loosely related or slightly out of context relative to the answer it supported. Since citation is the mechanism the same participants named as the key trust feature, the lowest-scoring factor is the one the trust argument depends on, and with $n = 5$ from one organisation this is an observation rather than a measurement.
- **Gamma's brief includes sentiment analysis and no sentiment result is reported anywhere.** Horizon scanning is defined as monitoring external data, detecting emerging risks and analysing compliance-related sentiment, and then neither the ablation, the qualitative rubric nor the workshop separates Gamma's contribution from Beta's, let alone the sentiment part of it from the rest. Consistent with the standing scope decision in [[Scope sentiment analysis dropped]], this is another component whose influence is fixed by design and never measured, which is the same failure shape as the auxiliary strategies in FinAgent and the dropped sentiment agent in [[AlphaAgents, Large Language Model Based Multi-Agents for Equity Portfolio Constructions]], reached here by omission rather than by ablation.

#### Limitations

- **RQ2 asks whether coordinated agents outperform independent single-agent baselines and the paper never runs that experiment.** There is no single-agent baseline anywhere, no two-agent configuration, no variant where Alpha does Beta's and Gamma's work itself. The "w/o RL loop" bar removes the iterative retrieval controller, not the multi-agent structure, so the ablation that would answer the paper's own second research question is missing from the paper that poses it. Everything the discussion says about measurable outperformance over independent baselines is, on this evidence, unsupported, and I should cite TRIAG as an architecture proposal with a retrieval evaluation attached rather than as evidence that MARL coordination helps.
- **Nothing in the evaluation is externally checkable, and the tuning results carry no variance.** The 0.93 is scored on 2500 regulatory queries curated by the team, with no inter-annotator agreement, no described held-out split, no statement of how the ground-truth clause per query was fixed, and availability only on request, so against a public benchmark such as [[FinanceBench, A New Benchmark for Financial Question Answering]] it is not comparable and does not bound performance on anything else. The sensitivity studies are the same story one level down, no seeds, no repeated runs and no confidence intervals anywhere in the paper, Fig. 8's convergence curves are smooth idealised traces with one noisy overlay, and the selected $\lambda \approx 0.6$ sits on the boundary of the swept interval $[0.1, 0.6]$, which usually means the optimum lies outside the range tested.
- **The Confidence field is declared in the communication protocol and then never used.** Beta and Gamma return $\langle \textit{Output}, \textit{Confidence}, \textit{Metadata} \rangle$, and confidence appears in no reward term, no fusion rule, no state block and no experiment. Output fusion is described as Alpha integrating the structured outputs using weighted logic with an adaptive thresholding mechanism based on statistical process control, and no weights, no thresholds and no fusion equations are given. <font color="#ff0000">The single most load-bearing mechanism for my project is the one the paper states in prose and never specifies.</font>
- **Homogeneity is acknowledged, defended weakly, and left untested.** The authors accept that three identical LLM backbones limit the system on tasks suited to structured or graph data, and argue that modern LLMs are versatile enough that role-specific DAPT plus prompting achieves specialisation. No experiment tests whether it does, there is no measurement of divergence between Beta and Gamma after adaptation, and heterogeneous architectures are deferred to future work. The claim that DAPT and prompts produce genuine specialisation is asserted rather than shown.
- **The local reward rests on cosine similarity to ground truth, which the authors concede cannot score a defensible non-obvious reading.** Their own closing limitation is that the reward may not fully capture the nuance of creative compliance strategies requiring novel legal interpretation. The deeper problem is that an agent optimising cosine similarity to reference text is rewarded for paraphrasing the source, which is a proxy for retrieval fidelity and not for correctness, and it will score a fluent, well-grounded wrong answer highly whenever the wrong answer is lexically close to the right clause. Combined with the workshop's 6.8 on reference correctness, the two weakest points in the paper are pointing at the same thing.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **The $\lambda$ split between global and local reward is the cleanest answer I have found to the credit assignment problem in my orchestrator, and it is directly portable.** My current design has one decision and one outcome, so there is no signal telling $A_V$ whether its volatility read was good independently of whether the trade made money. The TRIAG form gives each agent a local term scored against something it controls, a realised volatility forecast error for $A_V$, a spread or impact prediction error for $A_L$, a retrieval citation check for $A_F$, combined with the portfolio-level result through a single mixing weight. Their reported failure modes at both ends, selfish agents at low $\lambda$ and lazy agents at high $\lambda$, are the two pathologies I would otherwise have to discover empirically, so this goes into the design as a mechanism with a pre-registered sweep rather than a fixed value, and the boundary-optimum problem in their sweep means my range has to extend past 0.6.
- **The state vector is a worked counterexample that strengthens my conditioning set.** TRIAG conditions on agent embeddings, a task metric and the system's own hyperparameters, and on nothing about the environment. The design question I have been circling, whether $L_t$, $V_t$ and $P_t$ earn their slots when the orchestrator already sees what the agents produced, gets a partial answer here, because TRIAG's orchestrator has no way to distinguish an agent being wrong from the environment having changed, and its response to poor performance is to adjust learning rates rather than to reweight. I would put this to the supervisor as the argument for the ablations already planned, since Adaptive $-$ Liquidity and Adaptive $-$ Volatility are precisely the test of whether exogenous state adds anything over agent outputs alone, and TRIAG shows the degenerate case is a real design that people publish.
- **The memory gate is a retraining trigger I can lift almost unchanged, and thin-market data forces one change to it.** Writing to long-term memory only when the global reward clears $\mu_R + 1.5\sigma_R$ is the same shape as the question of when to re-estimate FIGARCH and GJR-GARCH parameters and when to refit orchestrator weights, and it is cheaper and more defensible than a fixed calendar schedule. The change the CSE forces is the direction of the test, since TRIAG updates on unusually good performance and what I need is an update on unusually *bad* performance, a regime break, so the gate becomes two-sided. The open question this leaves, and it is genuinely open, is whether $\mu_R$ and $\sigma_R$ over a rolling window are estimable at all on the short series I currently have, since a rolling baseline needs enough history to be a baseline and the confirmed dataset runs February 2025 to February 2026.
- **The Confidence field being declared and unused is the sharpest confirmation yet that comparable cross-agent confidence is unsolved in the literature.** Item 4 of the open matrix items asks how heterogeneous agents produce comparable confidence scores, and here is a 2026 journal paper that defines the field in its protocol, puts it in a figure, and then never computes with it, having had every opportunity. Read with the Correct / Incorrect / Uncertain contract in [[Enhancing Anomaly Detection in Financial Markets with an LLM-Based Multi-Agent Framework]], which is the only concrete cross-agent output vocabulary I have found, the position I can now defend is that $C_{i,t}$ is not a detail to be specified during implementation but the part of the orchestrator with the least prior art, and that is an argument for the main gap rather than a hole in it.
- **The retrieval depth curve gives $A_F$ a concrete budget and a reason to have one.** F1 peaking at five chunks and falling 0.19 by twenty, with precision halving, says that indiscriminate context injection actively degrades a grounded answer. For $A_F$ over CSE annual reports and announcements this argues for a small retrieval budget with reranking rather than a large context window, and it composes with the structure-aware chunking argument in [[Financial Report Chunking for Effective Retrieval Augmented Generation]] and the metadata-carrying node structure in [[Neo4j Graph-RAG for CSE]], since if only five chunks survive, each one has to arrive carrying company, period and statement type or the model cannot use it. TRIAG's own chunker, fixed-width at roughly 118 tokens with a 5.0-word standard deviation, is the design those two notes argue against, and it is a fair guess that some of the loosely related citations the workshop participants complained about are that chunker.
- **The hybrid dense plus sparse ablation transfers to the CSE with an expected sign flip, and that is worth stating as a hypothesis before I run it.** In TRIAG the dense path carries recall, 0.92 down to 0.70 without it, because regulatory queries are conceptual and do not share vocabulary with the statute. CSE filings are the opposite regime in one specific respect, a large share of realistic $A_F$ queries turn on exact tokens, a ticker, a line item label, a date, a currency, where BM25 should hold up better than it does here. So I adopt the hybrid architecture from this paper and predict the ablation comes out differently on my corpus, which is a cheap, falsifiable prediction to include in the evaluation plan and a genuine contribution if it holds.
- **The cost and latency table is the citation for why a distilled local model is a defensible choice, not a compromise.** A 1.5B distill at 210 ms median and roughly a twenty-fifth of GPT-4o's token cost, stable to about 75 concurrent users, is a directly usable precedent for running $A_F$ on a small local model instead of an API, which matters for reproducibility, for cost over a long backtest with many agent calls per day, and for not having a third-party model version silently change under a running experiment. The scoping caveat is that their latency figures come from a compliance chatbot answering one query at a time and not from a backtest issuing four agent calls per instrument per day, so the cost result transfers and the latency result does not.
- **This is the second state-aware orchestrator over homogeneous components in my reading, which raises the delta rather than the threat.** MacroHFT weights six identical DDQN sub-agents, TRIAG weights three instances of the same 1.5B backbone, and in both cases the components share a failure mode by construction, so the orchestrator is choosing between views that are correlated in their errors. TRIAG makes the point for me in its own future work, naming heterogeneous architectures such as GNNs alongside LLMs as the priority extension. The scoped claim I can now make is that weighting an econometric estimator against an LLM analyst against an RL allocator is a configuration I have not found in the reviewed literature, to be validated against further recent work, and the pre-registered weight-entropy diagnostic still applies, since if my weights collapse to one-hot I have built a router and have to say so.
