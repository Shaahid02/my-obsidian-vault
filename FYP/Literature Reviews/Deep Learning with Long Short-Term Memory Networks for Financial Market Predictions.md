European Journal of Operational Research 270(2), 654 to 669, Fischer and Krauss out of the Department of Statistics and Econometrics at Erlangen-Nürnberg. LSTM networks applied to directional prediction on the full S&P 500 constituent universe from December 1992 to October 2015, framed as statistical arbitrage rather than as forecasting, where the model takes <font color="#ffc000">a single feature, the standardised one-day return, over a 240-day sequence</font> and outputs the probability that a stock beats the cross-sectional median return tomorrow. Stocks are ranked on that probability, the top $k$ are bought and the flop $k$ sold, and the paper reports 0.46 percent per day before costs and 0.26 percent after, at an annualised Sharpe of 2.34. Benchmarked against a random forest, a deep net and a logistic regression on the same data, and this is the paper Krauss's group wrote after their 2017 ensemble study, so the comparison is against their own prior best rather than a straw man. No code release.

This one sits in my set differently from the portfolio papers. [[Deep Learning for Portfolio Optimization]], [[FreQuant, A Reinforcement-Learning based Adaptive Portfolio Optimization with Multi-frequency Decomposition]] and [[Deep Reinforcement Learning Framework for Diversified Dynamic Portfolio Allocation Across Global Equity Markets]] all learn an allocation directly, whereas this learns a ranking signal and hands it to a fixed mechanical trading rule, which is exactly the shape of $A_T$ feeding an orchestrator, so I am reading it as a specification for that one agent rather than as a competitor architecture. The second reason it earns a long note is Section 4.1.4, where the authors split their own sample into three eras and show the LSTM and the random forest trading places depending on the period, the cleanest published instance I have found of <font color="#ffc000">two heterogeneous predictors whose relative reliability inverts with market conditions while nothing in the system is allowed to notice</font>.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **Deep learning had not been tested at scale on a survivor-bias-free equity cross-section.** The authors state that to their knowledge no previous attempt had deployed LSTM networks on a large, liquid, survivor-bias-free stock universe, with prior applications confined to S&P 500 volatility, a small sample of foreign exchange rates, or news impact on specific companies. The first contribution is partly just scale, which is worth being honest about.
- **Their own earlier ensemble underperformed the shallow method inside it.** In Krauss, Do and Huck (2017), on the same data and period, the deep net returned 0.33 percent per day before costs against gradient-boosted trees at 0.37 and random forests at 0.43, which the paper calls surprising and attributes, quoting its own earlier text, to neural networks being notoriously difficult to train with configurations in parameter space left unexplored. So the motivating question is narrow: was that failure about deep learning or about the architecture.
- **Memory-free models have to be told which history matters.** The three benchmarks are all fed hand-built cumulative returns over $m \in \{1, \dots, 20\} \cup \{40, 60, \dots, 240\}$ periods, thirty-one engineered features encoding a prior about which horizons carry signal, while the LSTM receives the raw 240-step sequence and has to find them itself, so the experiment is as much about feature engineering versus learned representation as about architecture.
- **Neural predictions are black boxes and the literature stops at the performance table.** The second and third contributions are to characterise what the network selects and then reduce it to a transparent rule, a framing I want to borrow wholesale for the re-scoped attribution layer.

The premise underneath all of it is that a cross-sectional relative target on a large liquid universe is the right problem shape, which holds here because the S&P 500 gives roughly 500 simultaneously priced stocks every day. That is the assumption I have to interrogate hardest for the CSE, and I deal with it in limitations rather than arguing it three times.

#### Data and setup

Constituent lists from Thomson Reuters from December 1989 to September 2015 consolidated into a binary matrix so that index membership is reconstructed at each point in time, then daily total return indices from January 1990 to October 2015 for every stock that was ever a constituent, cum-dividend and adjusted for corporate actions and splits:

| Component | Setting |
| --- | --- |
| Universe | S&P 500 constituents, survivor-bias free, roughly 500 stocks per period |
| Study period | 750 training days plus 250 trading days, rolled forward by 250 days |
| Number of periods | 23 non-overlapping trading periods, out-of-sample December 1992 to October 2015 |
| Out-of-sample length | 5750 trading days |
| Feature | one standardised one-day return, sequence length 240 |
| Sequences per study period | roughly 380,000, of which 255,000 train and 125,000 predict |
| Target | binary, above or below the cross-sectional median return at $t+1$ |
| Trading | long top $k$, short flop $k$, $k \in \{10, 50, 100, 150, 200\}$, equal weight, one-day hold |
| Costs | 5 basis points per half-turn |

What to look for in that grid is the sequence count. Each stock contributes $750 - 240 + 1 = 511$ training sequences, which is not a lot, and the 255,000 figure exists because the sequences are <font color="#ffff00">pooled across the whole cross-section</font> and the network is trained on all stocks jointly rather than one model per stock. That is the single most consequential design decision in the paper for my purposes and the authors never flag it as a decision at all.

> [!NOTE]
> **The standardisation discipline is worth copying exactly.** Returns are standardised as $\tilde{R}^{m,s}_t = (R^{m,s}_t - \mu^m_{train}) / \sigma^m_{train}$ with the moments taken from the training portion of the study period only, footnoted as key in order to avoid look-ahead biases. Because they are re-estimated per period, the scaling also absorbs slow drift in the volatility level across eras without the network having to learn it, a second benefit the paper does not claim.

Benchmarks are a random forest with 1000 trees at maximum depth 20 and $m = \sqrt{p}$ features per split, a deep net of 31/10/5/2 neurons with maxout activation and dropout 0.5, and a logistic regression whose $L2$ penalty is cross-validated over 100 values from 0.0001 to 10,000. The random forest is the one that matters, being the best performer in their earlier ensemble study and a method requiring virtually no tuning.

#### Model architecture and training

The target is the piece I would reuse first, because it turns an absolute forecasting problem into a relative one:

$$Y^s_{t+1} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } R^{1,s}_{t+1} \geq \text{median}\left(\{R^{1,j}_{t+1}\}_{j=1}^{n_i}\right) \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

where:
- $R^{1,s}_{t+1}$ is the one-period return of stock $s$ over $t$ to $t+1$
- $n_i$ is the number of stocks in the universe in study period $i$
- the classes are equally sized by construction, since the split is at the median of the same cross-section

Three properties fall out of that at no cost, the classes being balanced without resampling, the label being invariant to the market's own move so the model never has to forecast the index, and the resulting long-short book coming out close to market-neutral, which Table 4 confirms with a market beta of $-0.0023$ that is not significantly different from zero.

Inside the cell the gates are ordinary, a forget gate and an input gate both sigmoid functions of the current input and the previous output, candidate values from a $\tanh$ of the same, and an output gate scaling a $\tanh$ of the cell state to give $h_t$. The one equation carrying the mechanism is the cell-state update, since it is the additive path the gradient travels along:

$$s_t = f_t \circ s_{t-1} + i_t \circ \tilde{s}_t$$

where $\circ$ is the Hadamard product, $f_t$ decides what fraction of the previous cell state survives and $i_t$ decides how much of the new candidate enters. The whole reason this architecture handles a 240-step sequence at all is that information reaching $s_t$ from $s_{t-1}$ passes through a multiplication by $f_t$ rather than through a weight matrix and a squashing nonlinearity, so a gradient propagated back 240 steps decays according to what the gates learned rather than according to repeated contraction.

Capacity is where the paper's engineering judgement shows, and it gives the one number I will actually reuse:

$$4hi + 4h + 4h^2 = 4\left(h(i + h + 1)\right)$$

where $h$ is the number of hidden units, $i$ the number of input features, and the three terms count the input weight matrices, the bias vectors and the recurrent weight matrices, four of each because there are four gated quantities. With $h = 25$ and $i = 1$ that gives 2700, plus 52 for the dense output layer, so 2752 trainable parameters against roughly 255,000 training sequences, which the paper reports as approximately 93 training examples per parameter and justifies by saying a high number of observations per parameter allows for more robust estimates in case of such noisy training data. Dropout is 0.1 on the recurrent layer, chosen after initial experiments on 1991 showed higher values went together with a decline in performance, the optimiser is RMSprop, the loss is cross-entropy, and training stops early on a 20 percent validation split with patience 10 against a ceiling of 1000 epochs.

#### Findings

![[Pasted image 20260927113001.png]]

- **The LSTM wins on every metric at $k=10$ and the margin over the random forest is thin.** Before costs, 0.46 percent per day against 0.43, 0.32 and 0.26 for the random forest, the deep net and the logistic regression, at standard deviations of 2.09 against 2.15, 2.62 and 2.69, giving annualised Sharpe ratios of 5.83, 5.00, 2.43 and 1.70 and accuracies of 54.3, 53.8, 53.7 and 52.2 percent. After 5 basis points per half-turn those returns become 0.26, 0.23, 0.12 and 0.06, so <font color="#ffc000">costs remove 43 percent of the LSTM's gross return</font> and take the logistic regression to indistinguishable from zero at a Newey-West $t$ of 1.6666 against a critical 1.9600. The formal test is weaker than the return column suggests, the Diebold-Mariano $p$-value for the null that the LSTM's forecasts are inferior to the random forest's coming in at 0.0143 against a Bonferroni-adjusted threshold of 1.67 percent, so the headline claim clears its own correction by roughly two thousandths, and the authors present that number rather than only the unadjusted one.
- **The advantage is a top-of-ranking phenomenon, and the ordering flips as the portfolio widens.** Daily returns fall monotonically from 0.46 percent at $k=10$ to 0.10 percent at $k=200$ and the Sharpe ratio from 5.83 to 3.30, but the random forest decays more slowly and takes the Sharpe lead at $k=150$ with 3.82 against 3.68, holding it at $k=200$ with 3.58 against 3.30. The paper notes this in half a sentence about the exception with $k=200$, and I read it as the more interesting result, since whatever the LSTM is extracting is concentrated in the extreme tails of its own ranking, which is precisely where a thin market cannot absorb the trade.
- **The edge is not stable across eras, and the two models take turns.**

  ![[Pasted image 20260927113002.png]]

  From 1993 to 2000 the LSTM is superior in almost all years, cumulative payouts on a 1 USD average daily investment reaching roughly 11 USD against the random forest's 8. From 2001 to 2009 the two run together until the crisis, where the random forest posts close to 0.9 percent per day in 2008 against the LSTM's 0.15 and finishes the era at 5.6 USD against 4. From 2010 to 2015 the LSTM oscillates around zero while the random forest goes strictly negative to roughly $-1.2$ USD. The authors read the 2008 divergence as tree-based methods being robust to noise and outliers during volatile times, and the post-2010 collapse as arbitrage by an increasing diffusion of such strategies among industry professionals. Both are interpretations rather than tests, but the pattern itself is measured and does not depend on either explanation being right.
- **What the network selects turns out to be a short-term reversal profile with a volatility tilt.**

  ![[Pasted image 20260927113003.png]]

  Top $k=10$ stocks enter the trade on a five-day cumulative return of $-4.05$ percent against the flop stocks' $+4.54$ and a cross-sectional mean of $+0.26$, both groups carry below-mean momentum over $t-240$ to $t-20$ at 9.1 percent against 11.7 for the cross-section, both sit at higher 240-day rolling volatility of 0.0303 against 0.0213, higher kurtosis of 5.81 against 4.80, and a market beta of 1.19 against 1.05, with the effect strengthening as $k$ shrinks. The strategy-level factor regression confirms it and gives the paper its strongest single number, an intercept of 0.42 percent per day at an $R^2$ of 0.0330 with significant loadings on short-term reversal at 0.3792 and momentum at $0.1241$, against the random forest's 0.34 percent at an $R^2$ of 0.1338, so the LSTM's return is both larger and less explained by common risk than its nearest competitor's.
  A three-line rule built on the most visible of those patterns, buying the top $k$ with the most negative five-day cumulative return and selling the flop $k$ with the most positive, held one day and equally weighted, yields 0.23 percent per day before costs, roughly 50 percent of the LSTM's, and loses its edge entirely after costs. So the dominant pattern accounts for half the gross return and none of the net return, and the authors conclude cautiously that additional explanatory power is contained in the other features, forming more subtle patterns not directly discernible from the aggregated perspective.
- **Execution realism costs roughly half the net return, and the paper measures it rather than asserting robustness.**

  ![[Pasted image 20260927113004.png]]

  Over the shorter 1998 to 2015 window where minute-binned QuantQuote data is available, the daily close-execution baseline returns 0.20 percent per day after costs at 53.76 percent annualised. Executing on volume-weighted average prices instead drops it to 0.16 percent and 42.87 percent, a weekly close variant to 0.11 percent, a weekly VWAP variant to 0.09 percent, and a weekly variant with a full day of waiting between signal and execution to 0.09 percent at 20.52 percent annualised. Every one stays significant with $t$-statistics from 5.93 down to 3.46 and every one stays economically meaningful, which is the paper's point, but the ladder from 53.76 to 20.52 percent annualised is the size of the gap between a close-price backtest and something closer to implementable, <font color="#ffc000">on the most liquid equity market in the world</font>.

#### Limitations

- **The cost model is a flat 5 basis points per half-turn with no size or depth term, on a universe chosen for being the 500 largest US stocks.** No spread, no depth, no impact and no partial fill anywhere, and the paper anchors the rate against Jha's 2 basis point assumption for the same universe, so 5 bps is if anything conservative in its own setting. The $k=10$ book holds twenty names and turns over completely every day, which is the configuration where order size against available volume matters most, and it is the one the paper headlines. <font color="#ff0000">This is the same execution-friction blind spot I have written into every trading paper in the vault so far</font>, with the difference that this one at least measures the VWAP haircut, so the friction is observed even though it is never modelled.
- **The signal's core is short-term reversal, which is partly a microstructure artefact, and the tests bound the problem without resolving it.** Section 4.4 is unusually honest here, noting that buying recent losers means buying stocks more likely to be quoted at the ask and selling recent winners more likely to be quoted at the bid, so part of the measured return is bid-ask bounce. The weekly and one-day-waiting variants are the right response and show the return surviving at about half strength, but survival on a universe with penny spreads bounds nothing about a market where the spread is a meaningful fraction of the daily move. On this evidence I can say the effect is not purely bounce on the S&P 500, and I cannot say what fraction would survive on a thin book.
- **Everything is one run and the crucial comparison is marginal.** No seeds, no repeated initialisations, no confidence interval on any performance figure, 23 study periods each trained once. The Diebold-Mariano and Pesaran-Timmermann tests are on forecast accuracy rather than on returns, so the return differences in Figure 3 carry no significance test against each other at all, and the accuracy test that does exist clears its Bonferroni correction at 0.0143 against 0.0167. Given that the random forest overtakes on Sharpe at larger $k$ and wins outright through the crisis, the defensible statement is that the two methods are close and the ordering depends on the slice, not that the LSTM is the better model.
- **Nothing conditions on market state, and Figure 5 is the bill for that.** Retraining every 250 days on a rolling window is adaptation on a fixed calendar rather than to conditions, and within a trading period the mapping from sequence to probability is frozen, so the paper observes its own signal decaying to zero after 2010 and its benchmark going strictly negative with no channel through which either observation could change behaviour. The authors read this as an efficiency finding about the market, which it may well be, and it is simultaneously a description of a system that cannot respond to its own deterioration.
- **The probability output is used ordinally and never checked for calibration.** Stocks are sorted on $\hat{P}^s_{t+1|t}$ and the extremes traded, with no reliability diagram, no Brier score and no analysis of whether a 0.7 prediction is right 70 percent of the time, and no abstention either, since every stock gets a score every day regardless of how much information its sequence carries. Since I want to use exactly this kind of score as an agent confidence input, the absence is worth naming as an open question rather than assuming the softmax output is usable that way.
- **The cross-sectional median target assumes a dense, simultaneously priced cross-section.** The label for stock $s$ is defined against the median of all $n_i$ stocks on the same day, which is clean when roughly 500 names all print every day. Stocks that stop trading are excluded from the trading period onward, a reasonable delisting rule, but there is no treatment of a listed stock that simply does not trade on a given day, because on this universe that case barely exists. It degrades quietly rather than loudly when it does, the median being computed over whichever subset happened to print while a stale price produces a spurious zero return that lands on the correct side of it about half the time.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **Take the target definition, and take it early, because it fixes several problems at once.** A cross-sectional relative label gives balanced classes without resampling, removes the need for $A_T$ to hold any view on the index, and produces a signal whose natural expression is a ranking rather than a point forecast, which is the form an orchestrator can actually weight against other agents. It also separates the technical agent from what [[Stock Price Prediction Using ARIMA Model, Evidence from Colombo Stock Exchange]] and [[Adaptive Stock Market Portfolio Management and Stock Prices Prediction Platform for Colombo Stock Exchange of Sri Lanka]] do, both forecasting a level for a single counter and therefore not scoreable cross-sectionally at all. The caveat from limitations applies, so I need a trading-day filter dropping counters with no genuine print before the median is computed, and the count that filter removes on an average day is itself a liquidity statistic worth reporting.
- **Cross-sectional pooling is the answer to the data-depth blocker, and it also sizes the network.** The usable training set is not the number of days, it is

  $$N_{seq} = \sum_{s=1}^{N} (T_s - L + 1)$$

  where $N$ is the number of counters that pass the trading-day filter, $T_s$ is stock $s$'s usable history and $L$ the sequence length. At $L = 240$ on the confirmed February 2025 to February 2026 set, $T_s \approx L$ and the whole exercise yields about one sequence per counter, so the paper's own sequence length is unusable here and there is no way around that. At $L = 30$ with $T_s \approx 240$ and a couple of hundred tradable counters, the same expression gives on the order of 40,000 to 50,000 sequences, within an order of magnitude of what this paper trains on. <font color="#ffc000">The short history is survivable only because the cross-section is the sample</font>, which reframes the data problem from one about years of history to one about how many counters clear a liquidity screen, a question I can answer from what I already have. Their observations-per-parameter heuristic then sizes the model off that count: at roughly 93 examples per parameter and 45,000 sequences the budget is near 500 parameters, and solving $4(h(i+h+1)) + 2h + 2$ for $i=1$ puts $h$ at about ten to twelve hidden units against their twenty-five. Since $L$ trades directly against $N_{seq}$ and therefore against $h$, the two have to be searched jointly on a small grid rather than fixed independently, which is the same open item already sitting against the estimation-window choice in [[Online portfolio selection with state-dependent price estimators and transaction costs]].
- **Figure 5 is the best single piece of published evidence I have for the main gap, and I should cite it as such.** Two heterogeneous predictors trained on identical data, one winning 1993 to 2000, the other winning decisively through 2008, the first winning again 2010 to 2015 by virtue of losing less, with no mechanism anywhere in the design to shift weight between them. A static equal blend would have been beaten by one of its own members in each of the three eras, and a state-conditional blend is exactly what my orchestrator is. This is stronger than the FinAgent and AlphaAgents cases I have been leaning on, because those are failures of a component that should have been switched off, whereas here both components are genuinely useful and the useful one changes. It also instructs my evaluation directly: report the three-way weighting comparison per era or per volatility regime, not only in aggregate, since an aggregate hides exactly this pattern.
- **Softmax confidence is the obvious candidate for $C_{A_T,t}$ and this paper gives me no grounds to trust it.** The probability is used ordinally and never calibrated, and the 2010 to 2015 stretch is the case that worries me, since there is no reason to expect a network to become less confident when its edge is arbitraged away rather than simply wrong more often. So the technical agent's confidence should be a rolling realised accuracy over a trailing out-of-sample window, conditioned on the liquidity and volatility state, rather than the model's own score, and using the score instead has to be earned with a reliability diagram on my own data. The same question recurs for every agent that emits a confidence and it is the part of $C_{i,t}$ I have least evidence about, so writing it down as unresolved is more useful right now than picking one.
- **The model's selection bias is the argument for why $A_L$ and $A_V$ are separate agents rather than post-processing.** Figure 8 says the network systematically picks stocks that just fell hard over five days, carry below-mean momentum, sit at 240-day volatility of 0.0303 against a cross-sectional 0.0213 and run a beta of 1.19. On the CSE that description selects the thin end of the board almost by construction, since counters that gap on low volume are the ones with the widest spreads and least depth, and Dissanayake and Nanayakkara's finding that unexpected illiquidity shocks depress CSE returns by 0.084 to 0.097, small caps more than large, applies to precisely that set, as [[Role of Market Liquidity in Sentiment-Based Return Predictions, Evidence from Sri Lanka]] sets out. So the liquidity channel is not only a cost model downstream of the decision, it is a constraint on the technical agent's selection, one measurement serving both. The volatility and persistence channel has the parallel job, because a strategy that buys recent crashes is buying into elevated conditional variance, and if shocks on the ASPI decay hyperbolically as [[Modeling Long-Term Volatility Memory Dynamics in the Colombo Stock Exchange]] reports, the exposure schedule after a shock is the difference between a reversal trade and catching a falling counter.
- **Copy the robustness ladder as the friction-aware evaluation protocol.** Daily close, daily VWAP, weekly close, weekly VWAP and weekly with a full day between signal and execution isolate execution price, rebalancing frequency and signal latency separately, and the 53.76 to 20.52 percent annualised spread across them is what realism costs on a liquid market. Two adaptations: the one-day-waiting variant is the cheap test for whether an apparent reversal signal is bid-ask bounce, so it belongs in my protocol as a validity check rather than a robustness afterthought, and the VWAP leg needs intraday CSE data I have not confirmed exists, in which case I say plainly that execution price realism is untested rather than substituting the close and calling it robust. Sweeping the cost rate across that ladder, the way [[Deep Learning for Portfolio Optimization]] sweeps it and this paper does not, gives the two-dimensional grid the rest of the literature reports one cell of.
- **The short leg does not transfer, and the consequence lands on the risk channel rather than on returns.** Short selling is not practically available on the CSE, so the implementable version is long top $k$ only, which drops the short contribution of 0.0017 out of 0.0046 daily before costs and, more importantly, destroys the market neutrality behind the market beta of $-0.0023$. A long-only book whose constituents average a beta of 1.19 is a leveraged bet on the ASPI wearing a machine learning label, and the alpha claim collapses into a factor exposure unless something else carries the risk control. That something is the volatility and persistence conditioning, which the short leg was silently providing here, so the transfer makes $A_V$ load-bearing in a way it is not in the source paper. It also means my baselines need a long-only cross-sectional reversal rule beside buy-and-hold, since beating the index with a high-beta long-only book proves very little.
- **Their black-box method is a cheap attribution template that fits the re-scoped explainability layer.** Characterise the selected set with descriptive statistics and factor loadings, then build the simplest transparent rule reproducing the dominant pattern and report what fraction of the return it captures. Fifty percent from a five-day reversal rule says more about what the model learned than any saliency map and costs nothing beyond statistics I am computing anyway. The caveat is that it characterises selections rather than explaining decisions, so it reports alongside the SHAP attribution over risk features rather than instead of it, and the honest framing of the 50 percent is that half the gross return is explained while none of the net return survives the rule, which is a result about how fragile the explained half is.
