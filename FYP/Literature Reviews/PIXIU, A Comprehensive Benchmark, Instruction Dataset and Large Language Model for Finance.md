NeurIPS 2023 Datasets and Benchmarks track, pages 33469 to 33484, https://doi.org/10.52202/075280-1454. Qianqian Xie, Weiguang Han, Min Peng of Wuhan University, Xiao Zhang of Sun Yat-Sen, Yanzhao Lai of Southwest Jiaotong, Alejandro Lopez-Lira of the University of Florida and Jimin Huang of ChanceFocus AMC. PIXIU is three artefacts released together rather than one method: <font color="#ffff00">FIT</font>, a financial instruction tuning set of 128,640 samples assembled from nine open datasets; <font color="#ffff00">FinMA</font>, four LLaMA checkpoints fine-tuned on it; and <font color="#ffff00">FLARE</font>, an evaluation benchmark covering six financial NLP tasks and two financial prediction tasks across fifteen datasets. The framing is openness, since BloombergGPT had appeared three months earlier with neither weights nor training data released, and the authors are explicit that their contribution is a reproducible stack rather than a new architecture.

This is the fourth benchmark note after [[FinQA, A Dataset of Numerical Reasoning over Financial Data]], [[TAT-QA, A Question Answering Benchmark on a Hybrid of Tabular and Textual Content in Finance]] and [[FinanceBench, A New Benchmark for Financial Question Answering]], and it is the only one that puts a language model on stock movement prediction and credit scoring alongside the reading tasks, so it speaks to $A_T$ and the orchestrator as well as to $A_F$. It is also the closest thing in the literature to a costing for building a CSE instruction set, which is a question I had been treating as open. Two of its own results argue against ever attempting that, and the third section below is where that turns into a decision. Per [[Scope sentiment analysis dropped]], the sentiment datasets in here are read as evidence about instruction tuning and evaluation, not as a component to build.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **No openly released financial LLM, and the one that existed was closed at every layer.** BloombergGPT is 50B parameters trained on a mixed general and financial corpus, and neither the model nor the pre-training data was released, so nothing about it can be checked or extended. The existing open alternatives, finBERT, FinBERT, FLANG, Mengzi-fin and BBT-FinT5, all sit below one billion parameters, which the authors argue limits generalisation.
- **No financial instruction tuning data, so domain models could not follow instructions at all.** The PLMs above need a task-specific head and supervised training per task, and BloombergGPT was never instruction tuned, so the zero-shot behaviour that makes LLMs useful in a pipeline was untested in finance.
- **Existing financial benchmarks evaluate language understanding only.** FLUE covers five NLP tasks and BBT-CFLEB covers Chinese financial NLP, and neither includes a prediction task. The charge is that a benchmark of sentiment and entity extraction does not tell you whether a model is usable for the decisions the domain actually makes, which is the same ecological-validity argument [[FinanceBench, A New Benchmark for Financial Question Answering]] makes about retrieval, aimed at a different missing piece.

#### Data and setup

FIT is assembled from open datasets rather than generated with self-instruct, and the reasons given are worth recording because they are the ones I would use: the open sets are expert annotated so quality is known, there is no commercial restriction inherited from ChatGPT-generated data, and they span text types and modalities that a synthetic set would not. Nine datasets, five task types, 128,640 instruction samples.

![[Pasted image 20260926160001.png]]

The instruction count is not the raw count. Domain experts wrote <font color="#ffc000">ten diverse instructions per dataset</font>, one for ConvFinQA whose multi-turn questions are already instructions in nature, and for FPB, FiQA-SA and NER all ten are applied to every sample while the remaining datasets get one sampled instruction each. That is why FPB's 4,845 raw sentences become 48,450 instruction samples and NER's 609 become 6,090, and it means the dataset mix by instruction count is a different distribution from the mix by underlying data.

Two templates cover everything. The general one is `Instruction: [task prompt]  Text: [input text]  Response: [output]`, and FinQA and ConvFinQA use `Instruction: [task prompt]  Context: [input context]  Question: [input question]  Response: [answer]`, with ConvFinQA's multi-turn conversations flattened by appending previous questions and answers into the context. The prompts themselves are the reusable part of this paper, and three of them show the range:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| FPB | "Analyze the sentiment of this statement extracted from a financial news article. Provide your answer as either negative, positive or neutral. For instance, 'The company's stocks plummeted following the scandal.' would be classified as negative." |
| FinQA | "Given the financial data and expert analysis, please answer this question:" |
| BigData22 | "Contemplate the data and tweets to guess whether the closing price of {tid} will surge or decline at {point}. Please declare with either Rise or Fall." |

> [!WARNING] What "multi-modal" means here
> The abstract claims multi-modality, and in practice that is <font color="#ffc000">historical price series serialised into the prompt as text</font>, alongside tweets, with tables likewise flattened into the context string. There is no separate encoder, no projection layer and no fusion mechanism of any kind, so the claim is about the *data types covered* rather than about the model. Worth remembering when this paper is cited as evidence that LLMs handle time series, because what it shows is that a text model can be handed numbers in a prompt, which the results below suggest it cannot do much with.

Licences are mixed across the assembled datasets, CC BY-SA 3.0, CC BY-NC 4.0 on FOMC, MIT and public, which constrains reuse in a way the "fully open" framing glosses over.

#### FinMA and the evaluation design

Four checkpoints, all LLaMA: FinMA-7B and FinMA-30B tuned on the NLP-task portion of FIT, FinMA-7B-trade tuned on the forecasting portion only, and FinMA-7B-full tuned on everything. AdamW throughout, learning rate 8e-6, weight decay 1e-5, warmup 5% of steps, maximum input length 2048. The 7B and 7B-trade models run 15 epochs at batch size 32 on 8 A100 40GB GPUs, 7B-full runs 3 epochs, and the 30B runs 20 epochs at batch size 24 on <font color="#ffc000">128 A100 40GB GPUs</font>. That last figure is the reproduction cost of the headline model and it is worth quoting whenever this paper is cited as democratising financial LLMs.

FLARE takes the test splits of those same datasets plus six that FIT never touches, FOMC, FiNER-ORD, ECTSUM, EDTSUM, German and Australian, which the authors describe as testing emergent and generalisation ability. Metrics are accuracy and weighted F1 for sentiment, average F1 over nine categories for headlines, entity F1 for NER, exact-match accuracy for QA, ROUGE, BERTScore and BARTScore for summarisation, and accuracy with the Matthews correlation coefficient for movement prediction and credit scoring.

MCC is the metric that carries the argument in the results, so it is worth writing out, because it is the one number in the table that cannot be inflated by a class prior:

$$\mathrm{MCC} = \frac{TP \times TN - FP \times FN}{\sqrt{(TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN)}}$$

where $TP$, $TN$, $FP$ and $FN$ are the counts of true and false positives and negatives. It is bounded in $[-1, 1]$, and a value of zero is exactly the score of a classifier that ignores its input, whatever its accuracy. The authors note themselves that Macro-F1 would be fairer on these imbalanced sets and that they report accuracy anyway to stay comparable with prior work.

The movement prediction label is a binary classification with a discarded middle, which shapes how the accuracy figures should be read:

$$y_t = \begin{cases} 1 & r_t > 0.55\% \\ -1 & r_t < -0.5\% \end{cases}$$

Samples whose next-period return falls between the two thresholds are dropped, so the benchmark removes the cases where a directional call would be least useful and least costly to get wrong, and what remains is not the decision a trader faces.

Comparisons are against ChatGPT, GPT-4, BloombergGPT and Vicuna-7B. FinMA is evaluated zero-shot, BloombergGPT's numbers are the 20-shot figures from its own paper, the other baselines are 5-shot on FIN datasets and zero-shot on the rest, and results marked with an asterisk in the table are imported from Li et al. 2023 and Wu et al. 2023 rather than run here. All results the authors ran themselves are the average of three runs.

#### Findings

- **Instruction tuning on a dataset's own training split buys large gains on bounded classification, and that is what the headline numbers measure.** FinMA-7B reaches 0.94 weighted F1 on FPB against GPT-4's 0.78 and BloombergGPT's 0.51, 0.97 average F1 on Headlines against 0.86 and 0.82, and 0.85 on FiQA-SA with the 30B at 0.87 against GPT-4's 0.80. The authors read this as domain tailoring mattering, which on this evidence is fair for the task shape, but the comparison is a model that saw these datasets' training splits against models that did not, so the size of the gap is a measure of supervision rather than of tailoring.

- **The paper's own seen against unseen split is the honest measurement, and it reverses the ranking.** On the six datasets absent from FIT, FinMA is at or near zero where GPT-4 is not, and the authors state this plainly rather than burying it.

- **Numeric reasoning is where the open 7B and 30B stack fails, and scaling does not close it.** FinQA exact-match accuracy is 0.06 for FinMA-7B and 0.11 for the 30B against GPT-4's 0.63 and ChatGPT's 0.58, and ConvFinQA is 0.25 and 0.40 against 0.76 and 0.60. Tripling the parameter count roughly doubles the score and leaves it an order of magnitude short. The authors attribute this to LLaMA carrying no mathematical data in pre-training, citing the GSM8K results, which is consistent with the pattern but is an attribution rather than something they test.
- **Every model is at chance on stock movement prediction once MCC is read instead of accuracy, and a specialised non-LLM baseline beats all of them.** Accuracies cluster between 0.43 and 0.57 across all three datasets and all eight models, and the corresponding MCC values sit within roughly ±0.05 of zero, GPT-4 at 0.03 on BigData22, FinMA-30B at 0.04, FinMA-7B-full at 0.08 on CIKM18 and -0.03 on ACL18. Credit scoring is the same picture, every tested model at an MCC of zero or below on German and only GPT-4 registering a positive 0.47 on Australian. <font color="#ff0000">An accuracy near 0.50 with an MCC near 0.00 is what a channel with no signal looks like</font>, and it is the dominant pattern in the prediction half of this benchmark. Against that, SLOT, a 2022 sequence model, reaches accuracy 0.55, 0.59 and 0.56 with MCC 0.10, 0.21 and 0.09, so instruction tuning a 7B model on price and tweet data does not reach a purpose-built model on the task it was tuned for.

  ![[Pasted image 20260926160003.png]]

- **Tuning a single model for the prediction task destroys its language ability and buys almost nothing in return.** FinMA-7B-trade scores 0.03 F1 on FPB, 0.16 on FiQA-SA and 0.28 on Headlines, against 0.94, 0.85 and 0.97 for the NLP-tuned sibling, so the classification capability is gone rather than degraded. On the task it was specialised for it reaches MCC 0.03 on ACL18, which FinMA-7B-full matches while keeping its NLP scores. On this evidence the specialised variant is dominated by the jointly tuned one.
- **The ECTSUM column is all zeros for every model including GPT-4, with BARTScore identical at -5.18 across all eight.** The authors explain that models consistently fail to produce output in the required format, which for ECTSUM is a list of 0 and 1 flags marking sentences for extraction. Eight different models producing byte-identical BARTScore is a degenerate-output signature rather than eight measurements of summarisation quality, so this column measures format compliance and nothing else.

#### Limitations

- **The central comparison is not like for like, and the paper's framing does not flag it.** FinMA is evaluated zero-shot on test splits whose training splits it was tuned on; GPT-4 and ChatGPT are 5-shot or zero-shot with no exposure; BloombergGPT's numbers are 20-shot and imported from a different paper; and the test sets were rebuilt to match BloombergGPT's data distribution rather than being the same instances. The direction of the FPB and Headlines gaps is safe because it is large and repeated across datasets, the magnitudes are not interpretable as a model comparison, and <font color="#ffc000">the abstract's claim that FinMA significantly outperforms BloombergGPT, ChatGPT and GPT-4 on most tasks is the version that travels into citations</font>, stripped of the supervision asymmetry that produces it.
- **The appendix contains the fair comparison and it is much less favourable.** FLANG-BERT, a 110M-parameter encoder, reaches 0.912 accuracy on FPB, 0.972 average F1 on Headlines and 0.83 F1 on NER, against FinMA-7B's 0.94, 0.97 and 0.59, and FinBERT is close behind at 0.872, 0.968 and 0.80. So a model roughly sixty times smaller is level on two of the three and substantially ahead on the third. The authors read this as PLMs needing supervised training and task-specific heads, which is true and is a real advantage for the LLM in a pipeline where tasks are not known in advance, but "these PLMs still beat our 7B model on the tasks both were trained for" is the sentence the table supports and the paper does not write.
- **The prediction tasks as posed cannot demonstrate what the paper wants them to.** The label discards every sample between -0.5% and 0.55%, there are no transaction costs anywhere, no position sizing, a single-step horizon and an imbalanced class distribution reported with accuracy as the lead metric on the authors' own admission. A model could score well here and still lose money, and, more to the point for me, the benchmark provides no way to tell the difference. This is the same gap between a classification metric and an economic result that the trading notes keep running into, arriving from the NLP side.
- **Temporal design is absent, and the leakage question cannot be checked.** ACL18 covers 2014 to 2016 and CIKM18 and BigData22 are similarly historical, all drawn from Twitter and public price data that plausibly sits inside LLaMA's pre-training corpus, and no pre-training cutoff analysis, no chronological split and no per-period breakdown appears. [[Predict the Future from the Past, On the Temporal Data Distribution Shift in Financial Sentiment Classifications]] shows financial sentiment performance degrading measurably as the gap between training and test period widens, so a benchmark that mixes periods without reporting them is measuring an average over conditions that behave differently.
- **The instruction set is never ablated, so the paper's own explanatory claim is unsupported.** Ten instructions per sample for three datasets and one for the rest means instruction diversity is confounded with effective dataset size, and the further-analysis section concludes from FinMA-30B failing to beat FinMA-7B that the quality of instructions rather than model size is what matters. That is a reasonable hypothesis and nothing in the paper tests it, since no run varies the instruction count, the instruction wording or the sampling while holding everything else fixed.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **This closes the question of whether to fine-tune an open model for the fundamental channel, and the answer is no.** FinQA at 0.06 to 0.11 exact match after full instruction tuning, against 0.63 for a frontier model that saw none of it, says the numeric reasoning deficit is in the backbone and is not reachable by the kind of tuning I could afford. Read with [[FinanceBench, A New Benchmark for Financial Question Answering]]'s 19% to 50% under realistic retrieval and FinQA's own sub-50% ceiling, the working design for $A_F$ stays a frontier model over structure-aware retrieval, with fine-tuning off the table rather than deferred.
- **Where instruction tuning does pay is bounded classification with a fixed label set, and there an encoder is the cheaper answer.** FLANG-BERT at 110M matching FinMA-7B on FPB and Headlines is a useful prior against reaching for a language model whenever text is involved. If any piece of my pipeline reduces to assigning one of a few labels to a short span, a small fine-tuned encoder is the component to reach for, and the LLM budget belongs to the channels that need reasoning over retrieved evidence.
- **Report every channel on seen and unseen data separately, as its own column, not as a footnote.** The most valuable thing in this paper is the internal contrast between datasets in FIT and datasets outside it, because it turns a headline win into a measurement of what tuning actually bought. For my evaluation that means no component reported on the data it was tuned or selected on, and the three weighting schemes compared on a period none of the agents touched, which is a stricter version of what the ablation plan currently says.
- **MCC beside accuracy for every directional output, and an explicit no-skill reference.** Accuracy near 0.50 with MCC near 0.00 is exactly how a useless $A_T$ would present itself if I reported accuracy alone, and this paper has eight models doing it at once. The best specialised model in the table reaches MCC 0.21 on US large caps with tweets and dense price history, so on thin CSE data the honest prior for a directional channel is lower than that, and <font color="#ffc000">the orchestrator has to be able to drive a channel's weight to near zero</font> rather than assuming every agent contributes. That is the same requirement the weight-entropy diagnostic was pre-registered to test, approached from the agent side.
- **FinMA-7B-trade is a clean documented case for keeping the four agents architecturally separate.** One backbone tuned for the prediction task loses its classification ability almost entirely, 0.94 to 0.03 on FPB, and gains nothing measurable on the task it was specialised for. A shared fine-tuned backbone across $A_T$, $A_F$, $A_V$ and $A_L$ would buy exactly this, and the heterogeneity argument in the main gap is partly an argument that the components should fail independently, which a shared backbone forbids by construction.
- **Unparseable output is a fourth outcome state and needs its own logging path.** ECTSUM's all-zero column is a format failure recorded as a capability score, and I would rather not reproduce that in my own component evaluation. The correct, incorrect and failure-to-answer taxonomy adopted from FinanceBench gains a fourth bucket, no parseable answer, which for the weighting function is a hard zero rather than a low $C_{i,t}$, and belongs in the same reporting breakdown as abstention.
- **The FLARE prompts and harness are reusable, and the cost of the FIT half is the argument against ever building a CSE equivalent.** The templates, the task prompts quoted above and the open repository give me a ready format for evaluating $A_F$ against a small probe set, and the instruction data gives me the costing for the alternative: nine expert-annotated open datasets assembled by a seven-author team with 128 A100s available. There is no annotated Sri Lankan financial QA corpus to assemble from, so the CSE version of FIT would mean primary annotation, which is the same second research problem that sentiment analysis turned out to be and was cut for. The defensible path is a hand-checked probe set on the scale FinanceBench actually released, stratified by reasoning type, and no instruction tuning anywhere in the design.
