European Journal of Operational Research 311 (2023) 333-353, Guo, Gu, Fok and Ching, out of Beijing Institute of Technology, SUSTech and the Advanced Modeling and Applied Computing Laboratory at HKU. Daily-rebalanced long-only online portfolio selection across four equity datasets, where the entire contribution sits on the cost side rather than the prediction side: the paper derives the exact analytical form of the <font color="#ffc000">transaction remainder factor</font>, proves it exists and is unique, gives an algorithm that computes it exactly instead of iteratively, and then builds two optimization models around it that are switched between by a hand-set market-state rule. The assembled algorithm is called SOPS, and its three parts are SEMA, a state-dependent price estimator, NPM, a net profit maximization model, and NPMRP, the same model with a risk parity penalty. No code release is mentioned.

This is the first non-learning paper in my portfolio set and it lands squarely on the friction-aware evaluation layer rather than on any of the four agents. [[FreQuant, A Reinforcement-Learning based Adaptive Portfolio Optimization with Multi-frequency Decomposition]] hit the same self-referential cost problem, that paying a fee reduces capital which changes the weights which changes the fee, and solved it with fixed-point iteration in its Algorithm 1. Guo et al. solve the identical object in closed form with a proof of uniqueness attached, so I now have two independent treatments of the one piece of machinery every other paper in the vault waves away with a flat percentage on turnover. The second reason it matters is structural rather than numerical: SOPS is a <font color="#ffff00">hard binary router</font> between two objectives conditioned on a count of assets in a detected state, which is the degenerate case of the orchestrator I am proposing, and it is prior art that sits closer to my main gap than the equal-weight baseline does.

> [!NOTE]
> **"State" here is not a regime model.** There is no Markov switching, no latent variable and no probability distribution over states anywhere in the paper. A data segment is labelled Bull when its realised mean return sits above a cross-sectional regression line fitted through mean against standard deviation, and Bear otherwise, and the market state is then a count of how many of the $m$ assets are currently Bull. The authors are explicit that this differs from traditional regime-switching, which they argue suits longer rebalancing horizons. It is cheap, it is unsupervised, and it estimates two parameters, which is the whole reason it is interesting for a short-history market.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **Transaction costs in online portfolio selection are computed approximately when they are computed at all.** Most OLPS studies drop costs from the decision process entirely, on the grounds that including them makes the wealth function highly nonlinear and kills any analytical update rule, which the paper traces back to Helmbold et al. (1998). The subset that does include them, through the transaction remainder factor, computes it with an iteration rather than solving it, and the paper's claim is that no prior work gives the explicit formula for $\omega_{t-1}$ in terms of $\mathbf{x}_t$ or establishes its existence and uniqueness. That claim reads as defensible to me on the cited set, though it is a claim about the OLPS literature specifically and not about cost modelling in general.
- **Prior bounds on the cost factor are portfolio-dependent and therefore not usable inside an optimizer.** Li et al. (2018) give bounds that depend on the $L_1$ distance $\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1} - \mathbf{x}_t\|_1$ between the old and new allocation vectors, which is the very thing being optimized over, so using them inside the objective reintroduces the nonlinearity. The paper's constant bounds $\frac{1-\delta}{1+\delta} \leq \omega_{t-1} \leq 1$ are the enabling result for everything downstream, and this is the cleanest piece of motivation in the paper.
- **Price predictors ignore that assets behave differently in different market states.** SMA, EMA and the adaptive moving average of Guo et al. (2021) all run over the full history or a fixed recent window regardless of what state the market was in when that history was generated, so a Bull-state prediction is contaminated by Bear-state data.
- **Risk parity is absent from OLPS.** The paper asserts no OLPS study explores risk parity, and uses the 2008 crisis performance of risk-parity funds, via Anderson, Bianchi and Goldberg (2012), as the argument for wanting it in volatile markets.

The unstated assumption underneath is that a linear, proportional, symmetric cost rate is the right cost model to be exact about. Being exact about the wrong functional form buys less than it looks like it does, and the paper concedes as much in its own future work section, where variable and concave costs and a market-impact term $\Delta r \propto \ln(\Delta x)$ from Potters and Bouchaud (2003) are both listed as extensions.

#### Data and setup

Four datasets, one fixed run each, no train and test split because none of the components are trained in the machine learning sense, and no walk-forward:

| Dataset | Assets | Period | Trading days | Window $w$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| MSCI | 24 | 2006.04 - 2010.03 | 1043 | 31 |
| NYSE-N | 23 | 1985.01 - 2010.06 | 6431 | 37 |
| TSE | 88 | 1994.01 - 1998.12 | 1259 | 25 |
| NASTDA | 24 (23 stocks + risk-free) | 2006.01 - 2010.10 | 1199 | 29 |

What to look for there is the window column, which takes a different value on every dataset with no stated selection procedure, and the fact that three of the four sets are the standard benchmark collection from Li and Hoi (2015) while NASTDA is the authors' own. The MSCI and NASTDA periods both straddle 2008, presumably deliberate given the risk parity motivation.

Parameters are $\delta = 0.05\%$ unit transaction cost, $\theta = 0.5$ decaying factor in SEMA, $c = 0.5$ and $\eta = 0.0001$ in the risk parity term, $\zeta = 0.1$ state switching threshold, and $l = 3$ linear segments for approximating $\ln(x)$. Competitors are 19 OLPS algorithms spanning all five families, including UBAH, UCRP, BCRP, Anticor, CWMR, CORN, EG, ONS, OLMAR, PAMR, TCO and UP. Metrics are cumulative return plus Sharpe ratio, information ratio against UBAH as the benchmark, and what the paper calls the Calmar ratio. Hardware is a single i7 at 1.80 GHz with 8 GB, and no runtime figure is given anywhere.

> [!WARNING]
> The paper's "Calmar ratio" is defined as $(\bar r - r_f)$ over $\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\min\{r_t - 1, 0\}^2}$, which is a downside semi-deviation and makes this the Sortino ratio, not Calmar. Calmar divides by maximum drawdown. The consequence is that <font color="#ff0000">no drawdown measure appears anywhere in the paper</font>, which for a method whose selling point is holding up in bearish markets is the single most conspicuous omission in the evaluation.

#### Method

The transaction remainder factor $\omega_{t-1}$ is the proportion of capital surviving the rebalance, and the equation defining it is self-referential because the cost depends on the post-cost weights:

$$\omega_{t-1} + \delta \sum_{i=1}^{m} |x_{ti}\omega_{t-1} - \tilde{x}_{(t-1)i}| = 1$$

where:
- $\delta \in (0,1)$ is the unit transaction cost rate for both buying and selling
- $\mathbf{x}_t$ is the target allocation for period $t$, the decision variable
- $\tilde{x}_{(t-1)i}$ is asset $i$'s allocation at the end of period $t-1$ after prices have drifted, itself $\frac{x_{(t-1)i}r_{(t-1)i}}{\mathbf{r}_{t-1}\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^\top}$

The weight difference is taken as $|x_{ti}\omega_{t-1} - \tilde{x}_{(t-1)i}|$ rather than $|x_{ti} - \tilde{x}_{(t-1)i}|$, which is what makes it exact and also what makes it circular. Theorem 3.1 proves a unique solution exists in $(0,1)$ by showing the residual function is continuous and strictly decreasing, and Algorithm 3.1 computes it by sorting the ratios $\tilde{x}_{(t-1)i}/x_{ti}$ below one, walking a counter up through the resulting sub-intervals until the residual turns negative, and evaluating a closed form on that interval. The payoff is the constant bound $\frac{1-\delta}{1+\delta} \leq \omega_{t-1} \leq 1$, which at $\delta = 0.05\%$ means $\omega_{t-1} \geq 0.999$ and lets the cost term be linearised with an approximation error the paper bounds at $\frac{2\delta^2}{1+\delta}\sum \hat r_{ti}x_{ti}$.

SEMA is the prediction step. History is cut into $d+1$ segments of $w$ days, each segment $l$ of asset $i$ giving a mean $\mu_l^{(i)}$ and a standard deviation $\nu_l^{(i)}$, and a per-asset least-squares line is fitted through those points assuming $\mu = \alpha\nu + \beta$. The state label is then whether the segment sits above its own line:

$$s_l^{(i)} = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } \mu_l^{(i)} > \hat\alpha^{(i)}\nu_l^{(i)} + \hat\beta^{(i)} \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

Segments sharing the current state are concatenated into one ordered sequence and EMA is applied to that sequence alone, recursively as $\hat r_{ji} = \theta + (1-\theta)\frac{\hat r_{(j-1)i}}{r^r_{(j-1)i}}$, rather than to the raw history. States are per asset and per segment, so two assets can be in different states in the same period. Note the selection uses the state of the last full segment $s_d^{(i)}$ rather than the partial $(d+1)$th, since the trailing segment may be shorter than $w$.

The whole-market state is a count. If $\sum_{i} s_d^{(i)} \leq \zeta m$, meaning at $\zeta = 0.1$ that more than 90% of assets are Bear, the market is bearish and NPMRP is used, otherwise NPM. NPM maximises $\sum_i \hat r_{ti}x_{ti} - \delta\sum_i \hat r_{ti}x_{ti}\sum_i |\tilde x_{(t-1)i} - x_{ti}|$, net return minus linearised cost, and NPMRP adds a long-only risk parity penalty in the style of Bai, Scheinberg and Tutuncu (2016):

$$\max_{\mathbf{x}_t} \ \sum_{i=1}^{m}\hat r_{ti}x_{ti} - \delta\sum_{i=1}^{m}\hat r_{ti}x_{ti}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\tilde x_{(t-1)i} - x_{ti}| - \eta\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sigma_{ij}^{(t)}x_{ti}x_{tj} - c\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ln x_{ti}\right)$$

where $\eta$ weights the risk term and $c$ trades portfolio variance against the log barrier that keeps every weight strictly positive. Both models become quadratic programs through a change of variables splitting $|x_{ti} - \tilde x_{(t-1)i}|$ into non-negative parts $f_{ti}$ and $g_{ti}$, with three propositions and a theorem establishing that the transformed problem's optimum maps back to the original, and $\ln(x)$ handled by a piecewise linear lower envelope of $l$ tangent-style segments. The resulting QPs have $2m$ and $3m$ variables and go to an off-the-shelf solver each period.

#### Findings

![[Pasted image 20260923230001.png]]

- **SOPS takes the highest cumulative return on all four datasets, and the margin varies enormously across them.** 14.8585 against AOLNPM's 10.1757 on MSCI, $9.1526 \times 10^6$ against OLMAR-1's $8.2320 \times 10^6$ on NYSE-N, 560.4279 against AOLNPM's 321.3223 on TSE, and 6.6460 against BCRP's 5.6676 on NASTDA. The NYSE-N margin is 11% over the runner-up on a 25-year horizon, while the TSE margin is 74%, so the headline sweep is real but the effect size is not stable across datasets. BCRP is a hindsight strategy and its appearance as runner-up on NASTDA is worth noting rather than treating as a fair comparison.

- **The risk-adjusted picture is much weaker than the cumulative return picture and the paper does not say so.** SOPS gives the largest SR, IR and CR on MSCI and TSE, but on NYSE-N the text concedes that SOPS, AOLNPM, Anticor-1, Anticor-2, OLMAR-1, OLMAR-2, TCO-1 and TCO-2 all give considerably higher ratios than the rest, which is a group of eight rather than a win, and on NASTDA the same is true of SOPS, BCRP, TCO-1 and TCO-2. So on the two datasets where SOPS is not clearly best on risk-adjusted terms it is one of four or one of eight, and the paper's summary sentence, that SOPS achieves relatively large values on all four sets, is accurate but does more work than it looks like.

- **The component ablation separates only in the last quarter of the run, and the separation is a single realisation.**

  On MSCI, EMA-NPM reaches 13.0259, SEMA-NPM 14.0270, SEMA-NPMRP 12.2729 and SOPS 14.8585, which the paper reads as SEMA beating EMA and the switching mechanism beating either model alone. Reading the curves rather than the endpoints, all four are indistinguishable for roughly the first 780 of 1043 periods and the entire ordering is generated in the final stretch through the 2009 recovery. - **The risk parity constraint does not produce a risk parity portfolio, and the paper is upfront about it.** On the 1004th trading day SOPS holds Stock 6, Stock 16 and Stock 23 at weights of 60.80%, 4.54% and 34.66%, with risk contributions of 0.0117, 0.0008 and 0.0081, a factor of roughly fourteen between the largest and smallest. At $\eta = 0.0001$ the penalty is close to inert, and what NPMRP actually delivers is the smallest return standard deviation of the four variants at 0.0244 alongside the worst cumulative return at 12.2729. Calling it a risk parity constraint oversells what it is, which is a mild diversification nudge, and the honest reporting of the risk contributions is what lets me say so.

- **The strategy is extremely concentrated and turns over almost completely every period.**

  ![[Pasted image 20260923230003.png]]

  SOPS holds two or three of 24 assets for most of the horizon, never more than six, and averages 4.6 in the periods where it holds more than one. Reading Fig. 12 against the parameters, the per-period cost proportion sits at its ceiling of $1 \times 10^{-3}$ in most periods, and since that ceiling is exactly $2\delta$ under the linear cost model, the portfolio is fully turning over in those periods rather than trimming. Cumulative cost over the horizon reaches 0.7528 against a cumulative return of 14.8585, and 765 of the periods carry nonzero cost. The paper's reading is that costs are small but not negligible, which holds at $\delta = 0.05\%$ and is a statement about that rate rather than about the method.

- **Parameter sensitivity is low across the four parameters tested.** Varying $\zeta$ from 0.1 to 0.2 changes cumulative return by at most 0.60% with an average relative change of 0.33%, $c$ from 3.5 to 5.5 by at most 0.77% with an average of 0.34%, $\eta$ from 0.0001 to 0.1 by at most 0.68% with an average of 0.18%, and window sizes from 25 to 38 move average prediction error by 0.38%, 0.16%, 0.43% and 0.41% across the four datasets.

#### Limitations

- **The state-switching contribution is never isolated from how often it fires.** The paper reports no count of how many periods were classified bearish, no timeline of the switch, and no comparison against a random or fixed-schedule router firing at the same rate. A threshold that can double from 0.1 to 0.2 while moving cumulative return by 0.6% is either robust or rarely binding, and nothing in the paper separates those, so there is no way to tell whether the 14.0270 to 14.8585 improvement comes from switching to NPMRP at the right moments or from switching so rarely that SOPS is mostly NPM with occasional de-risking. This is the same diagnostic problem as MacroHFT's near-binary learned mixture in [[MacroHFT, Memory Augmented Context-aware Reinforcement Learning On High Frequency Trading]], arriving from the opposite direction, and it is the specific thing my own weight-entropy reporting is meant to prevent.

- **A paper about exact transaction cost computation never varies the transaction cost rate.** $\delta$ is fixed at 0.05% for every experiment, and the sensitivity section covers $\zeta$, $c$, $\eta$ and $w$ but not $\delta$. Since the cost term enters the objective linearly and the strategy turns over almost completely each period, the cost drag scales close to linearly in $\delta$, so a rate five or ten times higher would change both the realised return and, more importantly, the optimizer's own trade-off between chasing the predicted return and staying put. Whether SOPS still beats the nineteen baselines at a frontier-market cost rate is exactly the question the paper's machinery is built to answer and exactly the experiment it does not run.

- **Linear proportional cost is the whole cost model, so "exact" is exact within a form that omits the costs that matter in a thin book.** There is no bid-ask spread, no market impact, no partial fill, no queue position and no volume constraint, and the same rate applies to buying and selling and to every asset regardless of size. The authors list variable and concave costs and a logarithmic impact term as future work, which is a fair acknowledgement, but it means the exactness result is exactness about commission, and <font color="#ff0000">the execution-friction blind spot survives in the paper that treats transaction cost most rigorously</font>, which is now the second time I have written that sentence about a portfolio paper.

- **No walk-forward, no seeds, no significance tests, and per-dataset window sizes with no selection procedure.** Every number is one run on one fixed sequence. That is more defensible here than in a deep RL paper since SOPS has no trained weights, but the four window values of 31, 37, 25 and 29 are still four choices made per dataset with no validation protocol described, and the robustness check varies $w$ while measuring average prediction error rather than cumulative return, which are not the same quantity and can move in opposite directions. Three of the four datasets are also the standard OLPS benchmark sets, which carry known survivorship characteristics, and NYSE-N in particular is 23 stocks that survived from 1985 to 2010.

- **Practicality is claimed and never measured.** The paper argues SOPS suits high-frequency rebalancing because it is single-step-forward and needs no multi-period calibration, but it solves a QP with $2m$ or $3m$ variables every period, preceded by a sort and a linear walk in Algorithm 3.1, and reports no runtime at all on any of the four datasets including the 88-asset TSE set. On an i7 at 1.80 GHz that is almost certainly fine at daily frequency, and the absence of the number means the intraday claim rests on an architectural argument rather than evidence.

- **The prediction and allocation stages share a single point of failure.** NPM and NPMRP are two objectives over the same inputs, SEMA's $\hat{\mathbf{r}}_t$ and the covariance $\Sigma_t$, so when SEMA mispredicts, both models fail together and the router has nothing to route to. That is the same homogeneity criticism I have been making of MacroHFT's six identically-architected sub-agents, and it applies here even more directly since there are only two branches and they differ by one penalty term.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **The TRF derivation goes straight into the friction-aware evaluation layer, and the constant bounds are the part I actually need.** My cost model has the same self-referential structure as everyone's, and I now have two solutions to it, FreQuant's fixed-point iteration and this closed form with a uniqueness proof. The closed form is preferable for a component I will run thousands of times across a cost-sensitivity sweep, and the constant bound $\frac{1-\delta}{1+\delta} \leq \omega_{t-1} \leq 1$ is the piece that lets a cost term stay inside a convex objective rather than sitting outside it as a post-hoc deduction. At a CSE-plausible round-trip rate of 1%, that bound gives $\omega \geq 0.980$, which is still tight enough for the linearisation to hold, and I should verify that numerically before relying on it rather than assuming the paper's 0.05% analysis transfers.

- **Rerunning their own sensitivity analysis over $\delta$ is a cheap, publishable contribution and it is the obvious CSE angle on this paper.** The method is fully specified, the QP transformation is written out, the cost rate is a single scalar, and the experiment they skipped is the one my market needs. Sweeping $\delta$ from 5 bps to somewhere north of 100 bps on their own datasets would establish where the crossover against the simpler OLPS baselines sits, and that crossover is directly the argument for why a frontier market needs different machinery. It also does not depend on the CSE data-depth question resolving favourably, which puts it on the short list with the wavelet comparison from FreQuant.

- **SOPS is the fourth baseline my evaluation design is missing.** My current three are equal weight, static learned weight and dynamic state-aware weight. A <font color="#ffff00">hard state-conditioned router</font>, a threshold rule that picks one of two objectives with no learning and no confidence input, sits between the second and third and is much closer to real prior art than equal weighting. If my adaptive orchestrator cannot beat a count-and-threshold switch, the soft-weighting contribution is not there, and I would rather discover that in my own ablation than have it asked in a viva. This is a direct proposed revision to the experimental design section of the scope note.

- **The heterogeneity argument gets a cleaner illustration here than anywhere else in the vault.** NPM and NPMRP differ by one penalty term over identical inputs, so the router is choosing between two objectives that fail together, and the paper's own result is that the switch buys 0.8 of cumulative return over always using NPM on one run. That is the sharpest available statement of what homogeneous branches under a router are worth, and it is the contrast I want against weighting a technical agent, a retrieval-grounded fundamental agent, a volatility agent and a liquidity agent that draw on genuinely different evidence.

- **SEMA is a candidate state variable for the orchestrator that costs almost nothing in data, though the arithmetic is tight on a one-year CSE sample.** Fitting two parameters per asset from $(d+1)$ points, each summarising $w$ days, is far cheaper than the FIGARCH route in [[Modeling Long-Term Volatility Memory Dynamics in the Colombo Stock Exchange]], which matters because the only confirmed ready-made CSE set covers February 2025 to February 2026. With roughly 240 trading days and $w \approx 30$ that is 8 segments for a two-parameter regression, thin but not absurd, and shrinking $w$ to recover segments costs precision in each $\mu$ and $\nu$, so pinning the smallest usable $(w, d)$ pair by simulation belongs beside the existing open item on minimum estimation windows. The labelling rule itself is also a testable alternative to MacroHFT's volatility tertile, and its advantage for $A_V$ is that it is per asset rather than per market, giving the orchestrator an asset-level state vector that can be aggregated by a count, as this paper does, or fed in directly. Both are crude and neither has a strong theoretical claim over the other, so implement both and let the ablation decide.

- **The log-barrier diversification term is reusable for $A_V$, and the lesson is to report achieved risk contributions rather than claim parity.** The $-c\sum\ln x_i$ penalty keeps every weight strictly positive and is trivially added to a quadratic objective, which is attractive for a thin market where I want to discourage the optimizer from putting 60% into one counter. But this paper shows a fourteen-fold spread in realised risk contributions while calling the constraint risk parity, so if I use a penalty of this shape I report the realised contributions alongside the weights and describe it as a diversification penalty, not as parity. The concentration result, two or three of 24 assets with near-complete turnover, is also a direct warning about what a net-profit objective does on its own in a market where that turnover would be expensive to execute.

- **The nineteen-baseline OLPS family is the right cheap benchmark set for a short-history market, and I should carry a few of them.** UBAH, UCRP, OLMAR, PAMR, CORN, ONS, EG and Anticor need no training data at all, which is exactly the property that matters if CSE history turns out to be twelve to eighteen months, and several of them beat learned methods on individual datasets here. Including three or four in my comparison tests whether the entire multi-agent apparatus earns its complexity on short data, which is the question a sceptical examiner asks first. Using UBAH as the information-ratio benchmark, as this paper and several of its citations do, is also worth adopting alongside ASPI or S&P SL20, since a passive equal-weight benchmark is comparable across studies in a way that a local index is not.
