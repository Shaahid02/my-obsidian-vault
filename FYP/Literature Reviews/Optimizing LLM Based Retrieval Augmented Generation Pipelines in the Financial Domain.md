NAACL 2024, Industry Track, pages 279 to 294, so the only peer-reviewed paper of the three RAG notes in this vault. Yiyun Zhao, Hanoz Bhathena, Prateek Singh and Saket Sharma with equal contribution, plus Bernardo Ramos, Aviral Joshi and Swaroop Gadiyaram, all at the Machine Learning Center of Excellence, JPMorgan Chase. A factorial benchmark of <font color="#ffc000">1,620 experiments</font>, six generative LLMs plus an extractive RoBERTa baseline, across fifteen retrieval conditions, nine prompts and two proprietary banking datasets. The design move that makes it useful is that retrieval quality is *simulated*, by manipulating a fixed retrieved list rather than by swapping retrievers, which isolates how the generator responds to retrieval quality from the question of how to achieve it.

The note this replaces was an accurate and already fairly analytical summary, better than the starting point for most of these rewrites, and what it lacked was the scoping and the conversion into decisions. It also sits differently now that the other two RAG notes exist. [[Financial Report Chunking for Effective Retrieval Augmented Generation]] and [[MultiFinRAG, An Optimized Multimodal Retrieval-Augmented Generation Framework for Financial Question Answering]] both vary the retriever and measure end to end; this one holds the corpus fixed and varies what the generator is shown. Between them I get both halves of the picture for <font color="#ffc000">$A_F$, the fundamental and retrieval channel</font>: how to retrieve better, and what it is worth downstream when retrieval fails, which is the regime I have already concluded $A_F$ will be in more than half the time.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **Very few studies investigate the contribution of each RAG component or the interactions between them.** Retriever, prompt and generator are usually varied one at a time or not at all, so nobody can say whether a reported improvement came from the retrieval change, the model, or the prompt. The factorial design is a direct answer to this and it is the paper's real contribution.
- **Public RAG benchmarks are likely present in the models' pre-training, so they overstate generalisation.** The authors argue that evaluating on internal data absent from pre-training is a better estimate of real-world behaviour. This is the strongest methodological argument in the paper and it is the same contamination worry I have recorded against every LLM trading paper in the vault, stated here as a design principle rather than a caveat.
- **Deployments need structured output and citations, and instruction-following is rarely measured alongside answer quality.** A RAG system that produces a correct answer in an unparseable format is not usable in a pipeline, and the paper is right that this is almost never reported.

#### Data and setup

Two banking corpora, neither released. The first is public webpages carrying general information on banking products, the second is internal guides for customer service executives detailing policies and protocols. Queries are real, either generated by actual users and gathered from production system logs, or written by subject matter experts. Documents are chunked to roughly 100 words respecting sentence boundaries, with chunks dropped if they are majority non-English or under ten words, and 800 user queries are sampled per dataset.

The pairing of questions to evidence is a two-stage pipeline worth noting because of what it involves. Subject matter experts annotate coarse article-level labels; <font color="#ffff00">GPT-4 is then used as a pairwise relevance filter</font> over chunks from those articles, returning a binary relevance label per chunk against the query; and separately a dense retriever contributes its top ten chunks per query for coverage, since the expert annotations are not comprehensive. The union goes to human reviewers who assign the final binary relevance label to every chunk and mark the answer span within it that serves as the reference answer.

The models are gpt-3.5-turbo-0613 and gpt-4-0613, Llama-2-7b and Llama-2-13b in both base and chat variants, and a RoBERTa answer-span extractor fine-tuned on SQuAD2 and Natural Questions as the baseline. Decoding is not held constant across families, with the OpenAI models at temperature 1 and a 700-token completion cap against greedy decoding and a 200-token cap for the Llama models. Context windows are 15.7K, 7.5K and 3.9K respectively, which at twenty 100-word chunks is not binding.

Answer quality is token F1 against the human-annotated span. Exact match is rejected because responses run to multiple sentences, and reference-free metrics are rejected on cost and on the grounds that they can be score-hacked, specifically that Llama-2 models produce long generations by copying many sentences from the source and so score high on token recall.

#### Experimental design

![[Pasted image 20260913004818.png]]

Fifteen retrieval conditions come from crossing two families with three depths. Retriever-Only returns what the retriever actually found, which may or may not contain a gold chunk, in default or shuffled order. Retriever-With-GT guarantees a gold chunk is in the list, in default position, injected first, or injected last.

| Family | Variants | Depths | Count |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Retriever-Only | default, shuffled | top-3, top-5, top-20 | 6 |
| Retriever-With-GT | default, gold first, gold last | top-3, top-5, top-20 | 9 |

The nine prompts are a factor grid over verbosity, citation format and whether an evaluation metric is named in the instruction, which is the structure that makes the prompt-sensitivity result interpretable:

| | Citation format | |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Simple instructions** | pipe `\|\|\|`, JSON, none | plus quote-required and metric-named variants |
| **Verbose instructions** | pipe `\|\|\|`, JSON, none | |

Instruction-following is measured four ways: the proportion of outputs in correct pipe format `<answer_span>|||<Document_ID>`, the proportion in parseable JSON with the required fields, the proportion of response sentences taken verbatim from the retrieved chunks, and the ratio of output length to reference-answer length as a detector of over-generation and copying. There are no formulas anywhere in the paper and none is needed; token F1 and four proportions are the whole measurement apparatus.

#### Findings

- **Retrieval failure, not generation, is the binding constraint, and it costs roughly half the achievable score.** On the policy guides at top-3, GPT-4 scores 34.3 token F1 when the retriever may or may not have found the gold chunk and <font color="#ffc000">61.8 when the gold chunk is guaranteed present</font>. Llama-2-13B goes 14.1 to 51.7 and GPT-3.5-turbo 26.0 to 46.9 across the same pair. Every generative model also clears the extractive RoBERTa baseline by a wide margin even on a metric that should favour extraction.

  ![[Pasted image 20261004140001.png]]

- **More retrieved documents help when retrieval is unreliable and hurt when it is not, in the same model.** GPT-4 under Retriever-Only rises from 34.3 to 38.6 as $k$ goes from 3 to 20, because deeper retrieval improves the chance the gold chunk is in there at all; under Retriever-With-GT it falls from 61.8 to 55.5 over the same range, because the only thing extra documents add once the gold chunk is guaranteed is distractors. Llama-2-13B shows the same reversal far more sharply, 14.1 to 21.0 against 51.7 to 33.8. This is the cleanest statement of the recall-precision tension in RAG that I have found, and it is why the authors conclude that retrieval optimisation cannot be reduced to maximising recall.
- **Where the gold chunk sits in the list is worth 9 to 15 F1 points at top-20, and costs more for weaker models.** Gold first against gold last: GPT-4 59.9 against 51.0, GPT-3.5-turbo 47.0 against 36.6, Llama-2-13B 39.3 against 23.9. Under Retriever-Only, shuffling the order barely registers, which the authors read as retrieval failures dominating and swamping any ordering effect, and that reading fits the numbers. The practical consequence is that re-ranking is not a marginal optimisation, it is worth more on this data than the gap between GPT-3.5-turbo and GPT-4 under some conditions.
- **Pseudo-helpfulness is named, defined and demonstrated, and it is the behavioural account of why abstention is hard.** When the gold document is missing, models answer anyway, drawing on context that is somewhat related but does not address the question, through to outright hallucination, rather than returning "No Answer Found". The authors are careful that even GPT-4 is imperfect at this while being better at it than the others. The framing matters: this is not a bug in a particular model, it is the instruction-tuned disposition to be helpful overriding the grounding constraint that RAG depends on.
- **Prompt variation barely moves the OpenAI models, and the apparent Llama instability is mostly one output format failing.** Across the nine prompts GPT-4 spans 0.48 to 0.51 token F1, GPT-3.5-turbo 0.34 to 0.40 and Llama-2-7b 0.16 to 0.31, which the paper reports as under 4%, around 6% and around 15% variation. Reading their heatmap against their instruction-following figure gives a narrower account: Llama-2-7b scores 0.24, 0.27, 0.24 and 0.16 on the four pipe-citation prompts and 0.31 on every one of the other five, and separately achieves 0.9% compliance with the pipe citation format. So the F1 spread is concentrated in prompts demanding a format the model cannot produce, not in general sensitivity to wording. That is my read of their figures rather than a claim the paper makes, and it changes the advice from "small models are prompt-fragile" to "do not ask a small model for a format it cannot emit".
- **Instruction-following separates the model families far more sharply than answer quality does.** Pipe-format citation compliance runs 98.7% and 94.0% for GPT-4 and GPT-3.5-turbo against 57.6%, 31.9%, 11.5% and 0.9% for the four Llama variants, and on JSON the split is 99.9% and 99.3% against <font color="#ff0000">0.0%, 0.0%, 0.1% and 0.0%</font>, meaning the open models of this generation essentially never produced a parseable JSON object with the required fields. The completion-to-reference ratio tells the same story from the other side, 0.9 and 0.6 for the OpenAI models against 3.0 and 2.9 for the Llama base models, which is the copying behaviour that inflates their token recall.

#### Limitations

- **Retrieval quality is simulated by manipulating one retrieved list, and no retriever is ever compared against another.** The authors state this plainly in their own limitations. The consequence is that the paper characterises how generation responds to retrieval quality and says nothing about how to obtain it, which makes it the exact complement of the chunking paper rather than a substitute, and means none of its numbers can be read as achievable by building a better index.
- **The answer-quality metric is compromised for the models that score lowest, in the direction that flatters them.** Llama base models produce roughly three times the reference length by copying consecutive source sentences, which mechanically raises token recall and therefore token F1. The authors identify this and keep the metric anyway, reasonably, since the alternatives are costly or hackable in other ways. The implication they do not draw is that <font color="#ffc000">the measured gap between the OpenAI and Llama models is a lower bound</font> on the true gap rather than an estimate of it.
- **Both datasets are proprietary and cannot be released, which is also the source of the paper's best methodological claim.** The argument that internal data absent from pre-training gives a better estimate of generalisation is correct and is the thing I most want to borrow, and it is precisely what makes every number here unverifiable by anyone outside JPMorgan. Nobody can reproduce a single cell, compare a new model on the same data, or check the annotation quality. Same situation as MultiFinRAG, arrived at for a better reason.
- **Decoding is not held constant across the families being compared.** Temperature 1 with 700 completion tokens for the OpenAI models against greedy decoding with a 200-token cap for the Llama models. Both are defensible defaults, neither is the same experimental treatment, and the 200-token cap in particular interacts with a metric scored on token overlap against a span. The effect is almost certainly small next to gaps of 20 to 30 F1 points, but in a paper whose whole contribution is a controlled factorial design, leaving a factor uncontrolled across the main comparison is a gap.
- **GPT-4 helped construct the dataset it is then scored on.** The chunk-relevance candidate pool was filtered by GPT-4 acting as a pairwise relevance judge before human reviewers adjudicated. Humans assign the final labels and the dense-retriever union adds chunks GPT-4 never saw, so this is not circular in the strong sense, but the evidence pool GPT-4 is later evaluated against was partly shaped by GPT-4's own notion of relevance, and no check for the effect is reported.
- **Six models from two families, all of 2023 vintage, no open model above 13B and nothing with a reasoning mode.** The authors list this themselves. Llama-2-13B is the largest open model tested, the field moved substantially within a year, and the vendor-versus-open gap measured here should be read as a dated snapshot rather than a standing property of model classes. The quantized 12B Gemma beating GPT-4o on multimodal questions in the MultiFinRAG note is the direct counterexample from eighteen months later.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **This is the half of the RAG picture the other two notes do not cover, and the three now compose into one design.** Chunking tells me how to build the index, MultiFinRAG how to handle statements and figures within it, and this one what happens downstream when the index fails to surface the right thing. The composed design for $A_F$ is structure-aware chunking with a separate table index to maximise the chance the gold chunk is retrieved at all, a re-ranking stage to put it first, and an explicit abstention path for when it is not there. None of the three papers specifies that stack and all three are needed to justify it.
- **Re-ranking moves from a nice-to-have to a required stage, and I can size the benefit from this data.** Gold-first against gold-last at top-20 is worth 8.9 F1 points for GPT-4 and 15.4 for Llama-2-13B. That is larger than the entire end-to-end gain structure-aware chunking bought in the chunking note. It also closes a reporting gap I flagged there: I noted that paper reports no precision@k or mean reciprocal rank, so nothing said where in the ranking the right chunk sat, and this paper shows that position is worth enough that <font color="#ffff00">$A_F$'s retrieval evaluation has to report rank, not just hit rate</font>.
- **The top-$k$ reversal is a tuning rule whose correct setting depends on which regime $A_F$ is in, so it cannot be fixed once.** More context helps when retrieval is unreliable and hurts when it is reliable. On the CSE, against a corpus for which I have not yet built or tuned a retriever, $A_F$ starts in the unreliable regime where a larger $k$ is the right default, and that default has to be revisited once retrieval improves. This belongs in the experimental design as a parameter that is re-swept rather than a constant carried over from a tutorial.
- **Pseudo-helpfulness gives me a new required experiment rather than only a caution.** I had concluded from [[FinanceBench, A New Benchmark for Financial Question Answering]] that abstention and error are different failures for the orchestrator and that $A_F$ should refuse rather than guess. This supplies the reason refusing is hard, the instruction-tuned disposition to be helpful overriding the grounding constraint, and the implication is that abstention needs its own test rather than being assumed from a system prompt. The concrete addition is a set of probe questions whose supporting evidence I have deliberately removed from the index, scored purely on whether $A_F$ declines, reported alongside the correct, incorrect and abstain breakdown on the normal probe set.
- **Instruction-following compliance is a hard constraint on the structured-output contract I have already committed to.** In the TAT-QA and MultiFinRAG notes I settled on $A_F$ emitting a typed object, value with scale, currency and period, as structured output rather than prose. This paper shows that the open models of its generation produced parseable JSON essentially never, 0.0% to 0.1%, while the OpenAI models managed over 99%. The specific numbers are dated and the counterexample exists, but the constraint is real: before the orchestrator depends on a structured contract I have to verify that the model I can actually run on available hardware produces it reliably, and if it does not, the fallback is a simpler delimiter format or constrained decoding rather than hoping and parsing defensively.
- **This corrects a cross-reference I made last turn, and the corrected version is the more useful one.** I wrote in the MultiFinRAG note that the same Gemma-over-Llama ordering appears here. It does not, since this paper tests no Gemma at all, and its finding runs the other way, a large gap in favour of vendor models over small open ones. What the two papers actually agree on is narrower and more valuable, that <font color="#ffc000">the backbone dominates the pipeline</font> in financial RAG; where they differ is which side of the vendor-open line the stronger backbone sat, and eighteen months of open-model progress is the obvious explanation. The MultiFinRAG note is fixed.
- **The contamination argument turns a weakness of my setting into an evaluation strength, and I should say so explicitly.** The paper's case for internal data is that public benchmarks are likely in pre-training, so performance on them overstates generalisation. CSE filings are public but obscure, and a Sri Lankan listed company's annual report is far less likely to be well represented in a frontier model's pre-training than a Morgan Stanley 10-Q or an Apple 10-K. Every LLM trading paper in my set carries the contamination problem I keep recording against them; my corpus largely does not, and that is worth stating in the proposal as a reason the CSE evaluation is more informative rather than merely harder.
- **Ceiling-setting, from a fourth independent direction.** Even with the gold chunk guaranteed present and injected into the first position, GPT-4 reaches 62.5 token F1 on a task where a human marked the exact answer span inside a 100-word chunk of clean English banking text. Perfect retrieval does not produce a correct answer, it produces roughly a 60% one on short factual spans. Set beside FinanceBench's 50% under a realistic retriever, the chunking paper's 53.19% end to end, and the low 40s on computed quantities in [[FinQA, A Dataset of Numerical Reasoning over Financial Data]] and [[TAT-QA, A Question Answering Benchmark on a Hybrid of Tabular and Textual Content in Finance]] with no retrieval required at all, the working assumption that $A_F$ lands below half on computed questions over CSE filings now has four independent supports and should be stated as a design premise rather than a worry.
