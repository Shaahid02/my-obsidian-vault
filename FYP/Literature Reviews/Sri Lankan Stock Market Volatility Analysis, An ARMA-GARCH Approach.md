Uses an ARMA(2,2)-GARCH family framework to estimate and forecast volatility on the Colombo Stock Exchange, comparing symmetric and asymmetric GARCH specifications on the ASPI from January 2018 to December 2022, a window that swallows the Easter Sunday attacks, COVID-19, and the 2022 economic crisis.

Core motivation: CSE is repeatedly described in the literature as a sluggish, developing market prone to periodic instability, and that instability is exactly what scares off long-term investors. If volatility isn't estimated accurately, neither investors nor policymakers get a reliable read on market risk, so the paper's whole pitch is that getting the mean and variance equations right for ASPI during a genuinely turbulent five-year stretch has direct value for market development, not just as an econometric exercise. The other half of the motivation is methodological: ARCH/GARCH models assume normality, which clashes with the fat tails and non-normal returns actually seen in financial data, so the paper wants to test whether asymmetric extensions (EGARCH, TGARCH, GJR-GARCH) actually earn their keep over plain symmetric GARCH in the Sri Lankan context specifically, since most of the supporting evidence for that claim comes from other markets.

#### Gap it's addressing

- ARMA-GARCH applicability in the Sri Lankan context specifically is limited. Most of the empirical grounding for ARMA-GARCH outperforming standalone ARCH/GARCH comes from other markets (China, Malaysia, international oil prices), and locally the closest comparable work (Zhang and Nadarajah, 2018) used ARMA-APARCH on CSE returns from 1985 to 2017, not ARMA-GARCH.
- Prior Sri Lankan volatility studies split into contradictory camps. Hewamana et al. (2022), Jahufer (2021), and Jegajeevan (2012) find asymmetric GARCH models fit CSE volatility and leverage effects better, but Jaleel and Samarakoon (2009) find no leverage effect at all in their EGARCH results, and Silva et al. (2016) and Morawakage and Nimal (2016) each use a single GARCH variant (GARCH(1,1) and TGARCH respectively) rather than comparing model families head to head. So there wasn't a study running the full symmetric vs asymmetric GARCH lineup against each other on CSE data with a consistent mean equation.
- No study had specifically targeted this compounded 2018-2022 window, where CSE absorbed the Easter attacks, a pandemic, and an economic crisis back to back, as its own volatility regime.

#### Data and setup

Daily closing prices of the ASPI from the CSE, 1 January 2018 to 31 December 2022, converted to log returns (1160 observations after periods of extended market closure were dropped from the series to avoid treating those gaps as outliers). The model is built in two stages, same general logic as the long-memory FIGARCH paper on CSE:

- **Mean equation**: ARMA(2,2), selected after ADF/Phillip-Perron confirmed stationarity but the ACF/PACF plots showed serial dependence beyond what a simpler order would capture. All four AR/MA coefficients came out significant (AR(1) = 0.79, AR(2) = 0.136, MA(1) = -0.509, MA(2) = -0.359).
- **Variance equation**: residuals from the ARMA model were checked for ARCH effects with a Lagrange Multiplier test (strongly significant at both lag 1 and lag 2), then five GARCH-family models were fit to those residuals: GARCH(1,1) under normality, GARCH(t)(1,1) under a skewed Student's t distribution, and three asymmetric models, EGARCH(1,1), TGARCH(1,1), and GJR-GARCH(1,1).

Model fit was compared using AIC/BIC and log-likelihood, residual adequacy was checked with the Ljung-Box test, and out-of-sample accuracy was tested with a 45-day rolling window forecast (21 October to 28 December 2022) scored on MSE, MAE, and RMSE.

#### Findings

- Every GARCH specification estimated a volatility persistence (alpha + beta) close to one, meaning shocks to ASPI volatility decay very slowly rather than dying out quickly. The paper doesn't push this further into a long-memory framework, but it's the same signal the FIGARCH paper picks up on more directly.
- Among the symmetric models, TGARCH had the best AIC/BIC fit and plain GARCH(1,1) the worst, so even within "symmetric" specifications there's a meaningful spread in how well they capture ASPI's behaviour.
- Between the asymmetric models, GJR-GARCH outperformed EGARCH on AIC/BIC for in-sample estimation. Both showed statistically significant asymmetry, EGARCH's leverage term was only weakly significant at 90 percent confidence (gamma = -0.038, p = 0.063), while GJR-GARCH's was clearly significant at 95 percent (gamma = 0.103, p = 0.022).
- The leverage effect itself confirms that negative news moves ASPI volatility more than positive news of the same size, and the paper reads this as idiosyncratic volatility driven by irrational investor behaviour, i.e. the market overreacts to bad news specifically rather than reacting symmetrically to information in general.
- Ljung-Box tests on the fitted residuals came back clean (no serial correlation left at 95 percent confidence), so the ARMA-GARCH specification is doing its job on the mean and variance jointly.
- The out-of-sample rolling forecast flips the in-sample ranking: EGARCH had the lowest forecast errors (MSE, MAE, RMSE) of all five models, ahead of both symmetric GARCH variants, with GJR-GARCH and TGARCH trailing. So the model that explains the in-sample dynamics best (GJR-GARCH) isn't the one that forecasts best out-of-sample (EGARCH), which the paper flags as a genuine tension rather than resolving cleanly.
- Overall conclusion: asymmetric GARCH models are more reliable than symmetric ones for both estimating and forecasting ASPI volatility, and the leverage effect is real and economically meaningful for CSE, negative information causes disproportionately high volatility.

#### Limitations

- The five models aren't run under a consistent distributional assumption. Plain GARCH(1,1) assumes normality, GARCH(t) uses a skewed Student's t, and the asymmetric models use whatever distribution best fit each one individually, so the AIC/BIC comparison across models is partly a comparison of functional form and partly a comparison of distributional assumption, and those two things aren't cleanly separated out.
- Only ARMA(2,2) is tested as the mean specification, with no reported comparison against other candidate orders, so it's not fully clear how sensitive the downstream GARCH results are to that particular mean equation choice.
- The persistence estimates sit close to one across every model, which is exactly the kind of result that motivates a long-memory model like FIGARCH, but the paper doesn't test for or model long memory at all. It stops at "close to one" without asking whether that's actually a unit root, near-unit-root short memory, or genuine long memory.
- Only ASPI is tested. There's no equivalent run on S&P SL20 (the blue-chip index), so unlike the FIGARCH paper's dual-index comparison, this study can't say whether the volatility dynamics and leverage effect it finds are a broad-market phenomenon or would look different on the more liquid, large-cap slice of CSE.
- The in-sample best model (GJR-GARCH) and out-of-sample best model (EGARCH) disagree, and the paper doesn't dig into why beyond noting the contrast, so it's left a bit open which model an investor or policymaker should actually trust going forward.
- Periods of market closure are simply excluded from the return series rather than modeled explicitly, which is a reasonable practical choice but does mean the "continuous" return series has silent gaps stitched together.

#### Why this matters for my project

- This paper is essentially the direct econometric anchor for my topic, it's the closest existing local study running the full ARMA-GARCH symmetric vs asymmetric lineup on ASPI, so my own volatility modeling work is either replicating, extending, or stress-testing this exact framework rather than starting from a blank slate.
- The persistence estimates being close to one here line up with what the long-memory FIGARCH paper found more explicitly. Read together, these two papers make a fairly strong case that CSE volatility isn't just clustering short-term, it's carrying memory that a standard ARMA-GARCH setup can pick up on indirectly (via near-unit persistence) but isn't built to characterize properly. That's a natural extension point if I want to push this paper's setup further.
- The in-sample vs out-of-sample disagreement (GJR-GARCH fits best, EGARCH forecasts best) is a useful cautionary note for my own evaluation design, whatever model I end up using, I need to report both estimation fit and forecast accuracy separately rather than assuming one implies the other.
- The leverage effect finding, negative news causing outsized volatility because of irrational investor behaviour, connects directly to the market liquidity and sentiment paper's behavioural read on CSE (turnover behaving like an attention/demand signal rather than a rational overvaluation signal). Both papers are independently pointing at the same underlying story, CSE investor behaviour doesn't follow the assumptions built into standard developed-market models, and that's a theme worth carrying across whichever angle my own project ends up taking.
- Since this study only looks at ASPI, the ASPI vs S&P SL20 segmentation gap flagged in the long-memory paper still stands here too, if I'm building on this paper's approach, testing whether the leverage effect and persistence hold up on the blue-chip index as well would be a fairly natural robustness check.
