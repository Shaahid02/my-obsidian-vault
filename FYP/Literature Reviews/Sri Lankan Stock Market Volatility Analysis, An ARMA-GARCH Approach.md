Uses an ARMA(2,2)-GARCH family framework to estimate and forecast volatility on the Colombo Stock Exchange, comparing symmetric and asymmetric GARCH specifications on the ASPI from January 2018 to December 2022, a window that swallows the Easter Sunday attacks, COVID-19, and the 2022 economic crisis.

Core motivation: CSE is repeatedly described in the literature as a sluggish, developing market prone to periodic instability, and that instability is exactly what scares off long-term investors. If volatility isn't estimated accurately, neither investors nor policymakers get a reliable read on market risk, so the paper's whole pitch is that getting the mean and variance equations right for ASPI during a genuinely turbulent five-year stretch has direct value for market development, not just as an econometric exercise. The other half of the motivation is methodological: ARCH/GARCH models assume normality, which clashes with the fat tails and non-normal returns actually seen in financial data, so the paper wants to test whether asymmetric extensions (EGARCH, TGARCH, GJR-GARCH) actually earn their keep over plain symmetric GARCH in the Sri Lankan context specifically, since most of the supporting evidence for that claim comes from other markets.

#### Gap it's addressing

- ARMA-GARCH applicability in the Sri Lankan context specifically is limited. Most of the empirical grounding for ARMA-GARCH outperforming standalone ARCH/GARCH comes from other markets (China, Malaysia, international oil prices), and locally the closest comparable work (Zhang and Nadarajah, 2018) used ARMA-APARCH on CSE returns from 1985 to 2017, not ARMA-GARCH.
- Prior Sri Lankan volatility studies split into contradictory camps. Hewamana et al. (2022), Jahufer (2021), and Jegajeevan (2012) find asymmetric GARCH models fit CSE volatility and leverage effects better, but Jaleel and Samarakoon (2009) find no leverage effect at all in their EGARCH results, and Silva et al. (2016) and Morawakage and Nimal (2016) each use a single GARCH variant (GARCH(1,1) and TGARCH respectively) rather than comparing model families head to head. So there wasn't a study running the full symmetric vs asymmetric GARCH lineup against each other on CSE data with a consistent mean equation.
- No study had specifically targeted this compounded 2018-2022 window, where CSE absorbed the Easter attacks, a pandemic, and an economic crisis back to back, as its own volatility regime.

#### Data and setup

Daily closing prices of the ASPI from the CSE, 1 January 2018 to 31 December 2022, converted to log returns (1160 observations after periods of extended market closure were dropped from the series to avoid treating those gaps as outliers). The opening diagnostics set up everything that follows: Jarque-Bera rejects normality on both the closing prices (422.170) and the returns (2829.449), residual skewness is negative at -0.280 with kurtosis at 11.080, and the Hurst exponent on the log returns comes in at 0.01 (Appendix Figure 1), so the return levels are about as strongly mean reverting as a series gets, before any variance modelling starts. The model is built in two stages, same general logic as the long-memory FIGARCH paper on CSE:

- **Mean equation**: ARMA(2,2), selected after ADF/Phillip-Perron confirmed stationarity but the ACF/PACF plots showed serial dependence beyond what a simpler order would capture. All four AR/MA coefficients came out significant (AR(1) = 0.79, AR(2) = 0.136, MA(1) = -0.509, MA(2) = -0.359), alongside a fifth estimated term of 1.571 (t = 52.424) that Table 3 labels Sigma but Equation 3.1 writes into the mean equation as its constant.
- **Variance equation**: residuals from the ARMA model were checked for ARCH effects with a Lagrange Multiplier test (test statistics of 165.916 at lag 1 and 96.776 at lag 2, both with vanishing p-values), then five GARCH-family models were fit to those residuals: GARCH(1,1) under normality, GARCH(t)(1,1) under a skewed Student's t distribution, TGARCH(1,1), EGARCH(1,1), and GJR-GARCH(1,1). The paper groups TGARCH with the symmetric models and counts only EGARCH and GJR-GARCH as asymmetric, which is a non-standard call since Zakoian's threshold specification is conventionally an asymmetric model, but I follow the paper's grouping below so its own AIC/BIC comparisons stay readable.

Model fit was compared using AIC/BIC and log-likelihood, residual adequacy was checked with the Ljung-Box test, and forecast accuracy was tested with a 45-day rolling window forecast (21 October to 28 December 2022) scored on MSE, MAE, and RMSE. The methodology section calls this an out-of-sample exercise, but both passages that actually discuss the results call it in-sample forecasting, so I treat the 45-day window as in-sample on the paper's own final wording.

#### Findings

- Every GARCH specification estimated a volatility persistence (alpha + beta) close to one, meaning shocks to ASPI volatility decay very slowly rather than dying out quickly, and the paper reads this as a finite persistence common to all five fits. That reading sits awkwardly against the model's own stationarity condition, since Equation 4 requires the persistence sum to stay strictly below one for the unconditional variance to exist at all, and the unconditional variance is precisely what that sum controls:

$$\sigma^2 = \frac{\omega}{1 - \alpha_1 - \beta_1} \qquad \text{and, for GJR-GARCH} \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{\omega}{1 - \alpha_1 - \frac{\gamma_1}{2} - \beta_1}$$

	where:
	- $\omega$ is the constant of the variance equation, estimated between 0.016 and 0.025 across the five fits
	- $\alpha_1 + \beta_1$ is the persistence sum, with $\gamma_1/2$ added in the GJR-GARCH form because the leverage term fires on roughly half the innovations
	- the condition stated in Equation 4 is $0 \leq \alpha_1, \beta_1 \leq 1$ and $(\alpha_1 + \beta_1) < 1$

	Plugging the published estimates in, GARCH(1,1) gives 0.214 + 0.786 = 1.000, GARCH(t) and TGARCH both give 0.226 + 0.774 = 1.000, and GJR-GARCH gives 0.177 + 0.0515 + 0.772 = 1.0005, so the denominator collapses to zero or goes negative and the unconditional variance isn't defined at the paper's own parameters. The paper describes this as finite volatility persistence without flagging that the stationarity condition it states in Equation 4 is exactly binding or marginally violated in four of its five fits. EGARCH is the exception only because its log-linear form puts beta on a different scale, not because it's better behaved.

- Among the models the paper groups as symmetric, TGARCH had the best AIC/BIC fit (2861.22 and 2881.45) and plain GARCH(1,1) the worst (3012.96 and 3028.13), so even within that group there's a meaningful spread in how well the specifications capture ASPI's behaviour. That result is hard to lean on though, because the paper defines a threshold parameter for TGARCH and then never reports an estimate for it. Equation 6 is where the asymmetry is supposed to enter, through a term that lets a negative innovation shift conditional volatility by a different amount than a positive one of the same size:

$$\zeta_t^2 = \omega + \alpha_1\left(\left|\varepsilon_{t-1}\right| - \gamma_1 a_{t-1}\right) + \beta_1\zeta_{t-1}$$

	where:
	- $\zeta_t$ is conditional volatility, estimated here as a standard deviation rather than a variance
	- $\varepsilon_{t-1}$ is the standardized residual and $a_{t-1}$ the innovation at $t-1$
	- $\gamma_1$ is the threshold term carrying the asymmetry, which Table 5 leaves blank

	With no estimate for $\gamma_1$ published, and with TGARCH's omega, alpha and beta rounding to the same three decimals as GARCH(t)'s (0.019, 0.226, 0.774), the TGARCH row as printed is empirically indistinguishable from the symmetric t-GARCH fit, which is likely why the paper grouped it with the symmetric models in the first place.

- Between the two models the paper does treat as asymmetric, GJR-GARCH beat EGARCH on both AIC (2858.35 against 2865.70) and BIC (2883.63 against 2890.98) for in-sample estimation. GJR-GARCH is also the specification I'd actually reimplement, and its mechanism is a single indicator that switches an extra variance term on only when the previous innovation was negative:

$$\zeta_t^2 = \omega + \alpha_1 a_{t-1}^2 + \beta_1\sigma_{t-1}^2 + \gamma_1 N_{t-1}a_{t-1}^2, \qquad N_{t-1} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } a_{t-1} < 0 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

	where:
	- $a_{t-1}$ is the innovation at $t-1$ and $N_{t-1}$ the negative-innovation indicator
	- $\gamma_1$ is the leverage term, estimated at 0.103 with $p = 0.022$
	- the paper writes $\zeta_t^2$ on the left and $\sigma_{t-1}^2$ on the right without reconciling the notation, and its printed definition of $N_{t-1}$ gives the condition $a_{t-1} < 0$ on both branches, which has to be a typo for $a_{t-1} \geq 0$ on the second

	At the reported estimates a negative shock therefore loads 0.177 + 0.103 = 0.280 onto next period's conditional variance against 0.177 for a positive shock of the same magnitude, so on this sample bad news moves ASPI volatility about 1.6 times as hard as good news. The two asymmetry terms don't carry equal weight, though: GJR-GARCH's is clearly significant at 95 percent (gamma = 0.103, p = 0.022), EGARCH's is only weakly significant at 90 percent (gamma = -0.038, p = 0.063), and EGARCH's omega isn't significant at all (p = 0.246). Across all five fits GJR-GARCH's AIC win doesn't make it the outright in-sample winner either, since TGARCH's BIC of 2881.45 comes in below its 2883.63.
- The leverage effect itself is consistent with negative news moving ASPI volatility more than positive news of the same size, and the paper reads this as idiosyncratic volatility driven by irrational investor behaviour, i.e. the market overreacting to bad news specifically rather than reacting symmetrically to information in general. On the evidence reported that reading rests mainly on GJR-GARCH, since EGARCH's leverage term clears only the 90 percent bar.
- Ljung-Box tests on the fitted residuals came back clean, with p-values running from 0.323 to 0.921 across lags 1 to 5, so no serial correlation is left at 95 percent confidence and the ARMA-GARCH specification is doing its job on the mean and variance jointly.
- The 45-day rolling forecast flips the estimation ranking: EGARCH posted the lowest MSE (0.874), MAE (0.799) and RMSE (0.935) of all five models, ahead of plain GARCH (0.901, 0.809, 0.949) and GARCH(t) (1.026, 0.861, 1.013), with GJR-GARCH (1.064, 0.875, 1.031) and TGARCH (1.11, 0.892, 1.054) trailing. Two caveats on how far that carries: Table 7 also reports a Mean row on which EGARCH is the worst of the five at 2.065 against TGARCH's 1.697, and the paper calls this exercise in-sample forecasting in both places where it discusses the result, so it isn't the out-of-sample validation the error metrics look like at first glance. The paper's own conclusion is that asymmetric models are more reliable than symmetric ones for both estimating and forecasting ASPI volatility, and that the leverage effect is real and economically meaningful for CSE.

> [!WARNING] Source reporting inconsistencies
> Four things in the paper don't line up internally, and each one matters if I cite its numbers:
> - The ARCH LM test reports p-values of 9.37e-37 and 1.388e-39 and is then described as *accepting* the null hypothesis at 95 percent confidence (p. 68). With p-values that size the test rejects no-ARCH, which is also what the rest of the paper proceeds on, so the sentence reads as a slip rather than a result.
> - TGARCH's alpha and beta carry p-values of 0.007 in Table 5 and 0.000 in Appendix Table 1 for the same coefficients, so one of the two is a transcription error.
> - The 1.571 term is labelled Sigma in both Table 3 and Appendix Table 1, but enters Equation 3.1 as the mean equation's constant, and the paper never reconciles the two readings.
> - The 45-day forecast is called out-of-sample in the methodology (p. 66) and in-sample in both the results discussion (p. 70) and the Table 7 commentary (p. 71).

#### Limitations

- The five models aren't run under a documented, consistent distributional assumption. The paper states normality for GARCH(1,1) and a skewed Student's t for GARCH(t) and GJR-GARCH, and says nothing at all about what EGARCH and TGARCH are fit under. So the AIC/BIC ranking across the five is partly a comparison of functional form and partly a comparison of distributional assumption, and with two of the five undocumented I can't separate those two things even in principle from what's reported.
- Only ARMA(2,2) is tested as the mean specification, with no reported comparison against other candidate orders, so it's not clear how sensitive the downstream GARCH results are to that particular mean equation choice.
- The persistence estimates sit close to one across every model, which is exactly the kind of result that motivates a long-memory model like FIGARCH, but the paper doesn't test for or model long memory at all. It stops at "close to one" without asking whether that's actually a unit root, near-unit-root short memory, or genuine long memory.
- Only ASPI is tested. There's no equivalent run on S&P SL20 (the blue-chip index), so unlike the FIGARCH paper's dual-index comparison, this study can't say whether the volatility dynamics and leverage effect it finds are a broad-market phenomenon or would look different on the more liquid, large-cap slice of CSE.
- The model selection never resolves. GJR-GARCH wins on AIC, TGARCH wins on BIC, and EGARCH wins on every forecast error metric, and the paper notes the estimation versus forecasting contrast without adjudicating it or acknowledging the AIC/BIC split at all. So it's left open which model an investor or policymaker should actually trust going forward, and the "asymmetric models are more reliable" conclusion is carried mostly by GJR-GARCH on estimation and EGARCH on forecasting rather than by any single specification doing both.
- Periods of market closure are simply excluded from the return series rather than modeled explicitly, which is a reasonable practical choice but does mean the "continuous" return series has silent gaps stitched together.

#### Why this matters for my project

- This paper is essentially the direct econometric anchor for my topic, it's the closest existing local study running the full ARMA-GARCH symmetric vs asymmetric lineup on ASPI, so my own volatility modeling work is either replicating, extending, or stress-testing this exact framework rather than starting from a blank slate.
- The persistence estimates being close to one here line up with what the long-memory FIGARCH paper found more explicitly, and the Hurst exponent of 0.01 sharpens that contrast rather than muddying it: the return levels are about as mean reverting as a series can be, while the conditional variance carries shocks that barely decay. Read together, these two papers make a fairly strong case that CSE volatility isn't just clustering short-term, it's carrying memory that a standard ARMA-GARCH setup picks up indirectly through near-unit persistence but isn't built to characterize properly. That's a natural extension point if I want to push this paper's setup further.
- The disagreement between estimation fit and forecast accuracy is a useful cautionary note for my own evaluation design, and it's worse than a two-way split: three different selection rules pick three different models off the same five fits. Whatever model I end up using, I need to report estimation fit and forecast accuracy separately, fix the selection criterion before I look at the results, and hold out a genuine out-of-sample window rather than relying on the kind of in-sample rolling exercise this paper runs.
- The leverage effect finding, negative news causing outsized volatility because of irrational investor behaviour, connects directly to the market liquidity and sentiment paper's behavioural read on CSE (turnover behaving like an attention/demand signal rather than a rational overvaluation signal). Both papers are independently pointing at the same underlying story, CSE investor behaviour doesn't follow the assumptions built into standard developed-market models, and that's a theme worth carrying across whichever angle my own project ends up taking.
- Since this study only looks at ASPI, the ASPI vs S&P SL20 segmentation gap flagged in the long-memory paper still stands here too, if I'm building on this paper's approach, testing whether the leverage effect and persistence hold up on the blue-chip index as well would be a fairly natural robustness check.
- The reporting inconsistencies above are a reminder to pull figures from this paper's tables and appendix together rather than from either alone, and to cite the appendix estimates where the two disagree, since those carry standard errors and t-statistics while the summary table doesn't.
