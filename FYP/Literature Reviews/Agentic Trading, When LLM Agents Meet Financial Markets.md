Xia, You, Wang, Liu, Qi, Wu and Zhang, Shenzhen University with one author at the Shenzhen Audencia Financial Technology Institute, 2026, fifty-nine pages of which roughly the last fifteen are appendices and references. It presents itself as a survey of LLM-based trading agents, but the taxonomy is not what it is selling. The authors screened a candidate registry of **92** records down to **77** included studies, then imposed an inclusion boundary admitting a study to the primary evidence tier only if the system emits tradable actions and those actions are evaluated in a closed loop, which leaves a primary empirical subset of **19**, and they report what those 19 papers actually disclose about data splits, transaction costs, universe construction, execution semantics and runnable artifacts. Almost none of them disclose any of it. I am reading this for two jobs, first as the citation for why my evaluation protocol has to be specified before the architecture is, an argument I have been making from individual papers and can now make from a counted corpus, and second because its minimum reporting checklist is the closest thing in this literature to a specification of what an agentic trading paper owes its reader, which I intend to build the CSE evaluation against rather than inventing my own.

#### Gap it's addressing

The survey's stated complaint about prior reviews is that they organise the field by task coverage or by model-level benchmark performance and treat execution as an afterthought, which produces three specific misclassifications: execution gets read as a byproduct of reasoning rather than as a first-class architectural component, mechanism gets conflated with function so that a multi-agent alpha-mining system and a multi-agent trading system land in the same bucket, and adaptation gets treated as an implementation detail rather than as a cross-cutting dimension with its own reporting obligations. Their answer is <font color="#ffff00">Architecture-Capability-Adaptation (A-C-A)</font>, offered explicitly as a working analytical lens rather than a validated taxonomy, plus an evidence ledger, a reproducibility audit and a reporting checklist that they name as the actual contributions.

Set against [[Large Language Model Agents for Investment Management, Foundations, Benchmarks, and Research Frontiers|the BlackRock investment-management survey]], the difference is the thing I most wanted. That survey has no review protocol at all, no search strategy and no inclusion criteria, so an empty cell in its taxonomy cannot be read as a gap in the field. This one states its databases, its query patterns, its date window and its exclusion categories, and it separates a primary empirical tier from a background tier so that percentages have a fixed denominator. Whatever else it gets wrong, the denominators are visible, and that changes what I am allowed to cite it for.

#### Review protocol and evidence scope

The formal search covers **2022-01-01 through 2026-03-09** over the ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, arXiv, SSRN and Google Scholar, expanded through backward and forward citation chaining, with 2022 chosen as the start because that is when LLM-based systems begin appearing as end-to-end trading agents rather than as isolated NLP or forecasting components. Screening ran in four steps, database and preprint keyword search, deduplication, title and abstract screening against the finance/trading domain, then full-text screening that assigns each retained record to primary evidence or background context.

Two criteria decide the primary subset, and the whole paper hangs off them: *Action Output*, meaning the system emits tradable actions such as orders, position changes or portfolio allocations, and *Closed-Loop Evaluation*, meaning those actions are evaluated in a backtest, simulation or live trading rather than only through predictive metrics. Figure 1 is the cleanest statement of where that boundary falls, and it is worth looking at because it is also a map of how my own components would be classified.

Everything left of the dashed line is out of the primary tier. FinBERT and StockBERT are prediction models, AlphaGen and FactorMiner add a decision layer but do not execute, and only systems from Partial Agents rightward carry memory, action and adaptation. Applied to what I am building, a sentiment channel or a volatility estimator on its own sits left of that line and enters as background, and only the orchestrated system with an execution layer would count as primary evidence under this scheme. That is a useful discipline for how I write up component-level experiments, because it tells me they are not evidence of trading value on their own.

The numbers that come out of the funnel are 92 candidate records, 15 final exclusions for being outside the finance/trading/portfolio/risk domain, 77 in the included evidence-mapping ledger, and that 77 partitioned into **19 primary** and **58 background**. Two reliability audits are reported. The screening-boundary audit (S1) covers 182 raw screening records but predates the final 92-record registry, so the authors retain it only as a screening-stage quality check and explicitly refuse to use it as evidence for extraction-level fields. A second extraction-level audit (S3) covers 15 fixed-seed primary studies, seed 20260426, reporting 100.0% agreement after adjudication. Missing or unclear reporting is coded conservatively as `NR/Unknown` rather than imputed, which matters for how the headline percentages should be read.

One corpus fact frames everything else: **18/19** primary studies were published between 2024 and 2026, and **17/19** are currently coded as non-peer-reviewed against 2/19 peer-reviewed. This is an early-stage literature being audited against a standard it has not yet had time to adopt.

#### The A-C-A lens and the reporting checklist

Architecture covers perception, memory, reasoning and action/execution. Capability covers alpha discovery, portfolio management and risk management. Adaptation covers in-context learning, supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning and self-evolution. Layered on top is an expert-system reading of the decision pipeline: perception is knowledge acquisition, memory and RAG are the knowledge base, reasoning is the inference engine, action and execution are the action interface, risk and compliance are rule constraints, and logs plus human oversight are the accountability layer. The framing is what makes the audit possible, because it gives each reporting gap a place to live.

Three mechanisms in the architecture chapters are specific enough that I would reimplement them rather than paraphrase them, and each is a rule rather than a model.

The first is the reasoning module written as a typed contract, which is the paper's device for stopping an agent from emitting an action it has not checked:

$$f\big(O_t,\, M_{episodic},\, P_{portfolio}\big) \rightarrow \big(A_{cand},\, \alpha,\, V_{check}\big)$$

where:

- $O_t$ is a timestamped state observation at decision time $t$
- $M_{episodic}$ is retrievable episodic memory
- $P_{portfolio}$ is read-only portfolio state, positions and risk limits
- $A_{cand}$ is a set of candidate actions
- $\alpha \in [0,1]$ is a confidence score
- $V_{check}$ is a validity check such as `holds < max_leverage`

The point of writing it this way is that risk constraints are enforced during or immediately after reasoning rather than at the final execution layer, so a hallucinated action never reaches the order interface. The second is the episodic-memory rule that addresses what they call the <font color="#ffff00">Oracle Fallacy</font>, an agent retrieving a similar past episode whose stored text already contains a post-hoc narrative of how it turned out. A valid episode is the tuple $(S_t, A_t, O_{t+k}, \tau)$ where $O_{t+k}$ is the outcome realised at $t+k$ and $\tau$ is the timestamp at which that outcome becomes observable, and the <font color="#ffc000">Outcome Embargo</font> is the retrieval constraint

$$\text{retrieve}\big(S_t, A_t, O_{t+k}\big) \;\text{ permitted only if }\; t_{now} \ge t+k$$

which is a leakage control I can implement in a retrieval filter and report in one line. They pair it with a suggestion that the relevance score carry a recency decay term $e^{-\lambda(t_{now}-t_k)}$ so that retrieval does not keep surfacing high-similarity episodes from a regime that has ended, which is the same drift concern that shows up in my volatility work from a different direction.

The third is the exposure cap they recommend reporting in display form rather than burying in prose, which caps each position as prices and volatility move:

$$w_i(t) = \min\!\left(w_i^{\max},\; \frac{\text{risk\_budget}}{\text{risk\_per\_unit}_i}\right)$$

That is deliberately simple, and it is in the paper because risk triggers, latency budgets and audit logs are inconsistently reported across the corpus, so the argument is about the disclosure convention rather than the formula.

The checklist itself is the part I will actually use. Seven minimum reporting items, retyped here because I want them as a working reference rather than as a figure I have to squint at.

| Item | What it demands |
| --- | --- |
| MR-1 Data & universe | asset class, universe construction, survivorship treatment, timestamp availability for all modalities, especially text and news |
| MR-2 Time-consistent splitting | train/validation/test dates, walk-forward or rolling details, embargo and purge rules; for adaptive search, the search budget and a sealed test set |
| MR-3 Action semantics | orders against target weights, decision timestamp, order mapping, price formation, portfolio I/O contracts and constraints |
| MR-4 Execution & costs | execution timing and price assumption, rebalancing frequency, fees, spread, slippage, impact model, execution metrics plus sensitivity to cost assumptions |
| MR-5 Leakage audit | explicit leakage vectors checked, with feature fitting, time hindsight and label leakage named, and `NR/Unknown` treated conservatively |
| MR-6 Artifacts & logs | code and data availability, explicit factor formulas or architectures, random seeds, immutable logs linking order IDs to traces, rollback criteria for adaptive systems |
| MR-7 Multi-agent evaluation | roles and permissions, message protocol, consensus mechanism, shared-state consistency, cross-play generalisation, crowding and impact metrics, role and communication ablations |

The obligations are then graded by study type, and this is the grid I should hold my own write-up against, since a full end-to-end trading system carries the heaviest load.

#### Findings

- **<font color="#ffc000">Protocol reporting, not architecture, is what the audit finds binding.</font>** Within the 19 primary studies, **2/19 (10.5%)** report an extractable time-consistent split protocol, **1/19 (5.3%)** specifies a transaction-cost model, **1/19** documents universe or survivorship handling, **11/19 (57.9%)** report execution timing or semantics, and **4/19** expose artifacts beyond narrative description. On split protocol specifically, **17/19** are coded `Unknown`, 2 are coded `other`, and zero report walk-forward or rolling evaluation. Evaluation types are 11 backtest, 3 live, 2 simulation and 3 benchmark. These are counts over a fixed, stated denominator, which is exactly what the surveys I have read so far could not give me, and the one I would quote first is the cost figure, because a literature in which one paper in nineteen models transaction costs cannot adjudicate any claim about net profitability.

- **The reproducibility floor is lower than the protocol floor.** Fifteen of nineteen primary studies sit at R0, no runnable artifacts at all, one at R1, three at R2, and none reaches R3, so **R2 or above is 15.8%**.

![[Pasted image 20260926233004.png]]

  The tier definitions are worth keeping because they are stricter than the usual "code available" claim: R2 requires complete code, accessible or clearly documented data and evaluation scripts, and still concedes that a pinned environment or an end-to-end pipeline may be missing, while R3 requires all of it. That zero in the R3 row is, on this sample, the single most citable number in the paper.

- **Execution semantics are the strongest boundary between a trading agent and a signal model, and the corpus treats them as optional.** The paper's framing of <font color="#ffc000">action definition risk</font> is the useful part: if an agent emits only target weights without modelling order timing, fill constraints and costs, evaluation quietly collapses intent into execution and the execution gap disappears by construction. They formalise the action module as $f(D_t) \rightarrow (A_{order}, A_{algo})$ with typed order fields and explicit failure modes for rejection, partial fill and timeout fed back into memory, and note that microstructure signals such as imbalance, spread and queue position are hard feasibility constraints filtering the action space rather than optimisation inputs, with feasible actions conditioned on $O_{t-\delta}$ to respect processing latency. Execution is simultaneously the best-covered protocol field in the audit and still missing from more than four papers in ten.

- **Coordination evidence is thinner than the multi-agent branding suggests, and attribution is missing where it exists.** Nine of the nineteen primary studies address coordination mechanisms at all, and the section-level primary count for the multi-agent chapter is 12 with the larger citation list made up of benchmarks and background agent-based modelling work that does not enter the primary total. The comparison across the three coordination patterns is where the asymmetry shows.

![[Pasted image 20260926233002.png]]

  Role-based systems are the only pattern reaching R2, hierarchical and market-ecology work sits at R0, and the execution-semantics tag is negative for all three rows, so no coordination pattern in the table reports how its decisions become orders. The authors are careful that role-based being the most auditable does not make it the best performing, and their protocol recommendation is the part I care about: <font color="#ffff00">cross-play evaluation</font> swapping individual agents to test interoperability against team overfitting, explicit communication constraints, crowding and impact metrics, and role or communication ablations to attribute gains to coordination rather than to compute. On their reading, complex decomposition currently obscures the source of gains rather than demonstrating them.

- **Adaptation and learning claims are mostly transferred from general AI rather than demonstrated in trading.** The section-level primary count for supervised fine-tuning is **N=0** and for meta-learning it is **N=0**, learning paradigms overall rest on 3 primary studies, one in-context learning and two reinforcement learning, and self-evolution carries 4 primary studies with every row of its comparison table tagged as background. Their reading is that self-improvement claims are not comparable without frozen execution semantics, a sealed test set and immutable logs with rollback criteria, since otherwise an apparent improvement cannot be separated from protocol drift, and they flag reflection as an unbounded search process needing a stated <font color="#ffff00">reflection budget</font>, because retrying until a profitable trade appears is testing into success.

- **The audited corpus is young and largely un-refereed, which bounds how hard the conclusions can be pushed.** With 18/19 primary studies from 2024 onward and 17/19 currently non-peer-reviewed, the authors describe their own synthesis as preliminary and the architectural patterns they identify as emerging tendencies rather than established practice. That is the right call, and it means the numbers above are a snapshot of a literature in its first two years rather than a settled characterisation of the field.

#### Limitations

- **<font color="#ff0000">The lens and the checklist are both author-curated and neither is externally validated.</font>** A-C-A is described by the authors as an exploratory analytical lens, its reclassification notes cover 20 works selected on criteria including citation count and availability of technical detail, the coding was done by one author and reconciled among the authors themselves, and the coding status is summarised qualitatively with placeholder agreement examples rather than kappa numerics. The reporting items have the same problem one level up, since the mandatory, recommended and optional designations reflect author judgement operationalised over a pilot coding of 10 representative papers with disagreements resolved by author consensus. The standard the field is measured against was set by the people doing the measuring, and while the paper says so plainly in three places, a different set of authors could have made MR-7 mandatory for multi-agent systems and produced a different set of failing grades.

- **A denominator of 19 makes every percentage brittle, and the section-level zeros are the most brittle of all.** One paper moves the headline figures by 5.3 points, so 10.5% for split protocols and 5.3% for cost models should be read as two papers and one paper, which is how I will quote them. The section-level counts are weaker still: N=0 for supervised fine-tuning and N=0 for meta-learning are statements that no paper in a 19-paper sample chosen under a narrow action-plus-closed-loop boundary did that thing, which is a long way from a claim about the field, and the paper's own reading note concedes that N counts indicate sparse explicit reporting rather than absence of the underlying practice.

- **The audit measures papers and the narrative occasionally slips into measuring systems.** The authors state the distinction correctly, that `Unknown` denotes information not reported clearly enough for reliable extraction under a conservative coding policy and is not proof that a mechanism is absent, and then several passages read as though a system without a reported cost model does not have one. For a corpus that is 89% preprints, much of what is missing is appendix material that a journal round would have forced out, and the survey cannot distinguish an author who did not model costs from one who did not write it down.

- **Several named frameworks are the authors' own inventions presented in the same register as surveyed evidence.** The Cascaded Controller, the Alpha-to-Trade Contract, the Hierarchy of Truth, Log-to-Narrative Verification and the Oracle Fallacy are all author synthesis, and to their credit the Cascaded Controller passage says outright that both the term and the pattern are their own recommendation rather than a contribution of the survey. They still sit in boxed callouts alongside the evidence summaries, and a skimming reader will not reliably separate the two. HiveMind (Xia et al. 2026) also appears as background in the coordination chapter without the self-citation being flagged.

- **It deliberately contains no performance synthesis, so it cannot tell me what works.** No returns, no Sharpe ratios, no cross-paper comparison of architectures on any metric, and the one performance figure that appears anywhere, the 53.17% cumulative return on SSE50 reported by [[Automate Strategy Finding with LLM in Quant investment|Automate Strategy Finding]], is quoted with `Split: NR, Execution: NR, Cost: NR, Universe: SSE50, Tier: BG` attached precisely to demonstrate that the number is illustrative rather than protocol-comparable. This is a defensible choice for an audit-oriented review and it is the correct answer to a literature this unreported, but it means the paper is a citation for why comparisons are unsafe and never a citation for a comparison.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **The reporting checklist becomes the spine of my evaluation chapter, with the CSE specifics filled in under each item.** Universe construction and survivorship under MR-1 is not a formality here, where delistings, long trading suspensions and illiquid counters make the definition of the tradable universe a design decision that changes results, and timestamp availability covers the sentiment channel, where publication time and availability time differ. Under MR-2 the commitment is walk-forward with a stated embargo, which also settles how the orchestrator's own tuning is separated from final evaluation. Writing the protocol section against seven named items rather than inventing headings lets a reviewer check it against a published checklist, which is a cheap credibility win.

- **The execution-friction work is now positioned by the field's own counts rather than by my assertion.** One paper in nineteen specifying a transaction-cost model, and zero coordination patterns reporting execution semantics, is a much stronger warrant for state-dependent friction in a thin book than the individual-paper evidence I have been leaning on, such as MacroHFT's flat 0.02% over roughly twenty round trips. The scoping has to be exact: this is a claim about what the audited papers report, not proof that nobody has modelled nonlinear impact, so it pairs with the outstanding verification of the 2026 market-impact citations rather than replacing it.

- **The reasoning I/O contract gives the heterogeneous-confidence problem a shape without solving it.** Their contract makes $\alpha$ an explicit per-decision output alongside a validity check, which is a cleaner interface than anything I had written down, and it tells me the confidence score belongs in the agent's typed output rather than being inferred by the orchestrator after the fact. What it does not supply is comparability, because an LLM analyst's self-reported $\alpha$, an econometric estimator's interval width and an RL policy's value spread are on three different scales and the paper never addresses cross-agent calibration. That remains the open question the orchestrator lives or dies on, and this survey narrows it to a calibration problem rather than a representation problem.

- **The outcome embargo and the recency decay are two memory rules I can implement, test and report in a paragraph.** The embargo is a retrieval filter, cheap to write and easy to state under the leakage audit, and it closes a specific leakage route that a naive episodic store would leave open in a market where post-event commentary is the main text available. The decay term is the retrieval-side version of the regime-drift concern I have from the volatility literature, and the useful discipline is the survey's framing of memory settings as an <font color="#ffc000">evaluation knob</font>: retrieval window, similarity function and truncation all change decisions under the same base model, so ablations have to report memory settings or they are not ablations.

- **Role and communication ablations are the published form of the diagnostic I already pre-registered.** The weight-entropy check, that if the learned orchestration weights collapse to one-hot the contribution reduces to regime routing, is the same class of test as their demand that coordination gains be attributed by removing roles or channels rather than assumed from architecture. Reporting it under MR-7 alongside a cross-play check, swapping one agent for a weaker or differently-specified version, converts a defensive self-check into a positive methodological contribution, and it is the answer to the objection that a heterogeneous ensemble is just more compute.

- **Fifteen of nineteen at R0 has a direct planning consequence and a direct opportunity.** I should assume reimplementation from paper descriptions for any baseline I want, budget accordingly, and stop treating "the authors released code" as a reasonable default when planning comparisons. The flip side is that with **0/19** reaching R3 on this sample, a CSE system released with pinned environment, data access instructions, seeds and an end-to-end evaluation pipeline would be at a tier no audited paper in this corpus occupies, and that is a contribution I can commit to independently of whether the orchestrator beats its baselines.

- **The time-scale taxonomy lets me justify the reasoning budget rather than assert it.** Their reactive, reflective and strategic split is explicit that LLM-mediated deliberation operates at second-scale latency at best and cannot support genuine high-frequency decisions, including ReAct-style loops which they classify as reflective rather than reactive. A daily or intraday CSE rebalance sits comfortably in the reflective band, so the architecture choice is defensible on the paper's own terms, and the same section supplies the feasibility line for the proposal through the annualised inference cost, trading days times decisions per day times effective cost per request, which is the arithmetic that decides whether a per-decision multi-agent debate is affordable at all.

- **Search budget binds hard on CSE-length data, and the paper names the failure mode.** Their adaptive-overfitting argument, that iterative refinement against validation metrics mines the validation set unless the final result is reported on a sealed test set never exposed to the refinement loop, applies directly to tuning the orchestrator's conditioning set on a short history, and the recommended disclosure is the search budget plus the sealed split. The honest design that follows is a small conditioning set fixed in advance, a stated count of how many variants were tried, and behavioural results reported beside return results so a thin margin is not the only thing carrying the claim.

> [!important]
> Cite this for the protocol and reproducibility counts, the inclusion boundary, the minimum reporting items and the coordination-attribution argument, always with the denominator attached and always as a statement about 19 audited papers rather than about the field. Never cite it for a performance comparison, an architecture recommendation or evidence that any design works, because it contains no results and says so.
