arXiv:2311.11944v1, 20 November 2023, Pranab Islam, Anand Kannappan, Douwe Kiela, Rebecca Qian, Nino Scherrer and Bertie Vidgen of Patronus AI, Contextual AI and Stanford. An <font color="#ffff00">open-book</font> financial QA test suite of 10,231 question, answer and evidence triplets covering 40 US-listed companies and 361 public filings from 2015 to 2023, mostly 10Ks. Every entry carries the evidence string and the page number that proves the answer, so a question can be scored on whether the model found the right place as well as on whether it got the number right. Sixteen configurations of GPT-4, GPT-4-Turbo, Llama2 and Claude2 are run across five settings, closed book, oracle, one vector store per document, one shared store over all documents, and the whole filing in a long-context window, and 2,400 responses on a 150-case sample are labelled by hand as correct, incorrect or failure to answer.

The note this replaces was a summary of the dataset construction with the results figures pasted at the end, and it had the right material in it, but it read the paper as a description of a dataset rather than as evidence about a task I have to perform. Of the three benchmark notes this is the one whose task shape actually matches $A_F$. [[FinQA, A Dataset of Numerical Reasoning over Financial Data]] ranks facts within a page and [[TAT-QA, A Question Answering Benchmark on a Hybrid of Tabular and Textual Content in Finance]] hands over the hybrid context outright, whereas FinanceBench makes the model find the page inside a filing that can run to 250 pages, which is what a fundamental and retrieval channel over CSE annual reports has to do. It is also the source of the <font color="#ffc000">correct, incorrect and failure-to-answer taxonomy</font> that [[Scope sentiment analysis dropped]] has already adopted for component-level evidence, so the definitions below are ones I am committed to rather than ones I am assessing.

> [!NOTE] The three-way label, as defined here
> **Correct** allows good-faith deviations, an answer given in billions where the question asked for millions, and very small rounding errors. **Incorrect** spans everything from calculations off by a small margin to ones off by orders of magnitude, and includes <font color="#ffc000">a right answer reached with logic or calculations that explicitly contradict the gold evidence</font>. **Failure to answer** requires the model to state explicitly that it cannot answer because it lacks access to the information. That third rule in the Incorrect definition is the one that makes this a test of the reasoning chain rather than of the final number, and it is the reason the taxonomy suits a channel whose explainability story is citation tracing.

#### Gap it's addressing

- **Existing financial QA datasets do not test retrieval, so they evaluate a task analysts are not actually delegating.** FiQA is aspect-based sentiment, which the authors correctly note is a small slice of what analysts ask. FinQA, ConvFinQA and TAT-QA give the model the relevant context and ask it to reason over it. The charge is that this breaks ecological validity, because the work being replaced or augmented is finding the information in a filing *and then* reasoning about it. This is the real contribution and it is the reason the paper matters to me more than its two siblings do.
- **Generic QA benchmarks contain few or no financial questions and do not transfer.** SQuAD, NarrativeQA, HellaSwag and HELM are named, along with TruthfulQA as the rare case with any finance content, and the argument is simply that strong open-domain performance cannot be assumed to carry into a specialised domain.
- **Without systematic evaluation the industry cannot judge deployment readiness or track capability over time.** This is a procurement argument rather than a research gap, and it shapes the whole paper, which is organised around whether a model could be trusted in a high-stakes live setting rather than around advancing a method.

#### Data and setup

Forty companies, 360 documents, 2015 to 2023. The document mix is 270 10Ks, 29 8Ks, 29 earnings reports, 27 10Qs and 5 annual reports, but the question distribution is far more lopsided than that: <font color="#ffc000">10Ks carry 9,530 of the questions, 93% of the total</font>, so despite the four document types this is effectively an annual-filing benchmark. Nine of eleven GICS sectors appear, from Information Technology at 25% of questions down to Materials at 1.8%.

The three question types are built differently and it matters for how the results read:

| Type | How built | Companies | Count |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Domain-relevant | 25 generic analyst questions posed to every company, dropped where not applicable | 37 of 40 | 925 |
| Novel generated | Annotator-written, directed to be realistic, varied and not purely extractive | 37 of 40 | 1,323 |
| Metrics-generated | 18 base metrics pulled from the three statements over 2015 to 2022, derivative metrics computed from them, questions built from templates with phrasing variations | 32 of 40 | 7,983 |

A reasoning taxonomy is applied to the domain-relevant and metrics-generated questions, $n = 8{,}908$: information extraction 2,493 (28%), numerical reasoning 5,897 (66%) and logical reasoning 518 (6%). The three are not exclusive, since the other two always involve some extraction to obtain the inputs.

Annotation used 20 recruited annotators with finance backgrounds who passed a screening test. Five left early and their work was discarded, thirteen contributed between 19 and 369 of the domain-relevant and novel questions, and two people did nothing but extract metrics, just over 2,300 each. A senior analyst reviewed roughly 20 to 25 cases per annotator per week, around 10 to 20%, and about 10% of the domain-relevant and novel questions were reviewed at the end. The final stages ran with four annotators.

The human evaluation set is 150 cases, 50 domain-relevant stratified so that each of the 25 unique questions appears twice, 50 randomly sampled novel and 50 randomly sampled metrics-generated. The authors state plainly that they did **not** stratify by company, year or industry. All models were tested in November 2023 at temperature 0.01 with a 2,048-token cap and default system prompts, Llama2-70b-chat through Replicate and the rest through their own APIs.

#### Evaluation design

There is no model here and no mathematics anywhere in the paper, so this section takes the place of the architecture section in the other notes. The design is five settings crossed with two prompt orders, and the settings are best read as a ladder from no information to perfect information:

| Setting | What the model is given | Why it is in the paper |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Closed book | The question alone | Naive floor, tests parametric knowledge only |
| Shared vector store | Top chunks from one Chroma store indexing all 360 documents | The realistic industry setup |
| Single vector store | Top chunks from a store built for that one document | Faster and better, but presumes the document is already identified |
| Long context | The entire filing pasted in, truncated to 95,000 to 100,000 tokens | Removes retrieval, at latency and cost the authors call impractical |
| Oracle | The text of the page the annotator used as evidence | Unrealistic ceiling, reference point only |

The vector stores use Langchain with Chroma and OpenAI `text-embedding-ada-002`. Chunking strategy, chunk size, number of retrieved chunks and any reranking are not described.

The prompt-order variable is the one worth writing out, because it produces the largest single effect in the paper. Context-First puts `Answer this question: [QUESTION]` followed by `Context: [START OF FILING] ... [END OF FILING]`; Context-Last reverses them so the filing arrives before the question. Both are tested for the long-context and oracle settings.

#### Findings

- **Retrieval is where the pipeline breaks, and the size of the effect dwarfs everything else measured.** Correct answers run 9% closed book, 19% with the shared vector store, 50% with a single store, 76% and 79% with the whole filing in context, and 85% with the evidence page handed over. Handing the model the right page is worth <font color="#ffc000">66 points over the realistic retrieval setting</font>, and the paper's own conclusion is the right one, that access to the right information and correct retrieval of it are the binding constraints rather than reasoning.

  ![[Pasted image 20260901111432.png]]

- **The gap between one store per document and one store over all documents is the most operationally useful number here and is barely discussed.** GPT-4-Turbo goes from 50% to 19% and Llama2 from 41% to 19%, purely from widening the search space from one filing to 360. The paper notes this as anticipated and moves on. The reading I would carry is that a single-document store is not a weaker version of a realistic system, it is a realistic system with the document-selection step already solved by someone else, and the 31-point drop is the cost of not solving it. On a 150-case sample that gap is far outside sampling error, whereas the 41% against 50% difference between the two models in the same setting is not.
- **The two models fail in opposite directions at identical correctness, which is the finding with the most direct bearing on my design.** On the shared store both score 19% correct, but GPT-4-Turbo fails to answer 68% of the time and is wrong 13%, while Llama2 is wrong 70% of the time and refuses 11%. The same pattern holds on the single store, 39% refusal against 11% wrong for GPT-4-Turbo, 5% against 54% for Llama2. The authors read a high refusal rate as preferable to a high error rate because it creates less risk of misplaced trust, and on this evidence I agree, but the important part is that a single correctness figure conceals two systems with completely different risk profiles.
- **Prompt order swings long-context accuracy by more than any model or retrieval choice in the paper.** Putting the filing before the question rather than after it takes GPT-4-Turbo from 25% to 78% and Claude2 from 37% to 76%, a 53-point and a 39-point difference from moving one block of text. The effect reverses where the context is short, with the oracle setting slightly better at 89% under Context-Last against 85% under Context-First. The authors hypothesise that models lose track of the question after thousands of evidence tokens, which fits both directions and is not tested.

  ![[Pasted image 20260926011001.png]]

- **Metrics-generated questions are hardest under retrieval and easiest under long context, which localises the difficulty to finding rather than computing.** Under the vector stores they are the weakest of the three types for every model, and under long context and oracle GPT-4-Turbo scores highest on them, above 90%. The paper's explanation is that the challenge with these questions is retrieving the right information, and the figure supports it. The complement it also concedes is that many domain-relevant and novel questions are answerable from general world knowledge about what a company does, which is why those two types hold up better in the closed-book and shared-store columns.

  ![[Pasted image 20260901111825.png]]

- **The qualitative pass names a response category that breaks automated scoring.** Alongside hallucinations and helpful refusals, the authors identify answers that are substantively different from the gold standard but still valid, common where a question does not specify units or which evidence would substantiate a qualitative judgement. They count these as correct on a good-faith basis, a judgement made by humans across all 2,400 responses. Any automated re-run of this benchmark will therefore score differently by construction, and [[Financial Report Chunking for Effective Retrieval Augmented Generation]]'s GPT-4-as-judge evaluation on this same dataset is exactly such a re-run, which is worth remembering when reading the two together.

#### Limitations

- **Only 150 of the 10,231 cases are public, so the headline scale is a claim about work done rather than a resource anyone can use.** The paper documents a 10,231-case benchmark and open-sources the 150-case human evaluation sample, which the authors chose not to stratify by company, year or industry. <font color="#ffc000">Every downstream paper citing FinanceBench is running the 150</font>, including the chunking work, so a result reported on it carries a 150-case sample's uncertainty regardless of the number in the abstract.
- **Single run, single prompt per configuration, no dispersion and no significance test.** Temperature 0.01 suppresses variance without eliminating it, and nothing is repeated. On 150 cases the binomial standard error near 50% correctness is about 4.1 points, so a 95% interval on any single cell is roughly ±8 points and on a difference between two cells roughly ±11. That is enough to separate 19% from 50% comfortably and not enough to separate Llama2's 41% from GPT-4-Turbo's 50% in the same setting, a distinction the paper draws in prose without qualification.
- **The retrieval baseline is deliberately naive and the abstract then generalises from it.** One Chroma store, ada-002 embeddings, Langchain defaults, no reranking, no hybrid search, no query rewriting and no described chunking strategy at all. The abstract states that GPT-4-Turbo "used with a retrieval system incorrectly answered or refused to answer 81% of questions", which is the shared-store figure, and does not say which retrieval setting it refers to; the single-store equivalent is 50%. Against that, [[Financial Report Chunking for Effective Retrieval Augmented Generation]] reaches 53.19% end-to-end on this dataset's public sample with structure-aware chunking, against 48.23% for its own best fixed-size baseline and the 50% reported here. So the defensible claim is that <font color="#ff0000">a default RAG pipeline fails on financial filings</font>, not that retrieval fails, and the difference matters because the second version is the one this paper is usually cited for.
- **Dataset integrity issues are named and never quantified.** The authors concede that some questions are ecologically valid but simplistic, answerable without any additional source and therefore inflating scores, and that some gold answers are ambiguous or contestable depending on an analyst's assumptions and priorities. Neither is given a share. At 9% closed book this hardly matters, but at 79% under long context the proportion of simplistic questions directly bounds what that number means, and it is not reported.
- **The annotation pipeline is described honestly and is thin at the points that determine label quality.** Twenty recruited, five discarded, thirteen contributing between 19 and 369 questions so the per-annotator load is wildly uneven, two people producing all of the roughly 4,600 base metric extractions between them, about 10% of the free-written questions reviewed, and four annotators left by the end. No inter-annotator agreement statistic appears anywhere. Set against TAT-QA's two-round validation with a separate approver and FinQA's eleven paid professionals, this is the weakest quality-control story of the three, which the authors partly offset by having the research team label all 2,400 model responses themselves.
- **Single-turn, single-company, large-cap and US-only, all of which the authors state.** No conversation and no cross-company comparison, and a universe of forty large, well-audited companies filing under SEC requirements. The public-filings restriction is fine for my purposes since CSE filings are public too, but the large-company bias is not neutral: these are professionally prepared documents with consistent structure, and on a CSE annual report the extraction problem is harder before any question is asked.

#### Why this matters for my project

- **This is the benchmark note whose task actually matches $A_F$, and it moves my expectation down again.** The other two hand over the context; this one makes the model locate it, and under the realistic setting a frontier model in late 2023 was correct on 19% to 50% of questions. Placed beside FinQA's sub-50% on computed quantities and TAT-QA's 42.5 on arithmetic, this is a third independent estimate and the first that includes retrieval, so the working assumption is that <font color="#ffc000">$A_F$ will be wrong or will abstain on more than half of computed questions over CSE filings</font>, and the orchestrator has to be designed for a channel that is frequently unusable rather than occasionally imperfect.
- **The evaluation taxonomy the scope note already adopts comes from here, and it should be cited as such with the definitions intact.** In particular the rule that a right answer reached by contradicting logic counts as Incorrect, because that is what turns the taxonomy into a test of the chain rather than of the number, and it is the rule that makes it fit a channel whose explainability story is RAG citation tracing. The good-faith allowances for unit deviation and small rounding travel with it, since dropping them would make my figures incomparable to anything in this literature.
- **Refusal and error are different failures and the orchestrator needs an abstain path distinct from a low-confidence path.** Two models at identical 19% correctness, one refusing 68% of the time and the other asserting wrongly 70% of the time, are not the same agent from a weighting function's point of view. A refusal is $A_F$ declining to produce evidence, which should drive $w_{F,t}$ to zero and redistribute across the other three channels; a confident wrong answer is the case nothing in the weighting can detect and is where a hallucinating fundamental channel does real damage. So $A_F$ should be built to refuse rather than to guess, and abstention should be a distinct state rather than a low value of $C_{F,t}$. That is the third time the answer on confidence has come out as a structural property of the output rather than a self-reported score, after FinQA's program step count and TAT-QA's operator posterior, and the pattern is now firm enough to put to the supervisor as a refinement of the conditioning set.
- **The single versus shared store result is a direct and cheap design instruction.** Fifty per cent against nineteen, from nothing but the size of the search space. The CSE has a few hundred listed companies each with annual and quarterly filings, so a naive index over all of it is the shared-store case exactly. The design that follows is to route on structured metadata first, ticker and document type and fiscal year, all of which I have, and only then retrieve within the selected document, which converts every query into the single-store case. This costs nothing to implement and is the single highest-value item in this note.
- **Prompt order is a fifty-point free variable and belongs in the experimental design rather than in an implementation footnote.** Evidence before question in long-context settings, question first only where the context is short. The wider consequence is that any retrieval comparison I report has to hold prompt format fixed across arms, because a formatting difference of this size would swamp whatever the arms were supposed to be measuring, and the same applies to the three weighting schemes if any of them touch a prompt.
- **Paired with the chunking paper, this is the argument for structure-aware retrieval in $A_F$, and neither half works alone.** FinanceBench establishes that a default pipeline fails on filings; [[Financial Report Chunking for Effective Retrieval Augmented Generation]] establishes that chunking on document structure recovers a few points of it, reaching 53.19% end to end on 141 of these questions against 48.23% for its own best fixed-size baseline. Citing FinanceBench alone would overclaim that retrieval fails, and citing the chunking result alone would leave the baseline unstated. The pair also keeps my framing honest, since structure-aware retrieval is not claimed as novel and the 2025 CSE Graph-RAG prior art in [[Neo4j Graph-RAG for CSE]] still stands.
- **The 150-case public sample is the size my own probe set can realistically be, which is reassuring rather than disappointing.** Three benchmark notes have now converged from opposite directions: FinQA's eleven CPAs and TAT-QA's thirty screened annotators over three months are both out of reach, and the artefact this paper actually put into the world is 150 human-checked cases. Fifty per question type is affordable. What I should copy is the stratification by reasoning type, extraction against numerical against logical, since the three behave differently under every configuration here, and what I should not copy is the sampling, because FinanceBench deliberately did not stratify by company or year and CSE disclosure quality varies enormously with company size.
- **Report the three-way breakdown per configuration, never a single accuracy figure.** The most informative thing in this paper's results table is that two rows with identical correctness have opposite failure profiles, which a single number would have hidden entirely. For the $A_F$ component evaluation that means correct, incorrect and abstain reported side by side for every retrieval configuration, and the same discipline extends to the orchestration ablation arms. This is the retrieval-side arrival at the same conclusion the trading notes reached through trade-count and turnover diagnostics, that the defensible result is the one reported with enough breakdown for someone to attack it.
